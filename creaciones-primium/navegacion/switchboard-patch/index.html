A cord is a loop of flexible copper braid with two tips on the end. It is held by a spring, so it hangs there until you need it, and the reason it rings against the wood when it lands is a matter of acoustics rather than engineering.

Line 101 · the pair

The cord and the jack

A connection on a telephone board is made by hand and undone by hand, in public, several hundred times a day. Behind the wooden faceplate there is a field of spring contacts, and in front of it a wall of holes. Put a cord through the right pair of holes and two people who have never met can hear each other breathe.

The cord is a loop, which means there are always two of them, and they are the reason a board looks the way it does from a distance: a mess of red over black, and behind it a row of people waiting to find out who is on the line.

Line 102 · the position

The operator at the board

The operator sat facing a wall of lamps with a headset and a list, and the job was a memory task performed under time pressure. A lamp, a name, a number: three things to hold at once while a queue of other lamps lit up. The training was weeks long and the pay was not much, and the work was done mostly by women who were never going to be told how good they were at it.

What they learned was that the board was not a telephone exchange but a routing problem with a human in the middle of it. The operator decided, from two numbers spoken out loud, which of several hundred possible routes was actually going to work that evening.

Line 103 · the signals

Lamp after lamp

Before the lamps came the ringing machine: a wheel that went round striking a bell every few seconds, stopping when a subscriber picked up. The lamps did the same job with light, which meant an operator could watch a whole floor at once and see immediately who had answered and who had not.

There is a particular pleasure in a signal lamp working properly, a small circle of light coming on at exactly the moment it is supposed to, and it was the one part of the job that felt like machinery rather than paperwork. Boards were arranged so the lamps were visible from the doorway, because a supervisor who could see the whole wall needed to walk in far less often.

Line 104 · the hours

The night shift

After midnight the calls were fewer and stranger: a wrong number, a hospital, a person who wanted to talk to somebody and did not care who. The board was quieter, the cords hung stiller, and the operators who worked the small hours tended to know most of the regulars by voice alone, which was information the exchange never held anywhere.

They also knew which circuits were bad. A line that crackled was a line to answer with the receiver off the hook, and the local knowledge of a night operator was, in effect, a maintenance record that nobody had ever asked her to write down.

Line 105 · the change

When the board went quiet

Automatic exchange arrived over about twenty years and it was not one event. First the dial, then the register, then the stepping relays, until one morning the operators were asked to supervise a machine that connected people without being told which hole to put the cord in.

The boards were not thrown away. They were sold, given to museums, and left exactly where they stood for a while, with the cords still hanging in their springs, which is a more honest memorial than a plaque. The work went to a building with no lamps and no operator, and the lamps stayed dark.

Line 106 · the end

The last call

On the last day the room was left alone with the board, and a great many people who had never worked a switchboard in their lives came to stand in front of one. The thing that surprises people is not the size of it but the intimacy: a desk-height object, a bit like a lectern, built for somebody to sit in front of all day and answer.

What the machine offered was the same thing it offered in the first hour of its life, which is a way of knowing that a person two miles away has put their hand on a particular object and is waiting for somebody to notice. No board has ever done that since, and a good many of the people who miss it were the ones who could do it fastest.
