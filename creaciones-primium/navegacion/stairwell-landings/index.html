Landing 01 · the landing

The landing

A landing is the only horizontal surface in a building that exists purely so that a person can change their mind. It has no function except to be somewhere to stand while you work out which way you were going, and that is why its size is such a strong signal about how a building was designed: a generous landing is a building that expects people to meet on it, a narrow one is a building that wants them out of the way.

Structurally it is a slab, and it is also the piece of the building that carries the stair. The flight arrives at one edge, the next flight leaves from the other, and the landing is what turns the direction back on itself so that the flights fit into a plan of about two and a half metres. The turn is the whole reason a stair exists, and the shape of the well above is simply that turn repeated six times.

Landing 02 · the railing

The railing

A balustrade is a code requirement, a fire requirement and a way of carrying the eye, and the three rarely agree. The building code wants a height and a maximum gap; the fire code wants a thing a person can be pulled over in an emergency; the architecture wants a rhythm that relates to the window above it. The result is almost always a compromise with a very specific look, which is why balustrades are such a reliable dating tool.

The physical part is simpler than it looks. Balusters are cast or turned, fixed to a bottom rail and a top rail, and the top rail is the only part anybody touches, so it is the only part that gets worn. A handrail polished to a shine at four hundred millimetres is telling you exactly how many years the building has had a stair in it, and the height at which the shine sits is a measure of who has been using the building.

Landing 03 · the window

The window

A stairwell window is a strange object because it is almost never a view. It is there to bring light down a shaft that would otherwise be black, to let hot air out at the top, and to give somebody standing on a landing something other than a wall to look at for the twelve seconds it takes to decide which way to go.

The proportions are almost always the same: tall, narrow, one sash, glass that was wired rather than plate, and a cill deep enough to sit on. Old stair windows are wired because the alternative was a net, and the wires are visible as a fine grid in the light when the sun is behind them, which is one of the more beautiful accidents in ordinary building. Modern versions are wired glass too, and the reason has never changed: a pane in a stair has to be unbreakable and it has to be cheap.

Landing 04 · the wear

The wear

The stone on a stair is a record of everybody who has used it, and the record is written in one place. Feet arrive on the front edge of a tread, so the nosing is where the wear is, and after a century the nosing is rounded and slightly dished in the middle and the pattern is the same on every tread, because the tread is the shape of a footstep and not a decision.

Where people live above a shop, the stairs also develop a boundary. The lower flights, in use all day, are polished to a shine; the upper flights, in use twice a day, are not. The line where the shine stops is almost exactly where the building stopped being retail, and it is one of the sharpest transitions anywhere in architecture. It is visible in the six landings above as the difference between a stone tread that has been walked and a stone tread that has merely been looked at.

Landing 05 · the void

The void

The well is the part of the stair nobody sees, and in a building of any age it is where everything else is. The lift shaft goes up through it, or the dumbwaiter did, or the pipe that serves every bathroom on the floor runs down the inside of it. A stairwell is the one part of a building that has to be open from bottom to top, which is why it is also the part that collects smoke, and why the door onto it is the single most important fire door in the building.

Because the well is open, the stack effect is enormous. Warm air rises out of it in winter, which is why stairwells in cold buildings are draughty and in warm buildings are unpleasantly hot, and why every attempt to insulate one has ended with a smoke-control problem. A modern building puts the stair in a pressurised lobby and puts a much bigger plant on the roof, and gains an efficient building and loses the thing that made the old stairwell worth having, which is that it is a shaft of moving air you can feel from the bottom.

Landing 06 · the day

The day

The shaft of light in a stairwell is on a timer. In the morning it comes in flat from the side and lands across the upper landings. By midday it is nearly vertical and drops straight down the void, which is the moment everybody who works in the building notices it and nobody mentions. In the afternoon it swings back and by dusk it is climbing the wall on the other side, having travelled up the well rather than down it.

Two of those three states are genuinely useful and one is decorative, and architects have argued about which matters for about two hundred years. The daylight version is cheaper to run and better to be in; the electric version can be on when somebody comes home at eleven at night. The stairwell above uses the daylight version, on the grounds that the shaft is a real object in the building and moving it is better than lighting a box. The landing that is lit is the one you are on, and the light reaches it by falling.
