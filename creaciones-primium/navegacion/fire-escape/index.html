Flight 01 · the plate

The plate

Every part of a fire escape is a flat bar, because a flat bar is what can be forged, bolted and painted. The stringers are the two long pieces along the sides of a flight; the treads are the short pieces across the top of them, riveted rather than welded because that is what could be done on a site in 1911; the handrail is a third stringer, turned out so that a hand has something to hold onto in the dark and on the wrong side of the ladder.

The detail that everybody forgets is the landing. A flight that goes nowhere is a set of steps; what makes it a fire escape is the horizontal platform at the top, big enough for two people and a stretcher, with the next flight starting at the opposite end of it. The whole structure is a chain of those landings, and the zigzag is not a style decision: it is what lets a stack of flights climb a wall while turning the climber back on themselves between floors.

Flight 02 · the rust

The rust

Wrought iron that has not been kept in paint will go to orange scale in about a decade, and then it will go to holes in about a decade after that. The failure is always the same and it is always at a joint: the paint fails first where two pieces of iron are bolted together, because that is where water sits, and the bolt head goes first, and a bolt head that has lost its nut is the beginning of a flight that will not take anybody's weight.

What the eye reads as a fire escape going soft is really a schedule slipping. A structure that was painted every three years is now painted every eight, and the first visible symptom is not a leak but an orange streak under the third-floor landing where the water has been running for a decade. The people who walk past it every day have stopped seeing it, which is the real argument for the annual inspection rather than the notice served on the owner.

Flight 03 · the anchor

The anchor

A fire escape is held to a wall by a small number of points, and each of them is a decision about the wall rather than about the iron. In a solid brick wall you drill and grout a bolt into the masonry. In a panel or framed wall there is nothing to drill into, so the bracket is bolted across the whole frame and the load goes into the studs. In a stone facade you cannot drill at all without consent, and the escape is held on a frame standing clear of the building on its own legs.

The consequence is that a fire escape is really a record of what the wall turned out to be. On the older buildings in this city it is brick, and the brackets go in four hundred millimetres and hold. On the ones built in the sixties the escape is a steel frame bolted to the slab edge, and it can be seen from the street as a separate object standing off the wall, because the wall is a curtain and there is nothing behind it worth anchoring to.

Flight 04 · the bell

The bell

Fire escapes were built in a city that mostly had no sprinklers and a great many wooden staircases, and the whole apparatus is aimed at one specific scenario: somebody on the fourth floor at two in the morning with a stair full of smoke behind them. Everything in the design follows from that, including the awkwardness. The drop ladder at the bottom is for people who cannot climb; the counterweights are for the people who can; and the gate on each landing is to stop a panicked mistake of jumping from the wrong floor.

None of it helps much if the alarm does not reach the floor. That was the argument of the last forty years: a means of escape that is not announced is only an ornament, and the evidence for a means of escape being used at all is very thin. What is left standing after all of that is honest enough, which is why an old fire escape on a brick building is still one of the most reassuring things you can see on a street, and why several cities have started listing them.

Flight 05 · the inspection

The inspection

The annual check is almost always visual. Somebody stands on each landing, hangs off the handrail with a decent amount of body weight, looks at the bolts, looks at the masonry around the anchors, looks for the orange streaks, and writes a date on a tag. It catches the failures that begin as corrosion and it catches very little else, because the failure it cannot catch is the one where a flight was overloaded for a decade and the bolts are still perfectly corroded-looking but no longer in the wall.

Even that has a fix, and the fix is expensive. Testing a fire escape properly means a drop test with a dead load or a proof load on every landing, done by contractors with a cradle, and it is done on a building at a time because it takes a fortnight. The best records are the ones kept by the owner rather than the contractor, because a contractor's tag is a statement about the day they came and an owner's file is a statement about the twenty years.

Flight 06 · the legacy

The legacy

Most of these are now not going to be used. Sprinklers, protected stairs, and a decade of nobody dying in a tenement fire have together made the external escape a survival mechanism rather than a plan, and the honest position is that in a modern building it is a second route that is never the route anybody takes. Several of them are simply in the way, and have been taken down over a night by a contractor with a lorry.

What is left is a type of building. The zigzag is a piece of ironwork language that came over from the fire stair of a ship, went onto the outside of a warehouse in the eighteen sixties, and ended up on every building in the district that was tall, cheap and built in brick. It is a form that expresses exactly one thing, which is that the people on the upper floors were not expected to have another way out, and it is worth reading once, properly, before the last of them comes down.
