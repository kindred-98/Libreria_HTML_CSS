Bench 3 · Tuesday, 07:40 · oak in the clamps

Every tool in the shop, on one belt.

A joinery works of four benches keeps its tools in eight bands: measuring, cutting, jointing, clamping, finishing, layout, hardware and coatings. The bank above runs sideways the way the belt ran in the old shop, tiles lift when you put the pointer on them, and every tile is a link straight to the paragraph that explains it.

Tenon saw blade, 19 in

The tool count on the band is the shop's own: thirty four pieces on the wall, plus the two bench planes that never leave the bench they were ground for.

01Measuring

Nothing is cut before it is measured twice

The measuring band holds the six tools that decide where a line goes. The boxwood rule is the oldest thing on the wall and it is the only one we refuse to let apprentices touch for the first month, because a rule marked in inches by a nineteenth-century engraver teaches a kind of care that a modern tape never will.

Dividing calipers are the other two. They were inherited from the founder's pattern shop and they are still used every week to step out the pin positions on a drawer front, because a stepped distance is faster than a calculated one and it can be checked by eye at arm's length.

02Cutting

Saws, chisels and the discipline of the bevel

The cutting band is five tools and no more, and the shop is quietly proud of that. A twenty inch panel saw with five points per inch, a brass-backed backsaw for the shoulders, a tenon saw fine enough for a half-inch tail, a one inch bench chisel and a two inch smoothing iron.

Iron is sharpened, not sharpened-up. A quarter inch of hollow grind is taken out with a coarse file and a stone in about nine minutes, once a week, and every iron in this shop has been sharpened the same way since 1961. A dull chisel is a chiselled wrist, and a chiselled wrist is how a drawer front ends up with a curve in it.

03Jointing

Mortise and tenon, the joint that lets the timber move

A housed joint in oak has to be loose enough to survive the winter. The plough plane cuts the groove to a measured depth, the mortise chisel squares the end, and the tenon is pared to a hair under so the drawbore can close it. The router on this band is the only powered tool on the wall, and it is used for housings that would take a day by hand.

Wedged through tenons are the default for anything that will be moved. The wedge is cut slightly oversize so the driving compresses the shoulders, and a well-fitted through tenon is stronger than the tenon itself, which is the whole point of the exercise and the reason the joint is still taught first.

04Clamping

Enough clamps, and a plan for the day

Six bar clamps, a face clamp for the panel saw, and a leg vise that is older than everyone who has used it. The rule in the shop is that you have to be able to name the clamp you need before you start, because the alternative is discovering at four in the afternoon that the glue has already begun to grab.

Parallel clamping blocks keep the pressure along the length rather than at the ends, which matters on a panel that is two and a half metres wide. A caul is used over the top so the jaw marks land in the offcut, not in the visible face, and the offcut is kept for exactly that reason.

05Finishing

A surface, not a skin

Finishing is a sanding problem before it is a coating problem. Four grits, always in order, never skipping one because the surface looks fine, because the scratch you cannot see at arm's length is the one the light finds at eight in the evening.

The scraper is the last tool of all. A cabinet scraper carded to a curl takes the surface flat without touching the grain, and a carded scraper leaves a surface that takes oil deeper than anything sanded to four hundred. A burnisher is used last of all on the inside of a drawer, where a hand will go for a hundred years.

06Layout

Knife lines, not pencil lines

Every line on this bench is cut with a marking knife, because a knife line has a shoulder and a pencil line does not. The knife stops the chisel at a place the eye can trust, and it leaves a witness mark on the joint face that shows at reassembly whether a component has been swapped.

A spokeshave is the awkward one and the indispensable one. It takes a curve out of a solid board in a way no plane can, and the shop keeps it sharpened and honed for exactly that work rather than flattening it into a general tool. A bevel gauge and a compass complete the band, and both are used to check the two others.

07Hardware

Ironmongery chosen at the bench

Hinges are bought in the week they are needed and held against the finished stile, because a 35 millimetre butt hinge that suits a door will not necessarily suit its frame. The band carries butt hinges, cup hinges, a full extension slide, a turned knob and two escutcheons, all in one finish so the drawer run matches.

Nothing is lacquered on the bench. A drawer that slides for a century will scratch, and a scratch in a lacquered surface is a black line, while a scratch in oiled oak is a slightly darker line in a surface that was already varied. The shop prefers the second problem and says so to every client who asks.

08Coatings

Oil, wax, and one tin of shellac

Hardwax oil for the doors, beeswax and a lint-free cloth for the tops, and a shellac that is thirty years old and still used for sealing the end grain of a tenon. Nothing else is allowed on this bench, and the tin of shellac is the only thing in the shop that gets an apology before it is opened.

Oiling is done in three coats, cut back with a fine abrasive between the second and the third, and left for a week before the piece leaves. A shop that oils furniture once is selling a finish, not making a surface, and the difference shows in four years when the customer calls about a bloom mark that would have been sanded out with a fifth coat.

09The shop

Four benches and a rule about the radio

Alder and Vane has been on the same corner since 1951 and has done nothing since but cabinet work. Four benches, eleven people, no production line, and a job book that still has a column headed what the client actually wanted written in pencil by somebody in 1978.

Visitors are welcome on Thursdays. The only rule is that the radio stays off during the finishing band, which is not superstition: the dust from a shellac rag travels further than anyone expects, and a shop that cannot hear itself is a shop that cannot hear a client changing their mind.
