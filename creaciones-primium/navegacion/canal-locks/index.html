Levels and machinery

Blue is the water in the cut. It stands at the level of the chamber the reader is in, so the whole cut fills and empties as the flight is walked, because the entire purpose of a lock is to lift a boat without lifting a river.

Rust is the gate ironwork. The leaves swing on their arms and the point of the gate is mitred so it presses upstream, which is why they close against the water and not with it.

Stone is the wall of the cut. The top three chambers are four hundred years old and the bottom two are ninety, and the difference shows in the coursing if you look for it.

The pale band on the stone is the weed line, the level of the worst summer on record. It has never been repainted and it is the only gauge in the district that nobody argues with.

Chamber 1 · 67.2 m · head of the flight

Nivel del Vado

The top of the flight, where the cut stops being a cut and becomes a canal, and where the gates above stand open more often than not because everything up there is at the same level and there is nothing for a gate to do. They were cast in 1822 in a foundry that no longer exists, and the pintles they turn on are the original ones, worn into a shape a modern replacement would not be.

A lock is a machine for convincing water that it is somewhere else. You shut the gate behind you, open a paddle two feet up the wall, and the chamber fills from the floor until it is level, which takes four minutes and looks like nothing at all. From this chamber the towpath runs for eleven kilometres without a lock, and the whole idea of the flight, two seasons of building and a hundred and thirty years of paying for it, is over.

Chamber 2 · 66.1 m · built around a kiln

Nivel del Horno

The second chamber is the one nobody photographs, because from the towpath it looks exactly like the first and it is not. The engineers of 1822 put it where it is because a lime kiln was already standing on the bank, and the chamber walls are partly the kiln. The curved end on the upstream side is not a design feature; it is a lime oven that was left where it was found.

The drop from one chamber to the next is a metre and a tenth, which sounds like nothing and takes about ninety seconds to do. The whole flight falls five and a half metres in total, which is why it was built at all, and why the mill race that used to run here was abandoned in 1968 without anybody bothering to write an obituary for it.

Chamber 3 · 65.0 m · the middle chamber

Esclusa Vieja

The middle of the flight and the only chamber rebuilt, in 1908, in the same stone and, as it turns out, to a different plan. It is two metres shorter than the others and its gates do not quite close on the first attempt, which the keeper corrects by hand every single time and which the operating instructions record as though it were a feature rather than a fault that has been paid for every year since.

Coming down the flight you open the gate above and let the chamber drain, and the water leaves through a culvert under the floor rather than over the sill, so the surface goes down quietly and nothing arrives at the next gate in a hurry. It is the quietest four minutes on the canal, and every keeper will tell you so, and every keeper will also tell you about the gate that does not shut first time.

Chamber 4 · 63.9 m · the longest chamber

Nivel de la Comedera

Twenty-six metres of chamber, the longest on the canal, and the reason a boat takes a quarter of an hour to go through a lock that is otherwise indistinguishable from its neighbours. Everything about it works, and all of it works slowly, and the walls are the coldest in the flight because they are the thickest and because the ground behind them is a gravel bank that was never meant to be cut.

There is a bench of dry stone in the middle of the chamber on the upstream side, put there for somebody to stand on in 1830 and never removed. Standing on it while the chamber fills is the best twenty minutes of the whole descent, and the gate keeper pretends not to notice and does not hurry you when you get off.

Chamber 5 · 62.8 m · ironwork of 1974

Nivel de las Ánimas

The only chamber on the flight with gates that are not original, put in during a single winter in 1974 by a crew of four who replaced both leaves and the machinery in eleven weeks and were extremely pleased with themselves. The ironwork is a different colour from every other gate on the canal and it will be for another two hundred years.

The name is older and has nothing to do with anybody who died here, as everybody assumes. It is a translation of a word for a boundary marker between two parishes, and the boundary ran through the chamber. The gate that replaced the old one was set half a metre upstream of where the old one was, on the parish side, and nobody has ever decided whether that was on purpose.

Chamber 6 · 61.7 m · level with the river

Nivel de la Noria

The bottom of the flight and the first thing any boat meets coming the other way: one chamber, two gates, and a drop of a metre and a tenth that takes about four minutes to do properly. The lower gates are oak four hundred years old and are still, in the strict sense, the original ones, reinforced twice and repaired so often that nobody would now call them original.

The waterline here is level with the river for about a hundred and forty metres and then begins, very slowly, to climb. The cut was built to take a mill that is no longer there, and the last two hundred metres of it are the remains of a mill race, so a boat leaving at six in the morning is arriving at a river that took a whole afternoon to get there. Boats leave at six and again at one, and the keeper has kept the log of every passage for forty-one years.
