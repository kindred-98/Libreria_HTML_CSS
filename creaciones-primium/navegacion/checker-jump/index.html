The garnet man is always on the square you are reading. It travels by jumping, which is the whole point of a draughts man and the only move in the game that is not a slide.

A square with a cross on it is a square that has already been passed and taken. A crossed square cannot be stood on again.

The far row is dark and has no numbers on this board. It is there so the near row reads as a row of a board and not as a strip of buttons.

Square 1 · the start of the row

Under the Chessboard

The twelve square board did not arrive by itself in Europe in the fifteenth century. It appears, fully formed, in a tenth century tomb in Egypt, carved on the lid of a game chest that now sits in a museum and shows two players with pieces arranged in rows on a checkered ground. The men on it are round and the board is ten by ten, which is not the number anybody expected.

So the short game did not come from a long game by being cut down. It arrived as a different game with a different board, and the resemblance to chess, which is the source of the word draughts and of a century of players assuming the two are cousins, is a resemblance in the furniture rather than in the rules. Chess is a game of threat. Draughts is a game of invitation.

Square 2 · ten by ten, and a chest lid

A Game of the Tenth Century

On the carved lid the pieces are already in their opening arrangement, twelve to a side, which is the number the modern game still uses. The board is ten by ten with the four corner squares left out, a shape that has never been proposed as an improvement by anybody, and the two players are carved facing each other with the pieces between them, one of them turning a piece over with his fingers.

Everything in the modern game is in that lid, including the fact that the turn is taken by moving a piece onto a dark square, which is the rule the players at the tomb appear to be playing. What the lid does not show is a king, and a game of draughts without a king, played with the same board and the same men, is a different game with the same name in every language that has one.

Square 3 · eight by eight, cut on the diagonal

The Short Board

The eight by eight board was cut from the ten by ten along one diagonal only, which is a piece of geometry so lazy and so clever that it is still the reason the two versions disagree about the first move. A diagonal cut through a ten square board removes two corner squares and leaves eight in the corner; a cut through both diagonals removes four and leaves the symmetrical board that almost every country now plays.

The result is that draughts comes in families. The international game is played on eight by eight with twenty four men a side and men moving diagonally forward only until they are promoted. English draughts is played on the same board with twelve a side, men moving forward and backward, and it is the only widely played version in which a man that reaches the far row and declines to be crowned simply stays a man and moves back again.

Square 4 · promotion and the crown

Men and Kings

A man that crosses the board is crowned, and the crowning is the single most contested moment in the whole game. Under most rules the man stops on the far row and waits, and if the opponent is given a chance to capture it, the man is taken before it is ever crowned and a great many players have spent a decade playing under a rule that quietly punished them for the pause.

A king is the same piece of wood with a different letter marked on it, and a king moves any distance along a diagonal. The board is small enough that a king can cross it in one move and the games where one appears early are decided quickly, which is why the opening of a real game is a long sequence of deliberate refusals. Two men that could be promoted are left sitting on the far row for thirty moves, and both players know exactly what is being declined.

Square 5 · the jump, and why it is compulsory

The Move and the Capture

A man moves one square diagonally, forward only in most of the world, and captures by jumping over an adjacent enemy man into the empty square beyond it. The jump is the reason the game is beautiful and the reason it is unpleasant: a capture is mandatory, so a player with three available captures does not choose the best one, and the length of the resulting chain decides the game far more often than skill does.

European draughts goes further and demands that every capture on the board be taken, in chains, in either order, before anything else happens. There are recorded positions in which sixty or seventy moves must be played in a single turn, and a player obliged to start such a chain has been known to resign on the spot rather than begin it. The man on the header above jumps because that is what a man is for.

Square 6 · the rule that had to be written down

The Rule of the Square

The last rule is the one nobody used to need. If a man reaches the far row and is not crowned, it moves sideways, and if the argument about whether that sideways move is legal is settled by pointing at the man rather than at a book. This is the rule of the square, and the reason it exists at all is that the promotion row is only one square deep.

Every tradition of the game has patched the same hole differently. Some refuse the crown and the man simply walks back, which turns a promotion into a two move affair and changes the value of the far row completely. Some crown it immediately and let the king sit under the gun while the opponent decides whether to take it. The board on this page has a far row, and it has no men on it, and the reason is that this argument has been running for about two hundred years without a result.
