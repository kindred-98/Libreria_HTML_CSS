Paper Fan

The header of this page is a folding fan lying open on a table. Six paper leaves are riveted to a bamboo stick, and the leaves are not fixed: whichever chapter you are reading is dealt to the centre of the fan, and the other five deal out to either side of it, tightest near the middle and loosest at the tips, the way cards come off a deal. The leaf you are reading also takes the red seal.

Leaf I

The First Fold

A fan begins as a single sheet, folded sixteen times like a small accordion, and it is not cut until the very end. The maker scores the folds with a blunt point, runs a damp cloth along them, and leaves the sheet under a board overnight so the creases take. A fold that has not been pressed will spring open on its own the first time it is used.

Sixteen folds is the usual number and it is not arbitrary. It is the most a sheet will take and still close smoothly on a rivet, and every maker who has tried fifteen or seventeen has ended up with a fan that either gapes at the tip or will not stay shut in a bag.

Leaf II

The Ribs

Strips of bamboo or bone are glued to the back of the sheet, one for every second fold, and then the whole thing is rolled from the first leaf to the last so the sticks lie flat. A fan with thin sticks flexes in the hand; a fan with thick ones holds an angle and does not. Most of the weight of a good fan is in the sticks, and the paper is there to be looked at.

At the base of the bundle a hole is drilled through everything at once, paper and sticks together, and a rivet is passed and peened. That rivet is the only fixed point in the object. Every angle you ever see in a fan is measured from it, which is why the fans at the head of this page are dealt around one small metal point.

Leaf III

Dealing the Leaves

When you open a fan in the hand you do not open it evenly. The leaves nearest the thumb travel a long way and the ones at the far end barely move, so the spread is always tight at one side and wide at the other. It looks like an accident of the wrist until you watch somebody who has done it ten thousand times, and then you can see that the unevenness is the point.

The leaves here are dealt that way. The one you are reading sits in the middle; the next one out is a small angle away, the one after that is further, and by the fifth the tip has swung a long way. Change chapter and the whole deal changes, because the fan is re-dealt from the rivet every time.

Leaf IV

The Red Seal

A painted fan is signed with a seal, pressed into the wet pigment in cinnabar or in the red of a cheap aniline that fades in about fifteen years. The seal is the maker's whole claim to authorship, and on the older fans it is placed off centre, near the tip of a leaf, where the signature will still be visible when the fan is almost closed.

That is why the seal on this one jumps. It is not decoration added to the page: in a fan the seal is the only part that says which leaf is the important one, and when the leaves are re-dealt the seal has to move with the leaf it belongs to.

Leaf V

A Fan's Season

Paper fans are made for a season and then, if they are good, kept for forty years. The traditional ones come out at the hot months and go back into their boxes, because paper that has been swung about in the damp will cockle and never lie flat again. A fan that has been left open on a table overnight wakes up with a set it will keep.

Cleaning is a dry business. A soft brush takes the dust off in one direction and never the other, and anyone who has tried to wash one has learned that the pigment lifts long before the paper does. The safest thing is to do almost nothing at all, and to store it rolled with the leaves closed.

Leaf VI

Folding It Back

Closing a fan is done in one movement, from the tip, gathering the leaves into the palm and letting them roll shut around the rivet. You do not push the leaves into place one at a time. A fanned fan is a spring, and the only correct way to deal with a spring is to let it collapse in the direction it was already going.

Then the tassel goes over the head of the bundle and the fan is a stick again, which is what it is for most of its life. The closed fan is the object; the open one is the fortnight a year when the object is allowed to be a picture.

The fan is the navigation: every leaf is a link into the chapters below, and the sealed leaf is the chapter you are reading.
