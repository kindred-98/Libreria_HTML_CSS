Cog Mesh

The header of this page is a gear train, cut and keyed into an oiled cast-iron bed. Only one wheel at a time is bolted to the output shaft. That wheel is the one that turns, and it takes its neighbour round with it against the meshing, which is exactly what the machine would do if you dropped a key into the right hub this morning. The counter and the sight glass read the position; the other four wheels wait, dark and dry.

Wheel I · the sump

The Oil

A gear train does not run on metal. It runs on a film of oil about four microns thick between two surfaces that never touch, and every calculation in the workshop is really a calculation about keeping that film in place. Too little and the film breaks, the teeth touch, and you get the grey scoring you can see on a wheel that has been run dry for an hour. Too much and the film thickens into something closer to syrup, carries the heat away faster than the teeth can be repaired, and the wheel starts to sweat.

The glass on the left of this train is the oldest instrument in the machine and the only one anybody looked at. Oil climbs the outside of a bearing when the bearing is turning and falls back when it stops, so the level in a sight glass is a rough average of how much work the train has done. On this page the level is tied to the wheel you are reading, which is not how a gearbox works but is at least honest about where you are.

Wheel II · the module

The Pitch

Two wheels only mesh if they agree on the distance from centre to tooth, and that distance is called the module. A one-and-a-half module is a wheel with a pitch radius of twelve millimetres carrying sixteen teeth, and a wheel of the same module carrying twelve teeth has a pitch radius of nine. They touch at the pitch circles and the contact ratio works out at a little under one and a half, which is the number that decides whether a tooth is in the mesh once or twice while it passes through the engagement.

Change the module and the wheels will not touch. This is the whole of gear design in one sentence: two wheels, one module, and everything else is the consequence. The pinions in this train are the smaller of the two sizes precisely because a small wheel with a small number of teeth is the fragile one, and the fragility is where the machine tells you about itself.

Wheel III · the train

The Train

Put a small wheel on a shaft and a large wheel next to it and you have made a reduction. The small wheel has to turn faster by exactly the ratio of the two pitch radii to make the teeth leave the mesh at the same rate they arrived, and no sooner do you add a third wheel than the ratios start multiplying instead of adding. A clock with a wheel for hours, a wheel for minutes and a wheel for seconds is not three wheels; it is one wheel and two reductions, and the arithmetic is done entirely in the diameters.

Direction is the other consequence, and it is the one that catches people. Every mesh reverses the sense of rotation, so a train of an odd number of wheels ends up turning the opposite way to the one that drives it. Nobody builds a train to get that; it is simply what the teeth do, and it is why a reversing lever on a lathe is a pair of extra wheels rather than a clever linkage.

Wheel IV · the keyway

The Key

A gear that is only resting on a shaft is friction doing the work, and friction is the one thing a gear is trying to avoid. So the wheel that has to drive something is pinned to its shaft by a key: a small rectangle of steel, half in the hub and half in the shaft, with rounded ends filed by hand so it slides in without hammering the wheel. The key is the only part of the assembly that transmits torque, and it is the part that everybody in the workshop can identify at a glance.

So the wheel you are reading on this page is keyed and the other five are not. That is the whole difference in the mechanism, and it is why only one of them turns. A wheel with a key in it is under load; a wheel without one is merely adjacent to something that is.

Wheel V · the pattern

The Wear

Every worn gear tells you how it failed. Scoring across the face of the teeth means the film of oil failed and metal met metal; pitting means the same thing, but later, after the oil finally got back in. A bright band down the middle of a tooth means the load never reached the ends, which is what a correctly meshed wheel looks like. A wheel that has been run dry shows the wear at the tip first, because the tip is where two teeth are pushing hardest and where the film is thinnest.

Brass is chosen for the pinions not because it is tough but because it is soft, and a soft wheel that fails does it by wearing a flat rather than by snapping a tooth into the machine. Steel wears slowly, brass wears quickly, and the workshop keeps spare brass on the shelf for the reason a hospital keeps the expensive plate to hand. Every seized gearbox in the world has a bright smear on one small wheel and a dark smear on everything else.

Wheel VI · the lock

The Stop

You stop a train by holding the wheel that drives it, and you never stop it by gripping the wheel it drags. Put a finger on the output and the load runs back through the train into the shaft, which is where the bearing is sized for it. Put a finger on a driven wheel and you are fighting a torque that has already been multiplied by every ratio behind you, and the wheel you touched will be the one with the new flat on it by morning.

This is the last wheel on the train and it is also the reason the other five are allowed to sit still. A gearbox with an escapement in it is a gearbox that can be told to wait, and waiting is the only instruction a machine ever obeys without being turned by hand. Drop the key into a different hub on this page and the train stops there, and the counter above the bed reads the wheel you have chosen.

The train is the navigation: every wheel is a link into the notes below, and the keyed wheel is the chapter you are reading.
