Sheet one · six of one hundred and four

Mounts, hinges and glue

A mount is a square of card with four corner hinges cut into it. Nothing is glued until it is decided, and on this sheet four things have been decided and two have not.

An undecided stamp sits loose, on its hinge, at the angle at which it fell. The angle is not a style. It is the record of how long ago anybody handled it.

The album spine is not decoration either. Collectors used to mark a page by feel, from the edge, and the spine is where the finger went.

Mount one · 2d · issued 1897

The Lighthouse At Kestrel Point

The first stamp in the album and the first one ever issued for the coast, printed from an engraving of the tower as it looked in 1874, which is to say before the balcony was added. The balcony was added in 1881 and the design was never redrawn, so every denomination of the first issue shows a lighthouse that no longer existed.

It is a cheap plate, deliberately, so that the postage for a letter from the headland cost one and a half pence all the way to the county. A later issue raised it and a great many letters stopped coming from the headland, which is the kind of thing a stamp album is really about and never says.

Mount two · 3d · issued 1911

The Ferry Under Way

Designed in the year the ferry was bought, and it shows the vessel broadside with a wake, which had not been done before and has been copied on the coast ever since. The engraver is recorded as having been given exactly one hour with the original and having spent the first twenty minutes asking what the wake was for.

This copy has a constant thinned plate across the word FERRY, so that ER is lighter than the rest, which the catalogue records as a plate wear and which the post office at the time recorded as a printing error. Both are true and only one of them mattered to the people who used the stamps.

Mount three · 5d · issued 1923

Harbour At Low Water

The stamp the collector in this album wanted for forty years and acquired in 1957 for the price of a dinner, from a stationer who had had one in a drawer since the war and did not know what it was. It is the frame of the issue, and the frame is empty on purpose: the engraver worked from a photograph taken from the harbour wall, and there is nothing in the picture but wet stone.

Four hundred and fifty copies were printed and the post office sold through them in nine days. The issue exists because the frame looked like a stamp and nobody on the committee wanted to be the one to say so at the meeting.

Mount four · 9d · issued 1938

Winter Gale

The only stamp in the album issued to raise money rather than to pay a rate, and it carries no denomination at all, only the words for the relief fund in three lines. It sold for more than the postage it replaced and it is the reason the issue is collected at all.

This copy arrived on a letter that had been through the nineteen-day gale and arrived anyway, so the corner is creased and the top edge is soft. It has never been cleaned. Cleaning it would remove the only evidence the album has of what the post was doing at the time.

Mount five · 12d · issued 1951

The Lighthouse Keeper

A portrait stamp of a man standing in a doorway holding a lamp, and the man is identified in the post office record as the keeper at Kestrel Point, which makes it the only portrait issue on the coast. He had been keeper for twenty-two years and the light had been automated two years earlier, so the stamp commemorates a job that had already ended.

The face was engraved from a photograph taken during the war by a visiting journalist, and the photograph has one imperfection, a shadow across the left eye that the engraver reproduced faithfully and that the post office spent four months querying before approving the plate.

Mount six · halfpenny · printed 1962, never issued

Printed And Never Issued

A halfpenny of 1962 that was printed, given to the postmasters for sale, and withdrawn eleven days before the sale date, because the halfpenny was being abolished and it was decided, late, that a coastal issue would be undignified. Nine hundred and forty sheets were destroyed and this is believed to be the only survivor.

It has no gum and it is not worth anything. It is in the album because the catalogue entry for 1962 is a single line, four words long, and the stamp is the only way that line has ever had a picture. It is also the reason the last mount on this sheet has a hinge that does not quite hold.
