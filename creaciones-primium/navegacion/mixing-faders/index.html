Channel 1 · headroom

Gain before anything

The first thing a desk taught its operators was that level is decided at the very front of the chain and never again. Set the microphone preamp so the loudest thing in the room leaves a few decibels of room on the meter, and everything after that point becomes a simple question of how much you want to hear. Get it wrong and no fader, no tone control and no amount of patience can bring the noise back down.

Broadcast desks of the nineteen-forties made the rule visible. Every channel carried a gain wheel and a small meter, and the operator was expected to ride the wheel as the room filled up, keeping peaks under the lamp without ever pushing the amplifier into its own distortion.

Channel 2 · the lever

Hands on the faders

A fader is a small machine for one variable. The travel is short, the law is exponential enough to feel musical, and the cap is sized so a thumb can find it without looking. On a six channel desk the caps form a line you read like text, which is why the eye keeps returning to that line even when the music is somewhere else.

Channel two on this desk is the one that moves on its own. When the notes reach this paragraph the cap climbs to unity and stays there, and the little lamp above it warms up: the console is telling you where you are in the take, not asking you to remember.

Channel 3 · sources

Tape, ribbon and wire

Before the console there was the patch bay: cables dragged across a rack, a ribbon microphone hanging from a boom, and a tape machine started by hand. Every source arrived at the desk raw, unbalanced and unlabelled, and the engineer learned the sound of each one by the way its fader felt under the fingers.

Those cables are why the strips were numbered and not named. A number is a promise you can read from across the room, and the label pencil written underneath it was the only documentation the desk ever had.

Channel 4 · summing

The summing bus

Six channels end up in one place, and that place is the bus. Everything the desk knows about balance, about which voice belongs on top, about how wide the room should feel, is decided by the relative position of six caps feeding a single amplifier. Strip the caps back to the same height and the mix becomes a committee.

Bad buses were where the noise floor lived. Every stage added a little hiss, and by the time six sources were summed the quiet passages sounded like weather. The engineers who learned this fastest were the ones who turned everything up and listened to what was there when nothing was.

Channel 5 · monitors

The listening room

The desk does not lie, but the room does. A control room adds its own bass, its own reflections and its own opinions, and a mix balanced there will fall apart in a car, a club or a pair of headphones. The best engineers of the studio era cut their monitors down until the mix was dull everywhere and only came alive in the room they trusted.

Switching off is part of the method. Turn the reference monitors down, let the ears adjust, and the balance you hear is closer to the one your listener will hear, standing up, in a different building, on a different night.

Channel 6 · the print

Printing the mix

At the end of the session the desk is only a control surface. The mix has to leave the room somehow: printed to tape by one pass of the whole programme, handed over as a file with the moves written down, or left as a set of stems that somebody else will argue with later. Whatever the medium, the decision stops being reversible at the moment the print button goes down.

So the last channel is the one nobody wants to move first. It sits where the night engineer put it, it lights when you reach this paragraph, and it holds the position until the take is over and the caps all fall back to zero together.
