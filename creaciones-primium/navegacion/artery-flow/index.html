Vessel 1 of 6 · Heart

01

Heart

Everything in this page begins as a squeeze. The heart does not push blood around the body so much as flick it: a sharp contraction that raises the pressure in the aorta, then a relaxation that lets the elastic wall of the artery take over and keep the flow going while the muscle rests.

That handoff is the whole trick. Without the elastic recoil, every beat would be a separate gulp and the organs would feel each one as a small internal earthquake. The vessel is not a pipe. It is a spring that happens to be hollow.

02

Branch

The aorta does not stay large. It divides, again and again, and each division is a negotiation between two opposing needs: a wide lumen moves a lot of blood quickly, a narrow one moves it fast under high pressure. The tree settles on branches a few millimetres across and calls it a day.

Blood that has left the heart at a brisk walk arrives at the smallest vessels barely moving. The system spends its pressure early so that the last stretch, the one that matters most, can be slow and gentle enough to let things out.

03

Bed

A capillary is a single cell wide, which is why it takes a network rather than a tube. Laid end to end, the capillaries in an adult would run to something like a hundred thousand kilometres, a cabling job whose only purpose is to put every square centimetre of tissue within a few millimetres of a supply.

Here the wall is one layer of flat cells with gaps in it. Oxygen, glucose and waste cross by diffusion, squeezed through those gaps under pressure, and the pressure is what keeps the whole arrangement pointing one way. Reverse it and the bed fills with fluid instead of blood.

04

Pressure

Pressure is not a feeling, it is a number with a range. Too little and the organs go hungry; too much and the smallest vessels, the ones with no muscle to contract, simply tear. What makes the system survivable is that almost every vessel can change its own diameter in seconds.

Arteries do it with a thick ring of smooth muscle that clamps down or lets go. Capillaries cannot, which is why they live inside organs that wrap them in their own pressure regulation. The vessels you can feel in your wrist are the ones that negotiated hardest for control.

05

Return

Half the volume in the circuit is on the way back, and it has a harder job. Blood arrives dark and burdened with carbon dioxide, and it has to climb uphill against gravity, from the ankles to the chest, without a pump of its own.

It manages with muscle. Every time the calf contracts while you walk it squeezes the vein, and one-way valves stop the squeezed segment from refilling backwards. Walking is, mechanically, a pump with a stride cycle. Standing still is not.

06

Long now

A vessel that has carried for fifty years is not the vessel it started as. The wall thickens and stiffens, the elastic recoil that smoothed out each beat gets worse, and the pulse arrives at the far end a little harder and a little later.

None of it is dramatic enough to notice day to day. The system is built for a slow decline, and the surprising thing is how much of it is simply maintenance: eat, walk, sleep, and the pump keeps its contract for a very long time.
