Mycelial net

Six things a fungal network does that a root system cannot. Touch a node in the card at the foot of the page and the hyphae around it will take light, the way a real net conducts the disturbance a few centimetres and then forgets it.

Network Forage Exchange Wood Fruiting Decay

01

Network

The visible fungus is a fruit. The organism is the net behind it, and in a mature woodland the net is the larger part by a very long way: kilometres of thread per square metre of litter, fusing with itself wherever two filaments meet by chance.

Because the threads fuse, the structure is a network rather than a set of plants. Materials move between fruiting bodies that may be metres and different species apart, and the whole thing behaves, locally, a great deal like a distributed nervous system with no neurons in it.

02

Forage

A hypha does not forage the way an animal does. It extends, and the chemistry of the tip reads the substrate directly: nitrogen, phosphorus, carbon and a long list of simple molecules that trigger growth in some species and stop it in others.

The result is a front that explores and consolidates at the same time, thickening wherever the return is good and dying back where it is not. From the outside, and from well above the soil, this is a search pattern that any animal ecologist would recognise.

03

Exchange

Trees connected by fungal networks have been shown to move carbon and nitrogen between them, preferentially towards seedlings growing in shade, and the experiments behind that claim are still argued over in detail.

What is not argued over is that mycorrhizal fungi trade. The fungus supplies phosphorus and water in exchange for a tenth to a fifth of everything the plant fixes photosynthetically, and that is an enormous amount of sugar to spend on a partnership.

04

Wood

Lignin is the reason wood lasts, and it is the reason a dead tree is not food. White rot fungi solved that by evolving the strongest oxidative enzymes known, capable of breaking a chemical bond that was deliberately designed to be unbreakable.

The process is slow and it is selective. Brown rots leave the cellulose behind and strip the lignin, so the wood goes brittle and light; white rots remove the lignin first and leave pale crumbly structure. Which one you are looking at tells you which strategy you are dealing with.

05

Fruiting

A mushroom is a delivery mechanism. Everything the net built over months or years is spent on a short-lived structure whose only job is to get spores into moving air, and a good cap manages the boundary layer over its gills to within a fraction of a millimetre.

Fruiting is also tightly tied to carbon supply in the net, which is why a good year for mushrooms follows a good year for the trees above them, and why a drought that barely changes the woodland floor can silence an entire flush.

06

Decay

Every fungal network is temporary, and every one of them is in a hurry. The average thread of a litter decomposer lives days or weeks, and the network is rebuilt constantly from the carbon that the dead thread releases as it goes.

It is a process that looks, from the top of the soil, permanent, because the wood it is working on will be there for a century. Underneath, the organism is anything but: a rolling population of filaments, born at a rate of metres per day, competing with everything else in the same handful of grams of soil.
