Reading the fan

The card standing out of the fan is the chapter you are in. It is dealt face up and squared to the table, and nothing else on the cloth is dealt at all.

Cards already read turn face down and lie into the discard side of the fan. They keep their place in the order so the hand can be counted after the fact.

The little red pip in the corner of each card is the printing house mark. Four of the six still carry it; the two late reprints carry the number instead.

The edge of the table is worn pale where forearms rest. On a real table that wear is the only honest record of how long the game went on.

Card I · the pack before it was a pack

The Origin of the Pack

Cards almost certainly began as something other than cards. The oldest surviving examples are leaves, slips of paper with writing on one side and nothing on the other, which is not how anybody plays a game and is exactly how you would print an almanac. There is a ninth century Chinese text that mentions paper money used to buy things from street sellers, and the leap from a slip of paper to a slip of paper with a picture on it is a very short one.

What is certain is that by the fourteenth century the thing had a back, because a card with nothing on the reverse cannot be picked up without looking at it, and a game you cannot handle is not a game. The earliest known European backs are plain, crosshatched, and made for the sole purpose of being invisible for the two seconds the card is in the air.

Card II · suits, bells, leaves, hearts

The Long Road East

The four suits that everybody now uses travelled a very long way to get here, and the names in several languages still show the journey. Italian players called them swords, cups, clubs and coins, and the suits were not religious at that point, they were the four things that could actually be won: a fight, a cup of wine, a cudgel, and money.

French players took the same four and renamed them to match the ordinary deck of playing cards already in use at the table, which was why the ace of spades in France is still called the ace of spears in parts of Italy. The German suit of hearts became the French heart, the German bell became the French diamond, and the names have been wrong in exactly the same direction for six hundred years.

Card III · hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades

The French Suit

The suit that won is the one that carries a number of ways of losing, and the court cards are the most interesting objects on the table. Each of the three face cards began as a picture with a name, and the names were fixed by a decree of 1526 in France that assigned ten figures to the kings, queens and jacks so that any court could print them. The decree exists, it is a real document, and it is the reason a German jack and an English jack are the same man in different clothes.

What is remarkable is how quickly all of this stopped being a change. The design of a fifty two card pack has not moved since the sixteenth century. Every addition since then has been to the outside of the deck: a joker in 1840 for the single joker game, and the two jokers of 1940, when a pack doubled to fifty four to carry back the two jokers a revival of euchre had temporarily made popular.

Card IV · number, suit, and a box in the corner

Corner and Pips

A playing card is a piece of information design under extreme pressure. It has to be identifiable at arm's length, in bad light, in about a fifth of a second, while a hand moves faster than the eye can track. The solution arrived early: put the rank and the suit in one corner, repeat it in the opposite corner, and leave the middle for a picture that the player will interpret emotionally rather than read.

The pips on the numbered cards are the other half of the answer. Early cards were court cards only, because court cards are the ones that carry narrative; when the numbered cards arrived the makers filled the middle with as many pips as the number said, arranged so that a card could be counted at a glance. The four of cups became four cups and stayed four cups, and the arrangement was copied so often that the ace of cups grew a leaf in the middle that nobody can explain and nobody has removed.

Card V · thumb at the top, two fingers behind

The Dealer Grip

Dealing is the one part of a card game that is done in public, at speed, by somebody the other people are watching, and it is also the part that takes the longest to learn. The grip is a thumb on the short edge of the pack and two fingers curled behind it, and the whole motion of the deal comes from the wrist. An experienced dealer deals a hand of thirteen in about nine seconds and the cards do not move out of line by a millimetre.

That consistency is the entire trick. A pack dealt by hand carries a small error in every card, and the errors lean consistently in one direction, which is why a badly shuffled pack gives a better game than a machine shuffle and why the shape of a hand is said to be the tell of a good dealer. The cards in this header are dealt in the sense that the fan closes on the chapter you are in and never rearranges itself, which is the one thing a real hand never does.

Card VI · the spring, the break, and the force

The Fan and the Sleight

A fan of cards is not a display. It is a way of counting quickly, and it is a spring for several other operations: the flick that sends a card into a hand, the break that bends a pack into two halves, and the cut that takes the top off and puts it at the bottom. All four are visible, all four are legal, and all four are performed a hundred times an hour by people who do not think of them as tricks.

The fan above is doing one of those jobs and only one. Six cards, a spring hinge at the bottom, and the chapter you are reading is the one that comes out of the fan, squared up and standing, while the rest stay where the deal put them. Everything the reader can see on the cloth is either a card in the hand or a card that has already been played, and the count in the top corner is the only number anybody needs.
