Soundings taken on the lower course

Bright water is the reach under the reader. It is the only part of the diagram drawn running.

Quiet water is the rest of the course. It is still moving; it is simply not the part you are on.

A post marks a landing. Posts are on alternate banks because the towpath changes sides at every one of them.

Stipple is mud, not water. In August two of the six landings cannot be reached at all.

Landing 01 · 0.4 m · north bank

Fuente del Humo

The river starts here in the way rivers rarely do: not in a spring you can stand beside, but in a wide gravel flat where the water comes up out of the ground over about two hundred metres and is already four feet wide when you can see it. On still mornings the whole flat steams. That is the smoke, and the place has been called that for longer than the road.

Nothing here is fast. The current is a walking pace and the channel wanders between gravel bars that change shape after every proper flood. Boats are poled, not rowed, for the first kilometre, which is a fact that surprises visitors and is entirely predictable from the state of the ground.

Landing 02 · 0.6 m · south bank

Vado de la Espina

Below the gravel flats the river gathers itself and picks a bank, and for two kilometres it runs hard against the south side against a cut of thorn and blackthorn so dense it looks like a wall. The landing is a gap in that wall, and the towpath crosses the water here because there is nowhere else to put it.

In August the water drops to two feet over the bar and everybody in the valley knows it, and the launch that normally runs the lower course is pulled up above the line and left there until the rains. In February the same landing is under a metre and a half of brown water moving fast enough to take a cart. Nothing about this stretch is repeatable, and the chart of it is more than a century out of date and still useful.

Landing 03 · 1.1 m · north bank

Molino Viejo

The river turns north here for the first time, a hard right-angle bend with a mill on the inside of it. The mill stopped grinding in 1961 and the wheel was never taken out, so it turns whenever there is enough water, slowly, making the noise of somebody sawing in another room. Nobody in the village lives there any more and the wheel still runs.

It is the best place on the course to learn the river, because the whole bend is visible at once and you can watch where the current actually goes rather than where you think it does. Fish do not take the inside of the bend. Boats do not either, whatever their pilot says on the first trip.

Landing 04 · 1.6 m · south bank

Zanja del Herrero

The deepest and the fastest reach on the whole lower course, and the one everybody local describes with the same three words: fast, cold, and no use stopping. The channel is cut hard into clay on the south side and the whole river is forced into a third of its width, and the sound of it is the loudest thing on the river for two kilometres.

There is a post on the bank, one of the six, and the name is a corruption of a word for the reeds that used to grow on the point and do not any more. A launch that stops here in high water will find its anchor dug out before the crew has the kettle on. The only sensible thing is to pass through and eat on the far bank.

Landing 05 · 1.4 m · north bank

Isla Larga

The river splits round a long low island of silt and reed, takes a kilometre to rejoin, and the channel that everybody uses is the northern one because the southern one silts up every year and never clears. Herons stand in the shallows in numbers that stop conversations. On a still evening the two channels make two different sounds.

Landing five is on the north bank at the upstream end of the island, on a spit of shingle that is dry in July and awash in October. The path in from the road crosses the southern channel on a footbridge built by the same people who built the mill and repaired roughly half as often. It is a good place to stop if you have time and a bad place to stop if you have not.

Landing 06 · 2.2 m · south bank

Desembocadura de la Sal

The last landing, and the only one where the river is salt. The water arrives with a tide in it and leaves with one, and for about four hours either side of high water it is possible to swim a hundred metres upstream, which everybody under fourteen has done at least once and nobody over forty admits to.

There is a channel marker at the mouth that has to be moved twice a year because the bar moves further every winter, and a keeper who walks the whole river once a week and knows every post, every bar and every year the river has come up over the road. He is the last person on this stretch who can tell you what the current is doing, and the diagram above is his, more or less.
