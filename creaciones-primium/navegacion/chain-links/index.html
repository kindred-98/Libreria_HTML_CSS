Chain Links

The menu of this page is a length of chain laid out on a steel bench. The six links are real links: each one passes through the next, and nothing about them is a list pretending to be metal. The link for the chapter you are reading lifts off the bench and folds down across its neighbour, pin flashing copper as it takes the load, while the links ahead of it hang loose and the ones behind it stay down. Watch the tension bar and you know how much of the run is behind you.

How the run reads

  • Spent: the chapter is behind you and the pin is dulled to a dark patina.

The tension bar is a single copper strip under the run. It stretches from nothing to the full width of the bench as you move from the first chapter to the last, and it never rewinds while you are still reading.

Link 01

Tackle

Tackle is the whole of a block and tackle, and the name is older than the vocabulary people use for it now. It is a set of pulleys and rope arranged so that the load is shared between several falls of rope, and the whole point of the arrangement is that the person hauling is lifting a fraction of the weight rather than all of it.

Nothing in it is clever. A block and tackle has no motor and no advantage except a longer rope, and the cost of that longer rope is friction at every sheave, which is why the pulleys are lined with bronze and why the blocks are reeved in a particular alternating order. Take the bearings away and the advantage quietly leaks out through the pin holes.

Link 02

Forged Coupling

A chain link starts as a piece of bar, folded in the middle, and the two ends brought together to be welded. A shackle is the same fold with the ends screwed instead of welded, which is why a shackle can be opened and a link cannot. One is a permanent joint and one is a temporary one, and confusing the two on a loaded line is the sort of mistake that gets written up for a long time.

The proportions are not free either. Too much bar in the link and the chain gets heavy and rigid; too little and it wears into an oval, and an oval link does not sit flat on a sprocket. Stud length, bar diameter and inside width have to agree with each other, which is why a chain is specified by those three numbers together and why parts suppliers are so particular about them.

Link 03

Proof Load

Every length of chain carries two numbers: the working load limit, which is the load it is designed to carry in service, and the breaking load, which is what it did in a test. The second is usually several times the first, and the gap between them is the safety factor written down rather than assumed.

The proof test is a one-off event. A sample is pulled slowly until it parts, the figure is recorded against the batch, and the rest of the batch is released on the strength of it. A chain that has been overloaded once but has not parted has still been permanently stretched, and it has quietly moved closer to its breaking load than the certificate suggests.

Link 04

Corrosion

Rust is not one thing but a family, and the members behave differently on a chain. The orange bloom that comes off an anchor in a harbour is loose and flaky and rubs off on your gloves. The dark scale that forms on a link that sits in salt water and then dries is thin, tough and bonded to the metal, and it removes diameter rather than merely staining the surface.

That distinction is the whole argument for galvanising. A zinc coat is sacrificial: it corrodes in place of the steel underneath, and the layer of white powder it leaves is a sign the chain is still protected. Once the zinc is gone the process stops, and from that point the link is eating itself at whatever rate the water happens to be salty.

Link 05

Studding

Studded chain carries a small stud cast into each link so that it grips a chainwheel instead of sliding. It is the difference between a wheel that pulls the chain round and a wheel the chain quietly slips on, and the slip is the failure everyone notices, because it happens without any part breaking.

A stud links a chain in half as quickly as an unstudded one, which is a fair trade for the difference in how it behaves when the load comes on suddenly. The other use is a flywheel: with a sprocket that has a lot of turning mass, the sudden arrival of a taut chain spins it instead of shock loading it, and the wear that causes appears in the pin instead of the teeth.

Link 06

Mooring

An anchor holds because of the shank, not because of the flukes. The shank is a length of steel that has to be able to take the whole load in tension and to drag along the seabed while the current tries to lift it, and the flukes are there to keep the load on the shank while it settles rather than to dig the whole assembly in.

On a rode, the line runs from the anchor through a shackle at the ring, along a length of chain, and up to the hawse. That length of chain is not decoration. It adds weight low down so the line lies along the bottom instead of cutting a catenary through the water, and it takes the first and worst of the load as the vessel surges, with the shackle as the link that holds it all together.
