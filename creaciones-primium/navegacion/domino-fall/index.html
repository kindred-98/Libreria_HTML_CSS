How a run falls

The tiles that are still standing are the chapters you have not reached. Reading further lays one more tile down, and it lays it down after the one before it, with a stagger, because that is the only way a line of dominoes falls.

The tile under the reader is turned over on the table and stays face up until the run passes it. A played tile is not returned to the set until the hand is scored.

The whole set is twenty eight tiles, seven pairs, and a tile is the same size whichever way up it is. The only number on it is the one you already know.

Bone, Wood and Pressed Horn

A domino is a small flat rectangle with a line across the middle and a number of marks on each half, and the numbers are counting marks: one, two, three, six. The set is twenty eight tiles, one for every way of pairing the numbers from nothing to six, and it is the same set in every country that plays dominoes, which is one of the few things that can be said about a game that has been played in every country that plays it.

The material has changed four times. Bone and wood are the oldest and the most expensive, and a bone set is thin, warm, and slightly yellowed by a century of hands. Horn is pressed and black and takes an ink line cleanly. Wood is ordinary and takes it badly. Modern sets are resin or ABS plastic, uniform, cold, and exactly the same weight in every box, which is the reason a plastic set is a fair set and a bone set is not.

The Double Six

The highest tile in a double six set is six six, and it is the tile everybody builds a hand around and nobody wants to be left holding. The ends of the game are decided by doubles, a tile that matches both ends of the table, and the arithmetic of a double six game is dominated by the number of doubles that are still unseen in the bag.

There is a shorter set, the double five, and a longer one, the double nine, and the longer set exists mostly in South America and mostly because the game there is played with a scoring system that rewards runs. A double nine set has fifty five tiles and takes a great deal of table, and a double five set has fifteen fewer and plays faster, and the reason the double six won everywhere else is that it is the shortest set that still gives a hand interesting decisions.

Thickness and Weight

A domino stands up because it is a rectangle, and the thickness of it is the only thing standing between a line of tiles and a line of fallen tiles. A thin tile is easy to push over and easy to stand up again, and a thick tile is neither. The thickness of a good tile is about eight millimetres and the weight is about eight grams, and the relationship between the two is what makes a run either a run or a nuisance.

This is also the whole of the etiquette of the game. A tile is turned over by putting a finger on it and drawing it towards you along the table, never by picking it up, because a picked up tile is a lifted tile and a lifted tile has a shadow, and a shadow on a table in a room with a low light is enough to slow the entire game down. The tiles above fall the way a good player drops them, which is to say they do not fall so much as get allowed to.

Tile 4 · 0 · 5

The Gap

A run fails at the gap. If the spacing between two tiles is more than the length of a tile, the first one falls and misses the second and the whole sequence stops, and this is the single most common way that a row of dominoes on a table ends with four tiles down and two standing and everybody looking at the two.

The spacing that works is about two thirds of the length of a tile, which leaves enough overlap for the falling tile to strike the next one near its top edge, where the leverage is best, and not so much that the tile is stopped dead by the table. Good players set the gap by eye at a distance of about a foot, and a run set by somebody who has never thought about it is either too tight, which fails by not moving at all, or too wide, which fails halfway.

Tile 5 · 6 · 4

Standing Up Again

Taking a line of fallen tiles down and standing them up again is the least interesting job in the game and the one that takes longest, and it is the reason dominoes are played with a partner. One of you plays and the other collects, and the collecting is not a pause between turns, it is half the time, and the rhythm of the two jobs is the actual tempo of a dominoes table.

Standing a tile up is a specific movement that everybody has to relearn after a while: the tile is turned flat, drawn back along the table, tilted onto its long edge, and pushed away from you until it is standing square. Done in that order the tile makes a sound. Done in any other order it slides, and a sliding tile knocks down three of its neighbours, and the person who knocked them down picks them up.

The Last Tile

A domino game ends when the players have blocked, which means there is no legal move left, and it is worth noticing that the tiles on the table are usually not finished. Most games end with a dozen tiles still in the middle of the table with two or three pairs on them, because the game is about not being forced rather than about laying everything down, and a player who lays everything down quickly has usually lost control of the board.

The header above is that rule made visible. Six tiles stand, the ones you have read lie down, and the run only ever goes one way, so the shape of the line tells you how far into the page you are without reading a single number off it. The last tile on this line is never pushed by the one before it. It is the tile a player puts down at the end, out of what is left, because the game is over.
