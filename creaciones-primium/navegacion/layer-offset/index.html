Layer Offset

The header of this page is a two-colour offset press seen from above: a cast bed, two ink drums, a rubber roller, and a chase of six plates locked in the middle of it. Three plates make every sheet here, cyan, magenta and a key plate in black, and the twelve of them never line up perfectly because nobody has ever managed to make them line up perfectly. Bring one sheet forward and its three plates slide apart on the bed so you can read each ink on its own. The other five stay in the chase, further out of register the deeper they sit.

Why nothing lines up

A two-colour press prints the cyan on one impression and the magenta on the next, with the paper held between them by grippers. Between the two impressions the paper stretches, the rollers are not perfectly true, and the room is not perfectly warm. The three plates at the head of this page sit within about a third of a millimetre of each other, which is better than a hand press and worse than a press with a registration camera on it.

Plate I · the impression

The Pull

Offset printing does not print the image. It prints a bump and lets the bump do the work. The plate is a thin aluminium sheet, grained so the ink has something to hold on to, and the image is burned into a photopolymer coating as a surface of microscopic hills and valleys. Paper laid on the hills takes ink; paper laid on the flats takes none. The press is called offset because the image never touches the sheet: it is transferred first to a rubber blanket, and the blanket is what meets the paper.

Which means a pull is not one event but a sequence of them, and the sheet at the head of this page is caught between two of them. Cyan goes on, the grippers carry the sheet round, magenta follows, and the key plate is locked in tight behind the first drum so that the three arrive in a fixed order. Get that order wrong and the key plate prints over wet cyan and the sheet goes grey where it should be black.

Plate II · the marks

Registration

Nobody aligns a sheet by looking at the picture, because by the time the picture is wrong it is too late. The sheet carries four cross marks, one in each corner, printed on the key plate, and the operator judges the run by those and by nothing else. The crosses should sit exactly on top of each other. In practice they sit a hair apart, and the whole craft of two-colour work is deciding how much of that is acceptable to the customer.

A tolerance is agreed in hundredths of a millimetre and written on the order, and the register mark on the bed of the press at the head of this page is where the operator puts the sheet to make that judgement. Bring a plate forward and it lands on the register, which is the one place on the bed where being a third of a millimetre out is actually useful, because from there you can see it.

Plate III · the offset

The Offset

Some work is better when the colours miss each other. Deliberate misregistration, where the magenta sits a fraction low and right of the cyan, was not a failure that got tolerated; it was a decision, made first by accident on a press nobody was watching and then made on purpose for eighty years of poster work. The eye reads the double edge as depth, and the sheets that look wrong in a proof are usually the ones that look right at a distance.

The six plates in the chase above are out of register by increasing amounts, so the deeper a plate sits the more its three inks are spread. That is not a fault in the chase, it is the printing: the six sheets in this section are printed on purpose, each one further out of register than the last, and the plate you are reading has been brought forward so you can see the three inks as three inks instead of as one colour pretending to be two.

Plate IV · the three

The Plates

Two colours on a two-colour press means three plates, because black cannot be mixed on the machine out of a colour nobody has. The third plate is the key plate, and it carries every piece of text, every rule and every registration cross, printed in a solid black that stays black on paper of every colour and under every light. The two colour plates carry only the large shapes, which is why a poster printed this way has enormous flat areas and very fine crisp lines.

Look at the plate in the chase above and you can see the arrangement: the cyan plate, the magenta plate, and the key plate on top, all three the same size and all three carrying the same picture in a different ink. Registered, they make one printed sheet. Separated, as they are when you look at one closely, they are what they always were, which is three metal rectangles and a decision.

Plate V · the depth

The Stack

Printed sheets are counted, not judged. A run of five thousand is delivered wet or dry depending on the stock, and the count is the only thing the customer actually checks. The pile on the skid grows all night, and the top sheet carries the number because the top sheet is the one anybody looks at. Everything below the top sheet is a guess, which is why the top sheet is printed last and put on the top.

So a stack is an argument about order, and the depth of a stack is the only honest measure of it. The chase in the press above holds six plates, and the further down the chase a plate sits, the more out of register it lies and the more of the run it represents. Take one out and the five behind it close up by a hair, which is what happens on a real press every time a plate is changed.

Plate VI · the last pass

The Drier

Offset ink does not dry so much as cure, and the difference matters for a week. On the delivery end of the press the sheet is hot and the film is still soft, so a stack of two hundred sheets put down immediately will block, and the block is permanent in the sense that it cannot be unprinted. The run goes through a dryer, then a cooling stacker, then a waiting period long enough that a thumbprint pressed into the corner of the top sheet comes away clean.

What comes off the end of the machine is therefore not a print but a delay. Every sheet in this section is still slightly soft, and the six plates above are the three impressions that made it plus the three that will make the next one, and the whole arrangement only makes sense because there is always another one behind. The register on the bed is where a sheet is judged, and the six plates never stop being out of register, which is the condition under which anybody learned to read them.

The chase is the navigation: every plate is a link into the notes below, and the plate out on the register is the chapter you are reading.
