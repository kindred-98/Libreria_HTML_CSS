01

Base

Every wing starts as a strut: a hollow tube of chitin running the length of the wing, thicker at the base than at the tip, with the haemolymph pushed through it under pressure. It is a cantilever and a fluid pipe at the same time, and almost everything else on the wing is bracing for it.

The joint at the base does the rest. A fly can sweep a wing through a stroke of a hundred and eighty degrees in three milliseconds, which means it must be folded out of the way on the return, and the third segment does that fold every time without being asked twice.

02

Strut

From the main strut the longitudinal veins run forward to the leading edge, where the wing is thinnest and has to be, because that is the edge that meets the air first. Cross veins close the cells into a lattice, and the pattern of that lattice is what the whole wing is named after.

Two families of insect dominate almost every aerial niche, and the division is structural. One carries its veins out to the margin in a network of branching cells; the other folds them into long parallel corridors, which is a lighter, stiffer wing and a very different solution to the same problem.

03

Cross veins

The membrane itself is double sided and almost featureless, two layers of cuticle with a thin space between them. The veins are not tubes inside it but folds in it, standing up on both faces at once, so the whole sheet is corrugated rather than perforated.

Corrugation is what stops a thin membrane from behaving like a flag. A flat sheet that size would flutter badly at every stroke; a ribbed one resists bending across the ribs and gives way along them, which is precisely the anisotropy a wing wants.

04

Panel

Where a vein crosses another, a small thickened plate is usually stamped into the membrane, a nodus or a crossvein node, and it is not structural. It marks where a trachea runs, carrying air and nerves to the sensory organs sitting in the cells near it.

Those organs are the reason a wing is not just a wing. A patch of campaniform sensilla along the leading edge reports strain back to the thorax, and the insect corrects its stroke on the next one, which is a control loop running in the time of a single beat.

05

Flex

A wing must be flexible along the span and stiff across it, and the vein pattern is the mechanism for both. Longitudinally the struts allow the sheet to curve into a cambered airfoil; transversely the cross veins stop the curve from flattening under load.

Passive camber is why insects do not need muscles in the wing at all. The thoracic oscillator sets the frequency, the vein geometry sets the shape, and the resulting aerodynamics in a small insect are Reynolds-number-dominated anyway, where the flow does not much care how clever the wing is.

06

Scale

The far third of many wings is a field of overlapping scales, a shingle of flattened hairs each with its own ribbed nerve and its own pigment. They weigh little, they add almost no stiffness, and they change the colour and the way the surface sheds water.

They also determine how the wing is seen rather than how it works, which is worth keeping separate. Pick the last vein on the wings above and it draws as carefully as the first: a destination is a destination, whether it sits on a strut that carries load or on a pattern that only carries a look.

Pick a vein and it is drawn from base to tip. Read downward and the drawn vein follows you.
