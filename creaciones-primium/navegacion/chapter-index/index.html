Chapter one · folio 7

The Quay at Low Water

The harbour emptied twice a day and filled again twice a day, and in between there was a stretch of black mud on which the whole town walked about like people indoors. Ammunition had been unloaded there since the fourteenth century. Nobody now remembered which war. The stones were still in place, worn into the mud by four hundred years of cart wheels, and the cart wheels had worn the stones into bowls.

A boy called Renner kept the harbour book for one winter, because his uncle had kept it for thirty and his uncle had finally stopped. His wage was a penny for every boat recorded and threepence if the cargo was named. He wrote in a small upright hand, and by February he had filled sixty pages and learned to keep two things out of the book: the weather, and everything anybody had said to him about the weather.

Chapter two · folio 23

The Ferry That Would Not Sail

The ferry to Corrow Point ran in summer for sixty-one years and stopped in 1949 for reasons that took the harbour commission three years to write down. The hull was sound. The engine was run every Monday and started first time. The master had thirty years on that crossing and could have reached the far side blindfold, which is exactly what the commission reported, in the tone of a man who did not believe it.

What had expired in March was a licence. Renewing it needed a survey, a fee, and a signature from a county office forty miles inland, and the office had moved buildings. Four summers passed before a young clerk with no particular interest in boats walked the forty miles on a Tuesday and signed the form. The ferry sailed again on a Friday, three days before the season closed.

Chapter three · folio 41

A Ledger in Two Hands

Silas Marrow wrote the harbour book for sixty-one years. He wrote it in a narrow, forward-leaning hand that got narrower, because he got older, and that was how everyone knew the date of an entry without asking: the letters stood up straighter in 1890 than they did in 1946. When he died in the September, the commission hired a man from the county who wrote round, even, and slightly too large.

The change falls in the middle of a page. The last fourteen entries in the old hand describe eleven herrings, a broken bollard, and a quantity of coal; the first entry in the new hand is the word Received. The town argued for a decade about whether to carry on. Half said a harbour book is a record and a record does not have an author. The other half said a harbour book is a trust and you do not hand a trust to a stranger. The book is still on the shelf, open at the seam.

Chapter four · folio 58

The Winter of the Long Gale

The gale came in from the west-north-west on the ninth of January and stayed nineteen days. On the third day the harbour mouth was closed to traffic by law. On the seventh, a warehouse roof went. On the eleventh, the market cross came off its plinth in a single blow and lay in the road for a month, and people walked around it the way you walk around a sleeping dog.

Three ships were overdue when the wind dropped. Two came back inside a week with their crews exhausted and their holds empty. The third was the Corriewick, out of Waterford, and nothing of her was ever found. The harbour book for that January has eleven consecutive days of the same sentence written out in full: No vessel entered. Marrow did not abbreviate it once.

Chapter five · folio 76

What the Harbour Kept

Above the pilot house there is a dry cellar with a stone floor and a door that swells shut, and inside it, at some point before any living memory, somebody began storing things. There are four hundred and eleven crates. They are numbered. The list is in the back of this book and it is astonishing reading: forty barrels of lamp oil, eleven clocks, a coffin with no name on it, ninety yards of good rope, a harpsichord.

Almost nothing on the list is still down there. The rope was sold off in 1911. The clocks went to a dealer in 1934. The lamp oil burned in a stove during the winter of the long gale, which is the only recorded instance of an emergency measure that worked. The cellar is still dry, the door still swells, and the town has never voted to empty it.

Chapter six · folio 94

The Salt Ledger, Closed

The harbour book was sold at the counting house in 1961 for the price of a good suit. The harbour had gone quiet in any case, the commission having voted in 1958 that there was nothing left to commission, and the building that housed the records was scheduled for a car park. The buyer was a collector of maritime paper who wanted the Marrow hand specifically and said so, out loud, in a room full of people.

He got it. Nine years later a conservator at the county archive opened the binding to check for a title page and found nine folded sheets behind the endpaper, in a third hand, older than Marrow's. They are lists of salt: what came in, what was sold, and what was owed to whom. The last line has been cut off by the fold. This book stops here because that is where the printed edition stops; the sheets themselves are still in a drawer in the county, and nobody has yet been able to read the last line without unfolding it by hand, which nobody wants to do.
