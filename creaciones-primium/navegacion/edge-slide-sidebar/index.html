01 Contents

Paper and shadow

Issue fourteen of Corte arrives in a week when three studios we admire gave up their offices and moved into laundries, dyehouses and one former telephone exchange.

We sent a photographer to each of them and asked the same question: what does your building still do that you cannot? The answers were about water, about noise, and about the particular grey that only survives on a wall that was never meant to be painted.

02 Feature

The fat face returns

Between 1974 and 1990, publishers used ultra-heavy serifs the way film posters used smoke: constantly, and with total confidence. Then the screens arrived, the hinting failed, and every display face quietly slimmed down until it disappeared.

A headline should be legible from the bus stop and slightly uncomfortable from the pavement. — Julien Roche, Type Foundry Basel

What changed is not taste but rendering. Variable fonts now keep their ink traps at twelve pixels, and a face drawn for a 48-point poster survives compression to a phone at arm's length. Our own cover was set at 340 points and still holds its hairlines on the digital edition.

The lesson for designers is old and slightly annoying: draw it at the size it will be read, not at the size it looks best in the specimen book.

03 Interviews

Nine questions for Marta Rego

Marta Rego has spent nineteen years drawing letters for packaging, museums and one very stubborn municipal transport authority. She agreed to talk in her studio, provided we let her keep sketching through the whole conversation.

Why pencil first?
Because a pencil argument can be erased without anybody losing face. Ink makes people defensive.
Worst brief you have taken?
A logo that had to work on a balloon, a bus and a favicon, with a budget for one of them.
How do you know a drawing is finished?
When I stop finding new things to dislike in it. That usually takes three weeks longer than the deadline.
Advice for a first-year student?
Set your own text at twenty points, print it, tape it to a door and look at it from across the corridor.

04 Workshop

A day on the press

The Heidelberg in the basement of our printer in Alcântara was built in 1961 and still starts with a shove. We spent a full shift there while the issue's eight spot-colour pages were made ready, which mostly consists of waiting, wiping and arguing about a quarter of a gram of ink.

  1. 06:40

    Cold start

    The flywheel is turned by hand until the oil reaches the top bearing. Nobody talks during this part.

  2. 09:15

    Make-ready

    Sheets are taped, pulled and read under a loupe until the solid orange stops mottling at the edges.

  3. 13:30

    Run

    Four thousand impressions at three thousand an hour, checked every two hundred for register drift.

  4. 18:05

    Wash up

    Blankets cleaned, forme locked back into the galley, and the day's misprints filed for the archive.

05 Colophon

How this issue was made

Everything you are reading was typeset on a system font stack, printed on uncoated stock and proofed twice: once on paper, once on a phone held at arm's length in daylight. The corrections printed on page 146 are the same three we argued about in production.
