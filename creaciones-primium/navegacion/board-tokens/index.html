How this board is played

The red token stands on the square you are reading. It hops there one square at a time and it never crosses a square diagonally.

A square scuffed pale has already been passed. The felt keeps a ring of wear where the dice land and nobody has ever managed to hide it.

Lacquered squares are numbered by hand in the corner. A house rule from 1934 put a crown on every fourth square, and the crown is still there.

The rail above the felt is brass, and the counter on it is the only piece of the board that was never painted.

Square I · the corner · before anything else

Laying Out the Board

The box is nine inches square and two and a half deep, and the lid is held on by a strip of linen tape that has been replaced three times and is now the only part of the box worth anything. Inside, folded into eight leaves, is the board itself. It was made in 1934 by a firm in Leeds that no longer exists, printed on three sheets of card and joined at the folds with strips of gummed paper, and it has survived four house moves, one flooded cellar and a dog.

Laying it out is the part everybody gets wrong. The leaves are not interchangeable. The two that hold the home squares go down first and end up at the top left, the leaves with the six printed on them go next, and the last leaf is the one with the instructions on the back, which almost nobody ever reads because it is upside down when the board is the right way up. Turn it over and the whole thing still works, but nobody does it twice.

Square II · a road that doubles back on itself

The Long Road

The route is not straight and was never meant to be. It runs right, drops a row, comes back left, drops again, and reaches the far side having covered less ground than a crow would. Every printed game of this family uses the same trick for the same reason: a straight road on a square board is a road that is finished before anybody has finished their tea.

Because the path doubles back, the squares cannot be read left to right and top to bottom. Players spend the first two turns of any game walking a finger along the route counting out loud, and the reason experienced players sound bored is that they are not counting squares. They are counting how many of the tokens are already past the bend.

Square III · printed by hand, twice

Counting the Squares

Each square carries a number, and each number was printed twice: once in the corner where it is read at speed, and once in a second position on the same square for when the board is turned round. The corner numbers were added by hand in 1934 by somebody who ran out of ink on the twenty-one and made the twenty-two from a different pen. You can see it if you know where to look and it has been pointed out to every new player for eighty years.

Counting matters because a turn is a number of squares, not a distance. Two and a three is five squares, and five squares is a fifth of the way round. When the board was new the route was sixty squares and a full lap was a serious undertaking, long enough that a game outlasted the light. Later printings cut it to thirty-six, and nobody has ever agreed whether that was an improvement.

Square IV · the hop, the turn, the run

The Token Turns

You cast two dice, add them, and walk the token that many squares. If the sum puts you exactly on a crown square you take another throw, which sounds generous and is not, because the fourth crown from home gives the turn back to your opponent. The house rule sheet pinned inside the lid for four decades called this the honest crown, and the phrase outlived the sheet.

The token in this header obeys all of it. It advances one square at a time rather than jumping the whole distance, which is slower than the real game and much easier to follow, and it never goes backwards, so if you scroll back up the board the token walks back and the squares behind it keep the scuff they have already earned. That scuff is the closest thing the board has to a record of the game.

Square V · materials, and what eighty years does to them

Felt and Lacquer

The squares are wood, faced in printed paper and sealed with a thin lacquer that was fashionable in 1934 and is now a museum of bad decisions. It yellows, it cracks along the grain, and it traps every ring a glass ever leaves. The felt underneath is a coarse green baize that has been lifted and relifted so many times that the nap lies flat in a path from the dice cup to the centre square.

Lacquering a square again is a fifteen minute job and everybody who has done it has made it worse. You cannot get the old lacquer off without taking the paper with it, so the repair goes on as a patch, and after four or five patches the corner number is unreadable and somebody sands it back and finds a second number underneath, printed slightly crooked, from an attempt nobody remembers.

Square VI · home, and the walk back

The Last Square

The last square is not a finish. It is the turn of the road back towards the corner, and a token that arrives there has to make a full circuit of the squares behind it before it is scored. On a thirty-six square board that is most of a game, and it is the point of the whole design: the last thing the game asks of you is patience, and it asks for it in the least dramatic room in the house, with the light off.

Then the tokens go back to the home squares, which is where this header starts. The board is folded into eight, the leaves are pressed flat under something heavy, and the box is closed with the linen tape. What is left of the evening is a felt path on the baize, a ring on the lacquer where somebody put their glass down, and a counter on the brass rail still reading the turn you stopped on.
