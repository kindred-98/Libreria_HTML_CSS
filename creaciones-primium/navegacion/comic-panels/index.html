About this issue

Six panels, one page, no splash. The printer told us there was no room for a seventh and we believed him for eleven years.

The panel you are on swells and takes the yellow burst. On paper that means the light in the drawing has moved to you.

Colours used in the whole run: two blacks, newsprint cream, one red, one yellow. The red has never once been used for the sun.

Panel one · page one, top left

Low Water

The harbour empties twice a day and fills again twice a day, and in between there is a floor of black mud with four hundred years of cart wheels worn into it. Ammunition came off ships here from the fourteenth century on. Nobody now remembers which war. The stones are still where they were put.

In the drawings the mud is always the same value as the sky, which is a lie the strip has told since panel one and will not apologise for. It keeps the eye on the waterline, where the actual story is.

Panel two · one penny a boat

The Boy With The Book

Renner kept the harbour book for one winter because his uncle had kept it for thirty and his uncle had finally stopped. The wage was a penny for every boat recorded and threepence if the cargo could be named, which it usually could, from the smell, on most days in February.

He also kept two things out of the book without being told to: the weather, and everything anybody said to him about the weather. The artist drew him small in every panel for the rest of the run, which readers took for modesty and which was actually just the truth about the job.

Panel three · three pages of nothing happening

First Time, Again

The engine starts on the first pull. It has started on the first pull every Monday for sixty-one years, and the sound of it is so ordinary inside that cabin that nobody in the frame looks up. Almond keeps his coat on. He has been keeping his coat on since the third of March.

Three pages of dialogue go by and none of it is about a boat. Two men arranging a delivery of coal in the tone of men who both know the arrangement cannot proceed. The reader is meant to get tired before the writers do.

Panel four · dawn, one long panel

Forty Miles For A Signature

The clerk is twenty-six, he surveys boilers, and he would rather have been surveying something with a chimney in it. The form asks three questions. Two are yes or no. The third asks whether the vessel is seaworthy, and she is, demonstrably, because he has looked at her.

He learns on the way back what he actually did. The previous page carries four signatures in four different hands and, beside them, one word repeated: renewed. On that page renewed does not mean the boat was made safe. It means somebody walked.

Panel five · the widest panel in the run

Four Hundred And Eleven

Eleven steps down and a door that has to be shouldered, and then a stone floor with crates on it in rows nobody can now account for. Forty barrels of lamp oil. Eleven clocks, stopped, none at the same hour. Ninety yards of rope that was good in 1890 and was sold off in 1911.

The only object in the cellar that anybody bought rather than stored is an upright harpsichorn with the lid closed. The list says nothing about where it came from. The cellar says nothing. The instrument says a maker's name and nothing else at all.

Panel six · last panel, bottom right

The Price Of A Good Suit

The auction takes eleven minutes because there is one lot and one bidder. A collector of maritime paper says, out loud, in a room full of people, that he wants the Marrow hand specifically. The artist has drawn the room without anybody in it, because the room is not the point.

Nine years later a conservator lifts the endpaper and finds nine folded sheets in a third hand. The last line is lost in the fold. The last panel of the strip is the fold, and it has never once been unfolded.
