The Velvet Draw

The header of this page is a working piece of stage drapery. Six lengths of velvet hang from a single brass rod over a dark house. Whichever act you are reading is the one that gets drawn aside and tied off with a gold cord, the other five gather back into the shadow, and the tally at the end of the rod counts where you are standing.

Act I

The Bolt

Velvet arrives at the workroom on a roll sixty metres long and two hundred centimetres wide, and nothing about it is negotiable. The cost of the cloth is calculated by weight, the weight by pile length, and the pile length is what a costume assistant learns to read first: a short pile crushes flat under a hand and never recovers its depth, while a long pile holds a shadow inside every fold and has to be cut with the nap running the right way.

Cutting a curtain from the bolt is a slow, boring job done at the end of the day. The cloth is laid out face down on the floor for a metre at a time, marked with chalk, and rolled forward as the knife follows the line. Forty minutes of that work stands behind every length you will see hanging on the rod above.

Act II

Hanging the Piece

The folds you see are not sewn. They are set by looping the heading of the cloth over a webbing tape and tacking it to a batten, and every fold is made by hand so the fullness stays even from the first pleat to the last. A two-metre drop with a quarter fullness gives the deep vertical troughs that make velvet read as velvet rather than as a flat painted panel.

The rod above the folds is not decorative. It carries the weight of the whole piece, and it has to sit dead level, because a batten out of true makes the leading edge hang in a curve that no amount of restitching will fix. Levellers are not a refinement here. They are the first hour of the job.

Act III

Drawing the Curtain

To open is to draw, and the draw is a counterweighted line of rope running over pulleys at grid height. One person takes the rope, leans back, and the whole weight of the house comes down through her arms. Release halfway and the curtain stops wherever you let it, which is why a draw can hold at any height at all: nothing about it is spring-loaded.

The heading of this page works the same way. The act you are reading is pulled out of the fall and left standing proud of its neighbours, and the folds that are not being read close up against each other in the dark, losing their depth one at a time. The cord at the end of the rod gathers the whole fall if you want to see it shut.

Act IV

Pile and Nap

Velvet is a fabric with a direction. Brushed one way it is deep and black; brushed the other it goes pale and grey, and the change is not a fading but a reflection. Crews learn this the hard way during a fit, because a hand laid flat on the cloth for a costume leaves a mark that takes a day to disappear.

Restoring the pile is steam, a soft brush, and a great deal of patience. You work from the hem upward with the cloth lying flat, and you never push, only lift. A velvet that has been brushed badly once will look tired for a season afterwards, and no amount of lighting will persuade the audience otherwise.

Act V

The Wings

Behind the main fall there are the legs, the borders and the tabs, and none of them are meant to be noticed. Each is a narrower piece of the same cloth, hung to mask the wings, the counterweights and the raw edges of the stage. Together they turn a room with a dozen visible problems into a frame with none.

They also do the lighting work. A border hangs low enough to cut the spill from the front-of-house lanterns, so the beam only reaches the actors and the top of the drop stays in the dark. Masking is not about hiding; it is about deciding exactly how much of the room the audience is allowed to see.

Act VI

Taking It Down

Every piece comes off the batten eventually, and the taking down is gentler than the hanging. The webbing is unpicked rather than cut, the cloth is rolled pile to pile, and the roll is wrapped in muslin before it goes anywhere near a van. A curtain folded the wrong way arrives at the next venue with a permanent crease through the pile.

That crease is the whole life of a piece of drapery in one line. Everything else, the hours of levelling and the years of brushing, exists so that the one thing an audience sees, which is a rectangle of colour standing in a dark room, holds its shape when the lights come up.
