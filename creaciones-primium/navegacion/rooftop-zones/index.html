Zone 01 · the slab

The slab

A flat roof is a floor that happens to be outdoors, and almost everything difficult about it follows from that. It has to carry the weight of a tank of water, a lift motor, a bank of plant and occasionally two hundred kilos of caretaker, while being unable to dry out from either side. The structural depth needed for that is why so many nineteenth-century buildings have a cornice you could park a lorry on and nobody ever has.

The surface is screed, not concrete. It is laid in bays between movement joints so that it can crack where somebody has already decided it will, and it is finished with a fall towards the outlets rather than level, because a flat roof is not a flat roof. What you see from a neighbouring window as a grey plane is in fact a very shallow gable, and the whole six zones above are laid out along it in the order the water would find them if you let it.

Zone 02 · the fall

The fall

The fall is the least visible and most important thing about a flat roof. Traditionally it is one in eighty, which is about twelve millimetres over every metre, and it sounds trivial until you walk it. It is enough that water never sits, and that is the entire specification, because standing water is what finds the seam that was not quite sealed and turns it into a pond with a depth of forty millimetres and a life of a week.

The trade is that a fall needs somewhere to go, and the somewhere is an outlet, and the outlet needs a route through the building, and the route needs a hole in a floor that somebody else designed. Most flat roofs are still engineered as a set of compromises with a parapet that is not quite high enough and a gutter that drains slower than the rain that fills it. The bay nearest the outlet is the one everybody looks at after a storm, which is why the zones on this roof are numbered in the direction the water actually travels.

Zone 03 · the membrane

The membrane

There are three generations of roof and the differences are mostly about how they fail. Bitumen felt is rolled out in sheets that are torched to the next one, and it is cheap, tough and completely opaque about what it is doing; it will tell you it is fine until the day it lets go. Liquid-applied membranes are a coating brushed or sprayed over the screed, seamless, and they fail by blistering when vapour comes up from below and finds a spot it can push.

Single-ply sheets are the current answer and have their own characteristic failure, which is wind. A sheet lifted at an edge in a storm becomes a sail, and once it is going nothing on the roof will stop it. Every design decision since the nineteen eighties has been about edges: how the sheet is fixed at the perimeter, how the upstand is dressed, and how many extra fastenings the wind zone calculation says the corner needs. The chalk outline above is not a survey mark. It is a drawing, in the cheapest available medium, of the thing a roofer is actually protecting.

Zone 04 · the drain

The drain

A roof outlet is a small metal box with a dome of wire mesh over it, and it is the point at which a flat roof becomes somebody else's problem. The mesh is there for leaves and it works, and it works right up until a single wet leaf lies flat across it and makes a seal, at which point the entire roof becomes a cistern held in place by a roof that was designed to be dry.

Two outlets are the minimum on anything large, because one outlet is a single point of failure with a design life of about a year. Beyond that it is a maintenance regime, and the regime is almost always the thing that fails: a system that needs clearing twice a year and a contractor twice a year needs somebody to be able to see it. Buildings with no access have drains that block, and buildings with access have drains that are cleared, and almost nothing else about the two roofs is reliably different.

Zone 05 · the tank

The tank

Water storage on a roof is a nineteenth-century habit that outlived its reasons, and the tank is still the heaviest single object on most of these buildings. A tank of a few thousand litres on a timber-framed roof is a structural problem that predates any of the waterproofing underneath it, and several buildings have been surveyed, found wanting and rebuilt for no reason other than the tank.

The pipework tells the story. Gravity cannot work uphill, so a roof tank feeds the storeys below it and is refilled by a pump that has to run whenever the level drops, which means a float switch, a stopcock in a locked box, and an overflow that discharges onto somebody else's property about four times a year. The tank at the far end of this roof is drawn with the water at the level it has been left at, which is neither full nor empty, because a tank that is genuinely full is a tank nobody has looked at in a year.

Zone 06 · the penthouse

The penthouse

A roof used to be one thing and is now six. A walkway, a plant area, a zone for the photovoltaics, a place where the extract fans go, a green patch nobody waters, and a small brick structure with a door that is the stair head. None of this is accidental: the fire regulations moved the stairs up, the energy rules moved the panels up, and the building services followed because there was nowhere else to put them.

It has been good for the roofs and terrible for the drainage. Every zone boundary is a change of level, and every change of level is a place where the fall has to be re-established by hand. The zones marked out on the slab above are not decoration and they are not a survey; they are the working subdivision of somebody's roof, drawn in chalk because chalk can be washed off and redrawn, and redrawn every spring by a caretaker with a stick of the stuff.
