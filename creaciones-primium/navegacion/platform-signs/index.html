Sign 01 · the platform

The platform

A platform is a raised strip of concrete with one edge that people must not stand behind, and almost everything else about a station is arranged around keeping that edge honest. The surface is laid in slabs so that it can be lifted and relaid without closing the place, the coping stones along the edge are set half a millimetre proud so that a train passing at seventy miles an hour makes a small noise, and that noise is the only warning most passengers ever get.

The platform is also the only room in the whole building that most passengers ever see, and it is where the station's entire attitude is set. Concrete, fluorescent light, a bench, a bin and a poster that has been up since before the last refit. The surfaces are chosen to be scrubbed several times a day, which is why nothing on a platform is soft, and why the signage has to be legible from thirty feet away through rain.

Sign 02 · the beacons

The beacons

Along the edge of a platform there used to be a line of lamps at intervals, and their job was not to light anything. They were reference points. A train driver approaching at night reads the position of a known lamp against the signal, and the rate at which the lamps pass tells them how fast they are going and where they have stopped, which is the entire navigation problem on an unlit railway.

The lamps are gone and the principle is not. Every signal, every speed board and every marker on the trackside is a beacon in the older sense: a thing in a known place that you measure everything else against. Station designers rediscovered the idea for passengers, and the result is the row of signs along the platform, each one hanging a little further forward than the last so that a person walking the length of the platform is being pulled towards the exit.

Sign 03 · the lettering

The lettering

Railway lettering was set in a single grotesque sans serif, and it is set in that because a sign has to be read in a fraction of a second by somebody who is walking, whose eyes are adjusting to a different light from the sign, and who may be carrying a bag in one hand. There is no lower case, no italics, and no letterform that depends on a serif to be legible at distance.

White on blue is not an arbitrary choice either. It is one of the two colour pairs with a contrast ratio high enough to be read through dirty glass, and it is the one that survives being back-lit, which is why the same blue appears on platform numbers, on departure boards, and on the small enamel plate screwed to the outside of a signal box. The six panels above are that pair, and the one you are reading has been brought forward into the light.

Sign 04 · the warning

The warning

Most platforms carry a painted line and, in newer stations, studs. The line is the older of the two and it is astonishingly effective, mostly because it is a continuous edge rather than a hazard marking: you cannot stand on a line without knowing that you are standing on a line. The studs are there for the people who cannot see the paint, and they are usually set back from the edge by a much greater distance than seems necessary until you understand that the object is a warning rather than a barrier.

The warnings that do not work are the ones nobody can act on. A sign that says mind the gap is read once and then ignored forever, because the gap does not change and the passenger has no way to improve at it. A sign that lights up, that moves, that is in the place where your eye already is, keeps being read. The four signs behind the one in front are not less true; they are just less urgent.

Sign 05 · the way out

The way out

Wayfinding on a station is a hierarchy rather than a set of signs. The first decision is which direction, the second is which line, the third is which platform, and a good system spends its size budget accordingly: the biggest type is on the thing you decide first and the smallest is on the thing you decide last. Most systems get this exactly backwards, which is why station signage is legible and unhelpful at the same time.

Colour is the fastest carrier of hierarchy and the fastest to be misread. The convention that yellow means exit and blue means platform is close to universal and is maintained for exactly one reason, which is that it is already in the heads of everybody in the station. A new station with a beautiful new colour code has to spend years and a great deal of money teaching it, and the teaching does not fully succeed for as long as the old station is still in use.

Sign 06 · the announcement

The announcement

The recorded voice in a station is a strange artefact. It is a voice with no body, arriving from above, using a vocabulary that is about one third longer than the sentence would need, and it is the only part of the building that addresses a person by name. Passengers tolerate it completely and rely on it absolutely, and the two facts sit uncomfortably together.

What the announcement is good at is the thing no sign can do: it tells you what has changed. The boards say what the plan was, the sign above the platform says where you are, and the voice says that the train is being held at the signal because of a fault on the line ahead. Six signs on a beam can tell you the whole shape of the station. Only one of them can tell you why you are still standing there.
