Reading the stones

A plain stack marks the route. Keep it, and add one stone from the ground when you go up.

A split at the base means the path leaves this point and rejoins the valley floor.

A tall five-stone cairn means water: there is a spring within a hundred paces.

Ochre means this is the stage you are reading, and the marked track below it is the ground already covered.

Stage 01 · 1 842 m

Coll de la Nieve

The track starts where the grass gives up, at a saddle of grey shale that stays white on the north side until the last week of June. There is a cairn here of eleven stones, the oldest on the whole path, and the first thing any guide does is count them out loud so that the party knows it has really arrived.

From the col the ridge drops south in a long false crest that looks walkable and is not. The real path turns left almost at once, behind a leaning marker post with a red top, and follows the shoulder down through a stand of dwarf pine. The wind at the col is honest about itself: if you can hear yourself thinking, you are in the right place.

Stage 02 · 1 610 m

Paso del Cierzo

The pass takes its name from the north wind, which arrives here in a straight line with nothing to slow it down. There is a stone shelter on the southern lip, three walls and a roof of slate slabs, big enough for six people and built in a hurry by somebody who was caught out exactly once.

Two hundred and thirty-two metres of descent in a kilometre, all of it on loose shale that moves underfoot. Walkers put their hands on the wall for the last hundred paces, which is a good idea, and then step off it at the first bend because the ground finally holds. Below the shelter the pine belt begins and the wind stops following you.

Stage 03 · 1 240 m

Los Almendrales

Somebody terraced this whole slope for almonds a long time before the path existed, and the terraces still hold: low dry-stone walls following the contour, a metre high, with a gap every twenty paces where the old water went through. Crossing them all costs an hour of stepping up and down for four hundred metres of actual walking.

Half the trees are dead from a blight that came through in the eighties and never fully left. The survivors are loaded in spring, and the ground between them is a litter of pale husks that makes the whole slope smell like a bakery for three weeks. It is the gentlest hour of the descent and the one everybody stops for.

Stage 04 · 940 m

Vado del Lobo

The stream here runs clean, cold and fast enough to be loud from twenty metres, and it crosses the path in a single clean drop of about a metre. After rain it is a different animal entirely: brown, loud, and carrying alder branches the size of a bench. The first three crossings of the season are marked with a red slash on the boulder above.

The name is older than the ford. A shepherd lost a dog here in a year nobody records, and the cairn on the near bank carries an extra flat stone on top that does not match the others. Most people add their own stone anyway, because that is what the marker is for, and the pile is now bigger than the one at the col.

Stage 05 · 620 m

Cortijo Quemado

A fire here in 1961 took the roof, the olive press and about four hectares of scrub, and nobody rebuilt. What stands is three walls of thick stone, a stone lintel, and a doorway that a grown adult can walk through without ducking. The threshing floor in front of it is swept; somebody is still using it, though the shepherd who sweeps it has never once come up the path.

This is the last place with shade and the last with a flat surface to sit on. The spring runs behind the ruin in a pipe of galvanised iron left by the insurance assessor, and the water comes out cold enough to ache. From the door you can see the whole remaining descent laid out in front of you, and it looks like fifteen minutes.

Stage 06 · 480 m

Laguna Honda

The pool sits in a closed basin with no visible outlet, which is why the water is so dark and so still and why it is a metre and a half deeper than the maps claim. It holds ice until May. Walkers come down to it, stand on the south shore for a while, and then go back up, which is a strange thing to do at the bottom of a mountain.

There is a final cairn on the shore, five stones, the tall one, for the water. The track ends there. The path back is the path you came, and the guide who walked you down will tell you so, twice, because nobody in the valley has ever climbed this on purpose.
