The archive is navigated two ways. The bar above moves you between the six sections of this page, and the drawer on the right remembers where you have been, with a back and a forward that behave the way your browser history does.

Section one · the front page

A newspaper that still sets its own type

Every issue in this archive was set in metal, printed on a flatbed press and read the same morning it came off the machine. The last hand-set front page went to press in 1974, and the newspaper has kept the original plate of it in a drawer on the second floor ever since.

The archive holds every page, from the first thin weekly of 1888 to yesterday, photographed at a resolution that survives being enlarged to a full wall. Reading it feels like reading a newspaper, which is the point: nothing about the interface pretends to be something else.

Section two · the chronicle

Floods, fires and one very long strike

The chronicle is the section the readers write to. It carries the deaths, the marriages, the lost cats and the flood of 1931 that took the low bridge and most of the wool store with it, and it has been scanned in full because nobody can be sure which paragraph a family will want one day.

Indexing the chronicle took eleven years and four people. Names are the hard part: the same woman appears as Mary, Mary Ann, and Mrs Robert Symes depending on the decade, and the index keeps all three, with a note explaining why.

Section three · markets and money

What the harbour was worth

The markets pages run unbroken from 1888, and the archive lets you plot a single commodity across a century and a half: coal first, then cotton, then shipbuilding, then, for one glorious decade, nothing at all while the yards stood empty.

The figures were transcribed by hand twice and typed by hand once, which is why the column widths drift across the page. A machine-readable version of every table exists and is corrected where the two disagree, with the original left visible.

Section four · arts and notices

Two pages that never sold a paper

The arts pages carried the theatre listings, the exhibitions, the obituaries of people nobody in the town had heard of and the occasional furious letter about the bridge toll. They are also the pages most often asked for at the counter, usually by descendants looking for a name in a photograph caption.

Photographs in the archive are described rather than interpreted. A caption says what is visible and where it was printed, and nothing more, because an archive that guesses is an archive that has to be corrected later.

Section five · the vault

Glass plates, microfilm and a fire in 1974

The vault holds the negatives, the microfilm runs and the plate library. A fire in 1974 took the office and the plate library with it, and the surviving material was rebuilt from microfilm that had been sent out for cleaning three weeks earlier, which is the single luckiest fact in the building's history.

Everything in the vault is now cold, dark and rotated twice a year. The digitisation is finished for the first ninety years and under way for the rest, and the queue is published so that anyone can see which decade will be next.

Section six · subscription

Reading the whole hundred and thirty eight years

A subscription covers the archive, the bound volumes and the newspaper itself, and it exists so that the digitisation can keep going. The archive is not a business that can pay for a scanning crew on page views alone, and the trustees decided long ago that the record is the point.

Readers in the reading room keep their own list of the pages they are working on. The drawer on this page is exactly that idea, in miniature: a short stack, clearly marked, and two buttons to walk back and forward through it without losing your place.
