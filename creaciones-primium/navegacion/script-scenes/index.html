Draft pages are the grey ones. When a scene goes to fair copy the camera department stops getting the grey version and starts getting the clean one.

The red circle is a grease pencil mark, the way a first assistant flags the scene a unit is standing by.

Page counts are from the second draft and have not been reconciled with the shooting order.

Scene 1 · Int. harbour master's office · 4 pages

Int. Harbour Master's Office — Day

The room is half window and half filing, and the window is doing the better work. Marlow stands with the harbour book open on the sill, reading the winter tally out loud in the voice of a man who has read it before and knows where it lies. Thirty-one vessels. Eleven hundred and four tons of coal. Two lost to the gale and one, the Corriewick, not returned at all.

A girl in a wet coat comes in out of the rain to ask after a boat, and Marlow does not have her boat in the book because her boat is a rowing boat and rowing boats are not in the book. He tells her so kindly. She leaves. He turns the page, sees that he has just read the number wrong by nine hundred, and starts again from the top without comment.

Scene 2 · Ext. salt quay · 3 pages

Ext. Salt Quay — Dusk

Low water, and the harbour is a floor. The stones come out of the mud in two long lines, worn into shallow bowls by four hundred years of cart wheels, and a boy of fifteen walks between the lines writing down what is above them. He is paid a penny for every boat and threepence if he can name the cargo, which he can, from the smell, most days.

He stops at the third hull because the third hull has a rope across its hatch and ropes across hatches mean customs, and customs men are indoors in winter. He looks at the rope for a while, decides it is not his business, writes the hull number anyway with a mark beside it that nobody has taught him. The mark is a small circle. It means look again later.

Scene 3 · Int. ferry cabin · 6 pages

Int. Ferry Cabin — Night

The engine starts first time, which it has done every Monday for sixty-one years, and the sound of it starting is so ordinary in this cabin that nobody looks up. The master, Almond, sits with his hands on the wheel and his coat still on. He has been sitting like that since the third of March, when the licence expired and the harbour stopped being a place ships went.

Dialogue is sparse and mostly administrative, which is the point of the scene: two men going over the paperwork of a ferry that could sail and might not, in the tone of men arranging a delivery of coal. Almond says the hull is sound. The county man says he has no authority over the hull. Almond says the hull was never the question. Neither of them says what the question is.

Scene 4 · Ext. Corrow Point landing · 5 pages

Ext. Corrow Point Landing — Dawn

The clerk walks in at first light carrying a folder he has walked forty miles for and is mildly annoyed about. He is twenty-six, he surveys boilers, and he would rather have been surveying something with a chimney in it. The form asks three questions and two of them are yes or no. He signs on the third, because the third asks whether the vessel is seaworthy, and it is, demonstrably, he has looked.

He does not know what he has done until the fourth page, where an older hand has written the previous survey date and next to it the word renewed, four times, in four different hands. He walks back the forty miles thinking about the word renewed, which on that page does not mean the boat was made safe. It means somebody walked.

Scene 5 · Int. dry cellar, pilot house · 8 pages

Int. Dry Cellar, Pilot House — Day

Eleven steps down and a door that has to be shouldered open, and then a stone floor with four hundred and eleven crates on it in rows nobody could now explain. Ninety yards of good rope. Forty barrels of lamp oil. Eleven clocks, stopped, and not one of them at the same hour. A coffin with nothing written on the lid and nothing written inside, which Marlow established in 1934 and reported in one line.

A harpsichord, upright, with the lid closed, is the object the camera keeps coming back to because it is the only thing in the room that was bought rather than stored. Marlow's list says nothing about where it came from. The cellar says nothing. The instrument itself, under the lid, has a maker's name on the fallboard and no other history at all.

Scene 6 · Ext. counting house steps · 2 pages

Ext. Counting House Steps — Late Afternoon

The auction is over in eleven minutes because there is one item and one bidder. A harbour book, incomplete, unsold since 1958, together with the harbour records building, which is to say together with a car park. The bidder is a man who collects maritime paper and who says, in a room full of people, that he wants the Marrow hand specifically, and does not apologise for saying it.

Final image of the film, held for a long time: the book open on a table in a county archive, nine years later, the endpaper lifted, nine folded sheets in a third hand, and the last line of the last sheet lost inside the fold. The camera does not unfold it. The scene is two pages long and it ends on the fold.
