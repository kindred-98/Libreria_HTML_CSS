A hole is a hole is a hole. A column punched at the top and not at the bottom means one thing to the machine and nothing at all to you, which is why the reading desk was always a place with a light over it and a clerk who had been doing it for years.

The six fields on this card are the only vocabulary it has. Whichever field is drilled, the machine reads that one, and the rest of the card stays blank until you ask for something else.

Field 01 · origin

The loom that taught the machine

Before the card there were cards: sheets of thick paper with holes cut along the top edge, each hole a different width, each width a different row of the pattern the loom was about to weave. A person read the pattern, a person punched the holes, and the loom did the rest without ever knowing what the picture meant.

The idea that a machine can be told what to do by nothing but the presence or absence of a hole is the whole of early computing, stated in cardboard. Everything that came later, from the tabulating rooms to the punch deck that went into the machine room, is a variation on that arrangement.

Field 11 · the grid

Eighty columns of truth

Standardising the card solved the problem the loom never had: sharing. A deck had to be readable by a machine in a different building, bought from a different company, ten years later. So the card got a fixed width, a corner cut so it could not go in upside down, and eighty numbered columns that meant exactly the same thing everywhere.

Eighty columns is not many characters. It is enough for a name, a number, a code and a bit of room, and the discipline it imposed on the people writing the programs is visible in every language we use now, where the line is still short and the data still has to fit somewhere.

Field 21 · the floor

The keypunch office

You did not write a program for the machine. You wrote a set of instructions on a coding form, and then you carried the form to a keyboard, and then you punched the form, one character at a time, with a machine that made a noise like a stapler. Typing it twice was standard, because a single missing hole was a night of hunting.

The room was loud and smelled of dust and warm electronics, and the skill was in the hands rather than the head. A good operator could punch a card without looking at the keyboard, and an office that trained its own operators could tell the difference between a deck that would read and a deck that would not.

Field 31 · the sorter

Sorting by light

A deck of cards arriving in a box was not a program yet, it was a filing problem. Machines sorted it: a hopper, a reader, a sorter with twelve pockets, and a clerk standing beside it pulling out every card with a hole in column thirty-one and sliding it into the seventh pocket.

That clerk was the human being in the loop, and the seventh pocket was the judgement. Whether a card belonged there was never written down anywhere, and the person who decided it usually went home at five and never thought about it again.

Field 41 · the deck

What a deck could say

A stack of cards is an argument in a very narrow language. Each card holds one record or one instruction, the order of the stack is the order of the argument, and the machine walks through it exactly once, at its own speed, without any idea of what any of it means.

The limitations were severe and strangely liberating. There was no room for a comment, no room for a doubt, no room to be elegant. A programmer working this way learned to spend a long time deciding what a single card should say, and very little time at all deciding how to say it twice.

Field 51 · the end

The last reader

For a while the card was the only sensible way to move information between one room and another, and then, without a great deal of drama, it stopped being so. The reader stayed in the building for a decade, kept working perfectly, and was finally unplugged in an afternoon nobody wrote down.

What survives is the habit. We still arrange things in rows, we still number the columns from the left, we still leave one field for a name and another for a number, and we still believe, somewhere, that a piece of information is more trustworthy when it has been put in a place that only one shape can occupy.
