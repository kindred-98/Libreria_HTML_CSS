This issue was assembled in four cities in eleven days, and the only thing the desks agreed on was the order of the pages. The river report runs first because it is the longest piece and the least likely to be read at the end of a flight.

Between them the four desks wrote eleven thousand words, threw away rather more, and settled on a single cover photograph that nobody is entirely happy with. That is the usual outcome, and it is usually the right one.

Three ports, one week, and a river that is measured differently in each of them. In the north the water is a boundary and in the south it is a road, and both descriptions are true enough to be quoted in the same paragraph.

The dispatchers filed from a ferry, a fishing boat and a lock keeper's kitchen. The kitchen was the quietest place of the three and produced the best sentences, which is not a coincidence.

The conversation lasted four hours and covered a single question: who draws a line, and what happens to the people who were already standing on it. The cartographer answers slowly and corrects herself twice, both times in the direction of more ambiguity.

Twice the subject asked to start the answer again, and both times the tape was kept. The magazine keeps both takes and prints the better one, which is not the same thing.

The atlas of this issue is eleven pages of margin and almost no map. What it shows instead is the space between two printed borders, a strip of land a kilometre wide that appears on one sheet and not on the next.

Our cartographer spent two years on that kilometre. The magazine is grateful, and the note in the margin says so, which is the least the magazine can do.

Thirty countries wrote in about the river, about the atlas, and about a photograph on page four that we had believed was of a lock and which several readers insist is of a bridge.

We checked. They were right, the caption was wrong, and the correction appears in this letter with the photographer credited in full, because a magazine that fixes its own errors in public is worth more than one that never makes any.
