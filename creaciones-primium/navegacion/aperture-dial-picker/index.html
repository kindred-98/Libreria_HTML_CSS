Stop 01 · Wide open

f/1.4

At this opening the depth of field at two metres is barely nine centimetres, so the focus has to land on the near eye and everything behind it dissolves into soft circles of light.

The iris is at its widest and the blades form a nearly round polygon, which is what gives the out-of-focus highlights their round shape instead of a polygonal one.

Stop 02 · Portrait

f/2

Half a stop down takes the edge off the softness wide open and gains a few centimetres of depth, enough to keep both eyes sharp in a three-quarter portrait.

Most fast primes are at their best here: contrast climbs, the corners stop smearing, and the background is still thoroughly melted.

Stop 03 · Reportage

f/2.8

The working opening for interiors and evening streets, where the shutter has to stay fast enough to freeze a person walking and the depth has to forgive a focus error of a few centimetres.

Autofocus points spread wider here because the lens no longer hides a soft frame at the edges.

Stop 04 · Street

f/4

Zone focusing becomes practical: set two metres and everything from one and a half to three is acceptably sharp, so the camera can be raised and fired without waiting.

Shadows stay open enough for daylight, and the hexagon formed by the blades begins to show in the specular highlights.

Stop 05 · Groups

f/5.6

Two rows of people, a table, a room: the depth that was measured in centimetres is now measured in metres and nobody at the back of the group falls out of focus.

This is also where most zoom lenses reach their sharpest corner to corner, before diffraction starts to bite.

Stop 06 · Landscape

f/8

Hyperfocal distance at this opening covers from a few metres to infinity on a fifty millimetre lens, which is why the foreground stone and the far ridge can both hold detail.

Any smaller and the aperture becomes a pinhole: the image softens through diffraction long before the depth of field gains anything useful.

Appendix

Care of the front element

Blow the dust off first, then a single pass with a lens cloth dampened in a drop of fluid, working from the centre outwards in a spiral. Rubbing a dry grain of grit across the coating is what scratches a lens, not the coating itself.

Leave the iris stopped down only for storage, never for months at a time, and keep the lens capped when the body is open so the sensor does not collect what the front element shed.
