01

Comb

The comb does not exist until the bees build it, and they build it out of something the bees made themselves. Every gram of wax in a frame cost the colony roughly eight grams of honey, which is why a colony that is failing on stores will not draw new comb no matter how much room you give it.

The geometry is not a choice either. Hexagonal cells are the only way to tile a plane with three walls at every corner, and the two sizes of cell in the frame are built to the same unit of measurement, so a bee moving between them never has to turn a corner mid-size.

02

Forager

A forager lives about six weeks and spends perhaps the last third of it on the wing. Before her first flight she does a short orientation flight around the hive, learns the position of the sun and the shape of the entrance, and starts building a mental map that she updates for the rest of her life.

That investment is the colony explaining why it can afford to lose her. A worker is cheap and replaceable; a forager with a map is not. Losing the foragers in one bad week can take a colony below the threshold it needs to recover at all.

03

Nectar

Nectar is a sugar solution, usually between fifteen and forty per cent, and it exists because plants have negotiated with insects rather than the other way round. The flower pays in carbohydrate for pollination, and the exchange rate is set by how thirsty the insect is.

A forager carries perhaps forty milligrams in her crop. The economics of a colony are simply that number multiplied by a few thousand workers, and a season of foraging is a season of arithmetic run at the scale of a body weight.

04

Ripe

Not all sealed cells are the same. A cell filled from below by hanging honey, that is by evaporating nectar in a comb cell whose rim already has a little wax, becomes the wettest thing in the hive and will not keep for long. Left alone it ferments and can spoil the whole frame.

So the bees push nectar up into cells that are already a third full, and they will not cap that. A capped cell is a promise that the honey is finished, and the wobbly full ones above are the ones the house bees are still working.

05

Cap

Capping is done in a single pass, from the edge inward, by a bee that secretes wax from glands under her abdomen and presses it on with her head. The result is a slightly domed cap with no seam, and a small air gap underneath that the beekeeper learns to recognise as the cell worth keeping.

Once capped, the cell is inert. The comb on the right of this page fills from the top down as you read: capped, matte and still, with the only living cell in the frame being the one holding whatever you are reading now.

06

Split

When stores are high enough and the frame is well filled, the colony stops treating the empty cells as real estate and starts treating them as a queue. Eggs go into them, larvae are capped in their own cells, and a new bee is being assembled while the queen keeps laying.

That queue is what a beekeeper is looking for in April. The comb has stopped being a cupboard and become a factory, and the empty cell you thought was space is the place where next month comes from.
