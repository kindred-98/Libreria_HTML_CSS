Lamp 1 · the light

The lamp

A signal lamp is a small bulb behind coloured glass in a metal housing, and it exists so that somebody in a cold room at three in the morning can be told something by looking up rather than by reading. The design brief is almost entirely about contrast: a light bright enough to notice through a dirty window, and dark enough around it that notice becomes certain.

Nothing about this is efficient. An indicator on a modern panel is a rectangle that changes colour, costs nothing, and is invisible from four rooms away. The lamp costs a bulb, a lens and a shade, and it can be read from the far end of a track in the dark, which is why the railway kept them long after they had become the slower option.

Lamp 2 · the run

The chain

Lamps are never hung alone. They run in series on a brass rail, each one fed from the one before it, and the state of the whole run is legible at a glance: how far the light has travelled is the same as how far the work has got. A signalman could read the position of a train from a row of lamps without counting anything.

The rail above these six is lit as far as the current lamp, and the light stops there. It is the oldest idea in this piece and the only one that needs no numbers at all: the chain is the position, and it is read the way a hand of cards is read.

Lamp 3 · the click

The relay

Behind every lamp is a relay, a small electromagnet that snaps a contact closed, and the snap is the sound the whole job is built around. It is a mechanical event with no ambiguity to it: either the contact closed or it did not, and the operator heard which one about a fifth of a second before the light confirmed it.

That delay is why the machines had a rhythm. A row of relays clicking at irregular intervals is the sound of a railway thinking, and a good signalman learned to hear a fault in it the way a doctor learns to hear an irregularity in a heartbeat, long before there was anything to see.

Lamp 4 · the hours

The night watch

The box was staffed all night by one person, and the room was deliberately made small, warm and badly lit, because a signalman who was comfortable would sleep. A lit lamp meant a train was approaching on the section in front of him, and the whole purpose of the night shift was to be awake and correct about the order in which those lamps came on.

Everything else about the job was waiting. The lamps told you something needed doing, and the doing was paperwork, a signal lever, and a lamp. Men and women who did it for decades described the time afterwards as the least active job they ever had and the one they thought about most.

Lamp 5 · the building

The signal box

A box was built where it was needed and pulled down when it was not, because the interlocking it contained was mechanical and the whole apparatus existed to guarantee that no two conflicting routes could ever be offered to a driver at the same time. The interlocking was the argument; the building was only a box to put it in.

They were also, unexpectedly, good places to work. High up, warm, with a view along the line in both directions and no interruptions except the ones that mattered. Several of the last ones were kept as offices long after the levers were disconnected, and the lamps stayed bolted to the wall where they had been.

Lamp 6 · the end

The last green

Colour signalling was the last change, and it arrived slowly, on routes where the failure of a single filament was a serious matter. Before that, a clear lamp meant a clear road and the colour only told you which kind of clear it was. Afterwards the system was easier to learn and harder to trust, which is the usual trade.

The lamps are still made and still sold, and they are still fitted to machines, and they still click. The chain of six above is a demonstration of the oldest arrangement there is: a light at the front of the run, an operator at the middle of it, and a decision at the end of it that somebody has to make by hand before anything is allowed to move.
