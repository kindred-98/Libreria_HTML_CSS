The header of this page is a six-light sash window in a cold frame. Every light has a pane in it, and the pane does not move on its own account: the light for the chapter you are reading slides its glass aside, the reflection on the surface travels with it, and the cold outside comes in through the gap it leaves behind.

Six lights, one sliding. The open light is the chapter you are reading.

Light I

The Sash

A sliding window has no hinge and no mechanism worth the name. There is a groove in the frame, a pane in the groove, and a hand. Everything else, all the parts a casement window needs and weighs, simply is not there, which is why a sliding window is the cheapest thing that can be called a window and the one that lasts longest in a house where nobody wants to paint.

The pane is held in the groove by a brush pile and by nothing else. Lift the pane out of the bottom groove and the whole window comes apart in your hands in under a minute, which is exactly how it is repaired and exactly why it can be repaired by anybody.

Light II

Cold Glass

Single glazing is cold to sit near, and it is cold because the glass is the only thing between a heated room and a winter sky. A pane loses heat fastest at its edges, where the glass is held in the groove and the seal has gone, and the first thing anybody checks in an old window is a cold spot in the corner of a light.

Condensation tells you the same story from the other side. It forms on the inside face of the glass as soon as the dew point crosses it, which in a cold room happens at the top of the pane first because the air there is warmest and least disturbed.

Light III

Sliding the Pane

To slide a pane you do not push it sideways. You lift the leading edge a few millimetres, clear of the groove, and then it runs. A sash that has been painted shut, or that has a brush pile which has swollen with age, will not do this at all and has to be freed before it will move a centimetre.

That lift is what you see on the header of this page. The pane for the chapter you are reading is lifted out of its groove and held a few millimetres proud of the frame, which is why the reflection on the glass sits a little higher than the reflection on the ones you are not reading.

Light IV

The Draught

The gap a sliding window leaves when it is open is not an accident, it is the design. An open light in a cold room is not a way of letting in fresh air, it is a way of letting out the warm air that has already been paid for, and the double-hung window next to it is the one that ventilates properly with a small opening at the top.

The lights on this page open one at a time, which is what a sash does when you only open the bottom corner. The cold comes in along the bottom edge first, and it is at the bottom of the frame, not the top, that you find the brush pile in pieces.

Light V

The Frame

The frame is the part everyone looks at and nobody notices. It has to carry the weight of the glass, the weight of the brushes, and the sideways push of somebody leaning on the pane to shut it, and it has to do all of that while being painted, and being painted again, and again, for as long as the window is in the wall.

Putty is the traditional answer and it is now the wrong one, because a modern frame will not hold a putty joint and a hard one has to be cut out in pieces. Flexible sealants took over for a reason, and the reason is that a window is not opened by sliding it up and down any more. It is lifted out and turned round on a workbench.

Light VI

Spring Cleaning

Once a year the panes come out and are washed on the inside face, and everybody who does this job for the first time is surprised by how much of the dirt is on the warm side of the glass. It is not smoke and it is not dust. It is the film the warm air leaves behind every time it touches cold glass.

Clean the outside first, then the inside, and dry both with a paper in the direction of the grain of the glass, because a cloth leaves a faint smear and the smear only shows when the sun is low and coming straight in. The pane goes back in the same way every time, in the same groove, with the same brush pile, and after that the window is as good as new and no heavier.

The window is the navigation: every light is a link into the chapters below, and the open light is the chapter you are reading.
