Stop 01 · the stop

The stop

A tram stop is a pole, a plate and a patch of ground, and the entire design problem is that a tram is a hundred and thirty millimetres from the kerb whereas a bus is three metres away. Passengers board from the pavement, so the pavement has to become the vehicle, and everything else on a stop exists to make that substitution work: a kerb, a clear standing area, and a rail edge that the boarding step will meet in exactly the right place.

What a stop needs most is width, and width is what it never gets. Two people waiting for the same tram need about a metre and a half each, and the classic four-post stop with a shelter and a timetable and a litter bin will put a dozen people into six. The stops on this street are spaced about two hundred and fifty metres apart, which is the walking distance at which people decide a tram is a service rather than a bus, and it is the only number in the whole arrangement that anybody measures.

Stop 02 · the post

The post

The post is a piece of steel tube with a plate on it, and its proportions have barely changed in eighty years because the loading is unchanged. A plate wants to be seen at an angle from across a street, a person wants to read a number at a glance, and a bus full of people will not wait for somebody who cannot work out which way round the plate faces.

The plate itself is vitreous enamel on sheet steel, fired, and it is one of the few outdoor signs that genuinely outlives its manufacturer. The colours have become a system rather than a design: the shape and the colour say the mode, and the number says the stop, and the small text underneath says the destinations. A modern post may replace the plate with a display, and the display can be changed from a distance, and the plate underneath it is still there because the plate works when the power is off.

Stop 03 · the track

The track

Tram track is flat-bottom rail laid in a groove, and the groove is the whole story of the tram. A groove gives the flanged wheel somewhere to go, which lets the rail be flush with the road surface, which lets a car cross it, which lets a tram run in a street that is still a street. The price is two running grooves per rail that fill with anything on the road and reduce the contact between wheel and rail to a thin steel edge.

Which is why the maintenance budget of a tram system is spent on the track and not on the cars. Grooves are cleaned, not by choice but because a tram that cannot find its groove will leave the rails, and a tram that leaves the rails on a street full of people is the single worst outcome available to a transit operator. The rails on this street are the oldest part of the installation and the most looked after, and that is not a coincidence.

Stop 04 · the board

The board

A printed timetable at a tram stop is a piece of paper in a frame, and its job is to tell you when a service that runs every twelve minutes will next come. That is a harder problem than it sounds, because it means predicting traffic, and traffic on a street with trams in it is not traffic. A printed board is therefore always slightly wrong and always useful, which is why operators have been reluctant to remove them even after the display arrived.

The display and the board disagree in a specific and irritating way. The display is right and the board is optimistic, and the gap between them grows through the afternoon and closes at the end of the day, and regular riders learn to read the difference without ever being told about it. The best compromise found so far is a display that shows the next two departures only, because a list of ten departures is a promise about a quarter of an hour that nobody is going to hold the operator to.

Stop 05 · the signal

The signal

Tram signalling is a compromise between railway practice and traffic practice, and it has been forced along by a single fact: the vehicle is on the road. A tram cannot be protected by a signal alone, because a car will not obey it, so the protection has to come from somewhere else. The result, in most systems, is a small signal head at the kerb that the driver can see and that the traffic cannot.

Some systems use line of sight, some use a tram priority signal ahead of the junction, and some use a priority lane with a detection loop that turns the traffic lights in the tram's favour when a vehicle is approaching. The detection loop is the interesting one, because it is a piece of road that has been given a job, and it explains why the stretch of track outside every junction on a modern tram route is the part of the line that feels different to drive over.

Stop 06 · the priority

The priority

Every tram scheme is an argument about the street, and the argument is always the same shape. The tram needs the road. The cars need the road. Somebody proposes a lane, and the lane is contested for about four years, and then the tram opens and everybody agrees within eighteen months. There is very little in the record of a hundred and thirty years of this that predicts the outcome well.

What has changed is the evidence. A tram in a reserved lane moves at a speed a car cannot, on a corridor the car cannot, and the six stops on this street are spaced for that speed. The pole, the plate and the lamp have not changed at all, which is the part of the story that is worth knowing: everything visible here predates the lane, and the lane is the only thing that made it worth waiting for.
