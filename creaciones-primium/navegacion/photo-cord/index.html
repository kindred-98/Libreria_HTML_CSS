Who is in this line

Nobody, in five prints out of six. The sixth is a hand. The hand belongs to the woman who took them and it appears exactly once, low and to the right, in a print she was told to put away.

The line is strung at a height where a person of average build can reach it without a chair, which is how you tell a line that is used from a line that is decorative.

One print has been taken off the line and put back so many times that the paper has gone soft at the corner. It is the one about the window.

Frame one · printed 1958

The Front Door

The house had a front door that was painted every four years and never the same colour twice, and the first print on this line was taken to record the fact of it. The paint is described in the negative as a red that is not the red in the print, which is the sort of note somebody writes when they are arguing with a shop.

The print hangs at the far left of the line, which is where it has always hung. It was rehung in 1961 and again in 1974 and the pin mark in the paper is the same hole both times, worn slightly to the left of centre.

Frame two · printed 1959

The Kitchen Table

Sundays were taken on the kitchen table because it was the only surface in the house with enough light and because nobody minded a camera on a table the way people mind it pointed at them. Six prints came out of that year and three of them survived. This is the best of the three and it is not a good print.

The subject is the table. Two chairs are cut off by the frame. On the table there is a jug and there is no bread, and the absence of bread has been the subject of at least one argument in this family about whether the jug was ever full.

Frame three · printed 1961

The Window Over The Sink

The most taken-down print on the line, and the one with the soft corner. It has been off and on the line perhaps two hundred times, always in the same order, always replaced before anybody could see it had been off at all, which is a family habit rather than a decision.

What is in the window is a wall opposite and the top of a gas holder. What everybody remembers about it is that for about two seconds on the right hand side there is a shape that could be a person. The negative does not have it. The print does. Nobody has ever resolved this and the print stays on the line.

Frame four · printed 1962

The Road To The Station

Four miles of road between the house and the station, hedged both sides, with a bend at the third mile that is the reason the road exists in the shape it does. The fog print was taken from the same spot at the same hour on four different days and only one of them held.

The pin for this print is a longer pin than the others, because the card is thicker, because it has been in the cold. It hangs noticeably lower. Nobody has ever swapped it for a matching pin and the line has never looked level.

Frame five · printed 1963

A Hand On A Rope

The only print with a hand in it, and it is not the photographer's, and it is the only one on this line that was ever asked to be destroyed. It was not destroyed. It was put at the end of the line, lower down, where people stop looking, which is a decision that has held for sixty years.

What the hand is holding is a coil of rope of the kind used on a lighter in a harbour, and the negative was found among eleven others of which ten are of this house. The eleventh is the back door.

Frame six · printed 1963

The Back Door, Closed

The last print on the line and the only one where the subject refused to be a subject. It is a door, closed, with a hasp and a padlock, and it is in focus, and it took eleven seconds to expose because the shutter was slow and the door was not going anywhere.

Eleven seconds is the longest exposure in the box and the print has a soft edge on the left that nobody has ever been able to explain, including the woman who took it. It hangs on the line at the far right, where the light is worst, and it is the one print that has never been straightened.
