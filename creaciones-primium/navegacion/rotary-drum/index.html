Turn the drum with the pointer, or read the panel that is facing you. The drum does not remember where it was left, which is why the index mark is the only honest thing on the front of this machine.

Panel i · the drum

The cylinder

Before a screen showed you what was happening, a cylinder did. A drum of paper turned slowly on an axis, a hand moved it along, and whatever was written on the drum was the current state of the world for as long as it took somebody to write over it. The information was not stored anywhere else because the drum was the only copy.

The machine is the size of a hatbox, weighs about as much as a full suitcase, and has no moving part more complicated than a bearing. Everything clever about it happened before it was manufactured: in the decision to use a cylinder at all, which makes a notice that goes round twice before it is replaced.

Panel ii · the notices

The panels

Each notice is a sheet of paper pasted to the drum, written in ink, and it has to be readable from two paces away while a train is arriving. That is a hard typographic brief, and the letters came out large, spaced widely, and usually in a mixture of printing and handwriting depending on how much time the person writing it had.

When the sheet was full it was pasted over. So a drum that had been running for a month looked like a drum that had been running for a day, and the only record of what had been said before was in the head of the attendant, who was the machine's memory.

Panel iii · the reading

The window

A strip of glass across the front turned a cylinder into a page. Whatever happened to be behind the glass was, by definition, the thing being read, and the arrangement has a quality that no screen has ever managed: there is exactly one thing there, and you can see that there are more things behind it.

You can also see how much is left. As a notice runs out of travel the window gives you the next one coming, edge first, in the time it takes to glance away and look back. People who grew up with this read the edge of the paper differently from people who did not.

Panel iv · the drive

The turn

Some of these machines were driven by a weight on a cord that had to be wound every morning, and some were driven by a spring that had to be let down at night. Either way the drum's speed was fixed by the machine and not by the reader, which meant that the reading speed of an entire platform was, in a small but real sense, decided in a workshop.

The pointer in the middle is a detent, not a handle. Press it and the drum moves one panel and stops exactly, which is the only electronic behaviour the machine ever had: a click, a wait, and a number.

Panel v · the person

The attendant

Somebody stood next to this machine for a whole shift. The job was to answer the machine, not the other way round: to know the next notice before it came round, to be writing when the drum reached the end of what was on it, and to keep the ink thick and the glass clean and the weight wound.

It was a lonely job by any measure and it produced a particular kind of attention, the kind that notices that the drum is slowing down four minutes before it does. People who did it for years could tell you what an entire station was like from the pace of its notices.

Panel vi · the last turn

Stopping time

At the end of the day the drum was unwound to the same panel it had started on, so that the first train in the morning would find the machine in a state somebody recognised. There was a place on the frame for a chalk mark, and the chalk mark was how a new attendant learned where the day was supposed to begin.

They are still made, in a few of them, and they are still wound at the end of the day, and the machine still has no memory at all. It is the simplest possible arrangement for keeping a public promise, and for two centuries it was the fastest way anybody had found of telling a stranger something they needed to know.
