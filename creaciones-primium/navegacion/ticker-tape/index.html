Tape 1 · the strip

The tape

The output of a ticker was a continuous paper tape, usually six inches wide, and it came out of the machine at whatever speed the news was arriving. There was no start and no finish to a tape. A story that ran out simply became the next story, and an editor who wanted to know where a report began needed a marker, a small object placed in the flow of paper to be noticed later.

The tapes were collected on spools, one spool per day, and read afterwards by people with more time than the machine had. What came off a spool was a day of the world in a format that could be unrolled, read at a walking pace, and cut into pieces with scissors.

Tape 2 · the print

The head

Printing a character meant a wheel, a hammer and a ribbon, and printing quickly meant all three happening forty times a minute without the paper tearing. The machine got the speed from a rotating shaft and the letters from two concentric rings of type, so the whole alphabet was a piece of metal about the size of a fist turning once for every word.

The failure mode was the ribbon. A ribbon that had been used for a week produced a pale, smeared line, and the operators learned to change them on a schedule nobody had written down, judged entirely by eye and by the complaints of whoever was reading the output.

Tape 3 · the pace

The feed

A ticker ran at a fixed speed, and every decision about pace was really a decision about the machine rather than the reader. Slow it down and the text comes out clear and the stories fall behind. Speed it up and the machine keeps up with a telephone wire, and the text arrives as a grey blur that only the young people in the room can read.

So the paper was read at twenty inches a second, standing up, in a room with other people talking, and a great deal of what a ticker operator did every day was look at a fast-moving grey line and turn it back into sentences.

Tape 4 · the extras

The extra

When a story was bigger than the wire could carry it went down the street instead, on a hand-delivered tape, and the street was run by a courier on a bicycle with a satchel. The courier carried a small machine, stopped outside the office, and called the editor on a portable telephone so that somebody inside could listen to the tape and decide whether it was worth setting in type.

This was the slow part of the business, and it was the part that decided what actually got printed. A report that was a good idea and two hours away was not a report. Speed was a political question, and the machines on both ends of it were the reason it had been asked.

Tape 5 · the room

Reading over a shoulder

The newsroom gathered around the machine, which was the point. Reporters, editors, a photographer, all of them reading the same tape at the same time and arguing about the next question, in a language made entirely of sentences half heard. A person who worked in that room was a person who had been interrupted four hundred times a day and had learned to keep the thread of a thought while being interrupted.

What the tape could not do was stop. It never waited for anybody to be ready, never finished a thought, and never repeated itself, which meant that the room learned a particular skill: how to pick up a sentence in the middle and be listening by the end of the first line.

Tape 6 · the end

The wire reels

The machine is gone and the tape is not, which is the usual situation with obsolete technology. Files of spooled ticker tape survive in newspaper archives and in the collections of people who could not throw anything away, and reading them is strange: a day of a city in four columns of type, with nothing in it about how anyone felt at the time.

A tape will not tell you which of the six lines you were reading, and it will not wait. It goes past at the same speed for everybody, which was the condition of the whole arrangement: you were either standing there reading, or you were not, and the machine did not care which.
