01 Index

Everything on
four axles

The Ferro catalogue lists the fifty-one vehicles kept by the valley railway: locomotives that still work, coaches that still carry passengers, and the wagons that carried the limestone which built half this county.

The list is maintained by eleven volunteers, three of whom have been here since the line reopened in 1979. Measurements were retaken last spring with a steel tape rather than a laser, because the tape has never run out of battery in the middle of a shed.

02 Locomotives

Three engines, one shed

Number 41, an 0-6-0 tank engine built for shunting, does the weekday work. Number 77 pulls the weekend specials and is the only one of the three permitted to run with passengers on the gradient beyond the viaduct.

The third, a diesel of 1968, is kept purely for shunting in winter, when the water troughs are frozen and nobody wants to explain to a board member why the car park is full of carriages.

03 Coaches

Teak that never needed paint

The 1957 lot comprises nine coaches in varnished teak, each thirty metres long, with gas-lamp fittings removed in 1971 and kept in a crate under the guard's van rather than thrown away, which somebody in 1971 would certainly have done now.

Seating is two plus one in first class and two plus two elsewhere. The buffers are original, the brakes are not, and the ride is firm enough that regular passengers have stopped mentioning it on the comment cards.

FH 12

First open

Twenty-eight seats, eight tables, varnish touched up twice since restoration and never fully stripped.

TS 31

Third saloon

Forty-eight seats in compartments of six, with the original call bells still wired to the guard's van.

GF 04

Grill car

Kitchen in service since 1994, licensed, and the only carriage that is never lent to other railways.

04 Wagons

The limestone train

Fourteen open wagons ran daily from the quarry to the cement works between 1922 and 1968. Eleven survive, four of them unrestored on purpose: the rust on those is the actual working surface and scrubbing it would only make them lighter and less true.

The brake van carries the tool chest, the kettle and the rule that nobody may ride in a wagon while the train is moving, a rule that has been broken exactly once and is now written on the inside of the door.

05 Signalling

Wooden arms and a bell

The line is worked by twenty-six semaphore signals and a bell code that has not changed since the grouping of 1923. Each lever in the box is painted to the standard colour for its function, and the locking frame physically prevents the combinations that would put two trains in the same section.

Visitors are shown the frame, the token apparatus and the kettle. The kettle is not part of the interlocking, but it has been in the same corner for longer than any of the levers.

06 Preserved line

Eighteen kilometres, kept open

The valley line closed to regular traffic in 1969 and was lifted in part before the preservation society bought the trackbed back, plank by plank, from three separate owners who each believed they owned the bridge.

Today the line runs from the market town to the quarry terminus, crosses a viaduct of eleven arches, and loses four metres of height over the middle section, which is precisely the amount of gradient a 1920s freight train could manage without drama.

07 Visit the depot

Saturdays, from eleven

The depot opens on Saturdays between eleven and three, and on the first Sunday of each month the shed is cleared enough to walk around a locomotive, which is the only way to understand how much of a steam engine is actually plumbing.

Boots are advisable, the floor is oily in places that look clean, and the tour ends in the mess room with tea, because every tour in the history of railways has ended in a mess room with tea.
