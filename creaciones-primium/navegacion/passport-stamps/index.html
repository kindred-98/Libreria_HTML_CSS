Visas and stamps are recorded in the order the holder travelled. The order is not always the order of the map.

Border marks on this passport

A pale impression is a stamp the passport still carries from a previous crossing. It has not been pressed on this pass.

A full impression is the crossing you are reading. It lands with the heel of a hand, which is why the ring is never quite even.

The rail under the map is the whole journey measured once, not twice, so it only ever fills forwards.

Entry 1 · Alderburg · 4 March

Alderburg, Land Border

The passport was issued here to a harbour boy who had been counting other people's boats for a winter and had, by the end of it, produced a book of figures better kept than the official one. The clerk at the counter asked him how he had learned to write a figure so that it could be read upside down, and he said he had not learned it, he had simply got tired of rewriting them.

The stamp is the oldest in the book and the only one with a full date in Roman numerals, because the die in 1954 was still the die from 1911 and nobody thought to change it. Two more were ordered later and both arrived broken.

Entry 2 · Corrow Point · 17 June

Corrow Point, Sea Transit

A transit stamp, which is not an entry, which is the entire difference between a man who went somewhere and a man who passed through. The passport went to Corrow Point because the boy who had learned the figure went to Corrow Point, and came back with a licence that had expired in March and a boat that was entirely capable of crossing an estuary in June.

The stamp at Corrow Point is the smallest and the faintest of the six, pressed with a pad that had run out of ink and was refilled halfway through the impression. You can see the join in the ring if you hold the page at an angle, which is the sort of thing this passport was bought for.

Entry 3 · Valdemar · 2 September

Valdemar, Land Border

Valdemar is inland, which is the only interesting thing about it. Getting there from the coast means a train, a bus and four hours of walking, and the border officer at Valdemar had a reputation for asking questions that were not about the passport. He asked, on this occasion, whether the holder had come to survey boilers, and the holder said no, and the officer stamped anyway.

The stamp is applied slightly off centre, low and to the left, in a way that suggests a wrist rather than a machine. Later stamps on this passport are all a little crooked. That is not an error of the die. It is the passenger getting braver.

Entry 4 · St Bride's · 21 November

St Bride's, Night Crossing

The night crossing is the one everybody in the family remembers and nobody can date. It happened during the nineteen days the harbour was closed, on a night when the wind was still doing what it had been doing since the ninth of January, and it is the only crossing on this passport that has no time written beside it, only a date and a word, NIGHT, added later in pencil.

The stamp ink here is nearly black, which means the officer used the office pad rather than the field pad, and the office pad was kept in a heated room. Somebody carried this passport a long way in very bad weather in order to have it stamped indoors.

Entry 5 · Kestrel Bay · 8 February

Kestrel Bay, Sea Transit

Kestrel Bay is where the four hundred and eleven crates went when the cellar above the pilot house stopped being a cellar and started being a problem. Ninety barrels of lamp oil went by ship. Eleven clocks went by ship. The harpsichord went by ship, upright, with the lid nailed down, which cost more than the boat.

The stamp is the cleanest of the six and the only one printed in a blue that matches the ink of the rail underneath the map, which is a coincidence the family has chosen to believe in. The officer at Kestrel Bay had a press that worked properly, and used it only once a day, at closing.

Entry 6 · Home Port · 30 June

Home Port, Closed

CLOSED is not a category of border control. It is what the officer wrote by hand, in the last empty box on the last visa page, because there was nothing left in the book to stamp and somebody had to mark the end of it. The date is the thirtieth of June, the passport expires the following spring, and the holder stops travelling three days before the ink on the Home Port stamp has properly dried.

Kept at home, on a shelf, above a desk, in a house that burned in 1974. It came out of the drawing room cupboard with the pages stuck slightly together at the Valdemar leaf, and it has been stuck there since.
