Slab 01 · the lay

The lay

A pavement is a decision about direction. The slabs go down in courses, each one turned ninety degrees from the one below, so that the joint lines run at right angles to the way people walk. The rule came from cart wheels, not from taste: a wheel that crosses a joint at an angle breaks the edge, and a broken edge is the first thing a foreman sees.

What you are looking at is the surface course, laid last, on top of a base nobody ever photographs. Below the lime-coloured slabs there is a bed of compacted hardcore, then a blinding layer of weak concrete, then the actual ground. The top is the only part of the pavement that anybody has an opinion about, and it is the part that costs the least and fails the soonest.

Slab 02 · the joint

The joint

The gap between two slabs is four millimetres of lime mortar, and it is the most misunderstood part of a pavement. People assume it is a weakness. It is the opposite: the joint is what lets the surface move with the ground underneath it without cracking, and a slab bedded tight against its neighbour is a slab that will split in its first hard frost.

It is also the only part of a pavement that is alive. Water sits in the joint, moss takes it, and the moss is not a defect but a clock. A street that has been walked for thirty years has a green line along its edges and a scoured middle, and the shape of that green is a more honest record of the street than any survey.

Slab 03 · the lifted slab

The lifted slab

Somewhere under every pavement is a bad surprise waiting to be expensive: a valve, a duct, a survey mark, a length of iron pipe nobody recorded. When the utility comes to it, the slab over it comes up. A triangle of three pieces is levered out, the work is done, and the patch is closed with whatever the yard had on the lorry that morning.

The lifted slab in this pavement is the piece you are standing on. It does not mark a place, it marks a state: you are in the middle of the street, one of six, and the pavement is open under your feet. Reading a city from its pavements means learning to notice which tiles are proud by a centimetre and which are dead flush, because that difference is where the money went.

Slab 04 · the repair

The repair

A slab fails from the top down, or it fails all at once. Frost gets into the pore structure, the water inside freezes and expands, and the face spalls off in a patch the size of a fist, leaving the rough grey aggregate exposed. The repair crew that arrives sees a face that is now rougher than the road it was supposed to outlive.

The cheap fix is a cold patch: clean the square, prime it, tip in a bag of bitumen-bound grit, float it flat, walk on it by lunchtime. The expensive fix is to lift out the neighbours, re-bed the whole bay on fresh hardcore, and relay it. Most streets get the first one for a year or two and then get the second, and the slabs that survive the process are the ones with a different number stamped on the side.

Slab 05 · the winter

The winter

Salt is the reason a pavement in a cold country looks the way it does. Grit is spread first, in a thin grey film that gets tracked indoors within the hour. Then the salt goes to work, and what it does is dissolve the lime in the binder and carry it away in the meltwater, so the surface goes pale, powdery and finally sandy underfoot.

Nobody in the trade argues about this any more. The pavement that lasts thirty years is the one the grit was skipped on, or the one laid in concrete blocks instead of cast stone, and both of those are more expensive to start and much cheaper to keep. The winter decides which streets survive, and it does not send a letter first.

Slab 06 · the count

The count

You can walk a street and know where you are by the paving alone. The slabs change width at a boundary, the joint pattern changes at a corner, the colour changes where one borough hands over to the next. In a town that has been rebuilt four times, the pavement is the only surface that has been rebuilt on top of itself so often that the layers are legible.

Six slabs, one always lifted. That is the whole idea of a pavement: it is a surface that hides the working of the city under it, and the only way to see the working is to move something. The green line in the joint, the proud tile over the stop valve, the polished middle where everybody turns for the bus. Count the slabs and you are counting the sections of the street, one at a time, under your feet.
