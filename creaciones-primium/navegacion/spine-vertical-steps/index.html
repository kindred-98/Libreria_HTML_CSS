Station 01

Reading the grain

Turn the board over under a raking light before a pencil touches it. Grain that runs straight from end to end parts cleanly under a chisel; grain that swirls around a knot tears the moment you cut against the direction of the fibres.

Drag a thumbnail across the face. The direction that feels smooth is the direction the fibres lie, and every stroke you make afterwards has to follow it. Wood never argues with a tool that travels the way it grew.

Bench noteMark the flattest face with a chalk cross and keep that face against the bench all day, so every measurement starts from the same reference.

Station 02

Marking and measuring

One honest line comes from a try square and a sharp pencil. Where the surface will stay visible, knife the line instead: the blade severs the top fibres and the tool finds that groove again on the next pass, even months later.

Measure twice from the same reference face. Boards are rarely square, and half a millimetre of doubt at the marking stage becomes a visible gap once the joint is knocked together.

Bench noteKeep the knife line on the waste side of the cut, so the fibres close over the mark instead of opening into a ragged edge.

Station 03

The crosscut

Set the plate lightly in the knife line and start with two short back strokes, letting the saw find its own track instead of forcing it. Once the kerf is started, use the full length of the blade and let the teeth do the work.

Cut just outside the line and stop about a millimetre short of the depth. The last of the waste falls away under a chisel, and the edge that remains is exactly the one you measured.

Bench noteThirty teeth per inch leaves a finish that needs no sanding; a coarse blade is faster but leaves a bruised surface you will chase later.

Station 04

Planing to a line

Clamp the board with the marked edge overhanging the bench and take shavings rather than slices. A well-set plane produces a ribbon you can read through, and the line on the end grain tells you the instant to stop.

Check for wind with two winding sticks, one at each end. If the far stick leans against the near one, take a pass along the high diagonal and check again before you touch the long edge.

Bench noteAlways plane downhill, from the thick end towards the thin end, or the trailing fibres will lift and leave a torn surface.

Station 05

The housing joint

Saw both shoulders of the housing to the depth line, then remove the waste in shallow passes with a bevel-edge chisel, bevel down, working from each end towards the middle so the centre stays proud until the last cut.

Test the fit with the mating board held square. A joint that closes under light hand pressure and still holds when you lift the assembly is the target; anything looser needs a shim of the same timber.

Bench noteChop the waste from both faces in equal passes. Clearing one side to full depth in a single effort blows out the exit surface.

Station 06

Oil and wax

Let the surface cure for a full day after the final pass, then wipe on a thin coat of hardwax oil with the grain and buff it away completely within ten minutes. Anything left standing turns sticky and clouds the figure.

Two or three coats build a surface that sheds water and still feels like timber rather than plastic. Lightly de-nib with four hundred grit between coats so the next layer has something to bite.

Bench noteAlways test the finish on an offcut from the same board; oak and cherry darken far more in the first hour than the sample chip suggests.
