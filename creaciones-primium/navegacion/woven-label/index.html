Six rows of woven tape. The row you are reading is the one with the stitches out.

Row I

The Fibre

The first line of a care label is a legal requirement in most countries and it is the only line that has to be right. Fibre content is declared as a percentage by weight, in descending order, and the percentages have to add to a hundred. A garment that declares fifty-five cotton and forty-five polyester has told you almost everything about how it will behave for the rest of its life.

The reason the percentages are by weight and not by anything else is that weight is the only thing anybody can measure quickly, cheaply and without destroying the cloth. It is also why a cotton-rich garment can feel like a synthetic one: the fibre is cotton and the finish is not.

Row II

Warp and Weft

A label is woven, not printed, and it is woven narrow. The mill runs a few hundred labels across the width of a loom and they come off the machine as a continuous tape with a weft thread at the edge of every label, which is what you cut through to separate one from the next.

Woven labels outlast printed ones by a very long way, because a printed label depends on an ink sitting on the surface of the tape and a woven label is the tape. A shirt bought in the nineteen-eighties can usually still be read, and you can feel with a thumb where the letters are thicker than the ground.

Row III

The Red Thread

Most care labels carry a thread of a second colour in the selvedge, and the international convention is that a red thread means do not wash. It is the oldest labelling standard still in use and it has not changed its meaning since it was invented for a sewing room in the middle of the last century.

The thread is not decorative. It is a marker that can be read at arm's length in a drawer, before the label has been unfolded, and the whole point of a label is that it has to be read before the garment is worn rather than after.

Row IV

Unpicking

Unpicking a seam is done from the loose end, one stitch at a time, with a seam ripper and never with a knife. The point of the ripper is that it goes under the stitch and lifts it off the cloth instead of cutting the thread in half, so the stitch comes out whole and the seam keeps its shape for the next attempt.

The stitch you are reading on this page is unpicked that way, one stitch at a time from the top down, and the cut widens as it goes. Nothing is hidden and nothing snaps back, which is the difference between unpicking a seam and tearing one.

Row V

Provenance

A good label names the country where the garment was made, not the country where the cloth was woven, and the two have been different for fifty years. The country of assembly is compulsory; the mill is usually a code in four letters, and the code means nothing outside the brand that registered it.

Some labels go further and print the actual mill, the sewing subcontractor and the date. Those labels are not marketing, they are supply chain regulation, and they are there because somebody had to be named before anybody had to be paid properly.

Row VI

Sewing It Back

A label that has been unpicked is not an emergency, and it is not worth a trip to the tailor. It is four seconds with a needle and a doubled thread: back through the same holes, in and out at the edge of the original stitch line, and the seam goes back to the strength it had before.

Sew it back at the same length of stitch and with the same thread, and the row on this page closes again exactly as it was, which is the point of a seam that has been unpicked properly rather than cut.

The label is the navigation: every row is a link into the chapters below, and the row with the stitches out is the chapter you are reading.
