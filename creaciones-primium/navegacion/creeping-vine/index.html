Chapter 1 of 6 · Rootstock

01

Rootstock

A vine begins underground, in the dark, doing arithmetic nobody sees. Every shoot that will ever appear on this wall is already being priced in the root: which metre of soil is damp, which pocket of gravel will hold through August, how far the roots can safely spread before they start arguing with the drainpipe.

That hidden work is the reason a creeping plant looks so confident on a wall it has never seen. It is not improvising when it finds a seam. It is following a map it drew months earlier, in the dark, with a chemical vocabulary we are only now learning to read.

02

Tendril

The first reach is almost always wasted. A tendril uncoils into empty air, sweeps a slow arc, and finds nothing worth holding. Climbers call it a negative trial, and it is not a failure: the sweep is how the tendril learns the texture of the wall, how rough the plaster is, whether the light says there is a crack or a gutter behind.

Once a tendril touches something cool and rough enough, it thickens within hours, wraps tighter than the grip of a hand, and begins to look less like a searching filament and more like a cable under tension.

03

Decision

Leaves are expensive. Each one is a flat panel of evaporating surface, and the plant has to earn it. A shoot will hold every leaf folded in its bud for days, waiting for light to reach a threshold it can measure, and only then open the blade flat in a single irreversible gesture.

Once open, the leaf never closes again. It will yellow, it will be chewed, it will drop in an autumn storm, but the moment of unfurling is a one-way door. Watch the runner on the left: the leaves arrive already spread, never as rolled buds.

04

Nodes

Where a leaf leaves, a node stays behind, and nodes are where the plant argues with itself. Buds sit at the angle between two stems, dormant, holding the possibility of a second direction. The vascular bundles that fed the leaf are re-routed, and the stem above the node swells slightly as it takes over the load.

Walk down a mature vine and you can read its whole history in the swelling: thick where it carried heavy seasons, pinched where something was cut and never forgiven.

05

Flowers

Reproduction is a gamble the plant takes only when it has already won the wall. Years of pure climbing, a decade of leaves held open against the light, and then a single week of flowering that spends everything stored in the root. Insects arrive, pollen moves, and the vine becomes briefly the most important object in the garden.

By the time the petals fall the leaves will have thinned. Climbing is what the plant does; flowering is what it survives having done.

06

Aftermath

What is left is not a plant but a scaffold. The woody stems stay fixed to the plaster for years after the leaves have gone, holding position in the dark, and the dormant buds in each node wait out the winter with their plans intact.

Then the light comes back over the wall, the threshold is crossed, and the runner starts again from the same wood, one more storey higher, opening its leaves flat against the morning.
