The only rule on this table

The chip that is lifted off the pile is the chapter you are reading. It is the only chip with a shadow under it, because it is the only chip not resting on anything.

Everything below it has been read and stays in the stack. The stack only ever grows downwards, and the pot on the rail is the sum of the chips in it, so a longer read is a bigger pot.

Chips that have not been read yet wait in the rack on the right, face up, in order. A chip leaves the rack once and never comes back to it.

Chip of 5 · the first chips were not circles

Clay, Then Compression

The first playing chips were slices of bone, and the reason for the round shape was not a decision anybody took. A bone counter is cut from a long strip, and when it is finished it chips off at the corners, so a maker who wanted a hundred counters that would survive a pocket discovered within a week that a disc has no corners to chip. The circle is a solution to a problem in durability that has been carried forward for six hundred years without anybody knowing why it is there.

Clay came later and is the ancestor of everything on this table. A clay disc is pressed, dried, and then baked with enough heat that the surface vitrifies, which is why an old clay chip is orange rather than brown and why a chipped one shows a pale core. Composite and plastic chips followed, and the whole point of the change was weight: a stack of plastic chips does not read as money to anybody, including the person who stacked it.

Chip of 25 · six rectangles, rotated

The Edge Spot

A casino chip is not one disc. It is six thin rectangles of plastic, pressed together, with the edge spots moulded into the seam at six evenly spaced points around the rim, and the reason for the six is that a stack of six fits against a rack of six and a dealer can see every denomination in a stack at a glance without touching it.

The edge spot is also the answer to the only real problem with stacking round things, which is that a round chip can be placed on a round chip in any rotation and the stack looks the same from above no matter what is underneath. The six spots put a clock face on the rim. A dealer reading a stack can see at a glance whether the chips are mixed, and in a game where the whole point is to know what is in front of you, that one feature is worth more than everything else on the chip.

Chip of 50 · what the colours are for

Denominations and the Colour Code

Chip colours are not decorative and they are not universal. Different rooms use different codes, the codes are not consistent between a poker room and a card room in the same building, and the number is always printed on the chip as well so that the colour is a shortcut and never the information. The chip in your hand is a piece of equipment you read, not a piece of information you memorise.

What the code is for is speed under pressure, and the pressure is specific. In the middle of a hand somebody has to answer the question what is in front of me, and the answer has to be available from a metre away while a hand of cards is being dealt. A stack that is entirely one colour is a stack nobody has to think about. A stack with two colours is a decision.

Chip of 100 · where a chip lives before it is used

The Rack and the Stack

A rack is a tray with a sloped floor and a lip, and its only job is to stop the chips from sliding when somebody leans on the table. The chips sit in it in rows, face up, in denomination order, and a dealer takes them from the bottom up so that the last denomination to be reached is the one that runs out last. Every rack on this page is laid out the same way, and the order of the chips in the rack is the order they will be needed.

Stacking is the opposite of racking. A stack is vertical, unstriped, and counted by the dealer rather than by the player, and the difference matters at the end of a hand when somebody claims a pot and the dealer counts it out loud in front of everybody. The claim is only meaningful because the count is public, and the count is only fast because the stack is uniform.

Chip of 500 · by the edge, never by eye

Counting a Stack

A chip stack is counted twice and the two counts are made by different people. The first is by eye, in fives, because the edge spots divide a stack into fives and a trained eye reads the spots without touching anything. The second is by hand, a thumb at a time, in front of everyone, and it takes four times as long and is the only count anybody trusts.

This is why the stack on this page is built one chip at a time rather than appearing all at once. A stack that changes height in one movement is a graphic. A stack that grows a chip at a time is a count, and the count is the part of the ritual anybody in the room is actually watching.

Chip of 1000 · everything in the middle of the table

The Pot

A pot is not a sum. It is a heap in the middle of a table, and the sum is an abstraction of the heap that only exists after somebody has counted it. Between the betting and the count the pot is a physical object, and the physical object has properties the number does not: it is heavy, it is in the way, and it can be slid toward you before anybody has counted it.

The pot on this header is a number, and the number is the sum of the six chips: five, twenty five, fifty, one hundred, five hundred and one thousand. Read the whole page and the pot is one thousand six hundred and eighty, which is the sum of every chip on the table and the only figure in the header that has changed since the first paragraph was written.
