Day one · Friday 20 June · shipping

The Ferry Sails After Three Summers

The licensing office moved in the March of a year nobody wrote down, and for three summers the harbour had a boat that was sound, a master with thirty years on the crossing, and no document. The newspaper ran four paragraphs on it in 1955 and did not run it again until the boat moved, which is the correct ratio and everybody involved agreed on that.

On the Friday it went out with nine passengers, one of them the clerk who had walked forty miles to sign a form. It came back at nine in the evening against a rising tide, and the cutting in the courier does not mention this, because by then there was nothing to say.

Day two · Monday 4 November · the record

The Harbour Book Changes Hands At Mid Page

Silas Marrow's letters grew narrower as he got older, which meant that the date of any entry in the harbour book could be read off the page without asking anybody. When he died in September the last fourteen entries in the old hand were followed, on the same sheet, by the word Received in a round even hand slightly too large.

Half the town held that a record has no author and a record is not a trust. The other half held the opposite and said so in the meeting, at length. The book is still on the shelf in the records building, open at the seam, and the seam has been described by two archivians as the most argued-over half inch in the county.

Day three · Tuesday 21 January · weather

Eleven Days On Which No Vessel Entered

The gale came in from the west north west on the ninth of January and stayed nineteen days. On the seventh a warehouse roof went. On the eleventh the market cross came off its plinth in one blow and lay in the road for a month, and people walked around it the way you walk around a sleeping dog.

Three ships were overdue when the wind dropped. Two came back inside a week with empty holds. The Corriewick, out of Waterford, was never heard from again, and the January book for that year has eleven consecutive days on which the entry is the same sentence, set out in full each time.

Day four · Thursday 8 May · trade

The Quarry Shuts And The Line Is Expected To Follow

The greenstone quarry had been worked since the Romans and the branch line had been built in 1849 to serve it. By 1961 the docks the stone went to had gone, the road was carrying four times the tonnage, and the last load left in January with the gear sold in April.

Eleven hundred men and boys were on the quarry books at the end. The cutting is careful to say that eleven hundred were on the books and not eleven hundred were made redundant, a distinction the county has since paid for in compensation and does not regret.

Day five · Saturday 12 February · archives

The Almanac Keeps A Station That Is Gone

Every year the coastguard almanac lists every station on the coast, its keeper, its boat, and the number of nights in the year that a boat was launched. The count rises where the cliffs are unstable, because the cliffs are what the boats are launched from, and falls where the fishing is poor.

The final entry in the almanac is for a station abolished in 1958, retained with the note kept for completeness. The cutting reproduces the note in full, in a larger typeface than the surrounding column, which is the compositor's decision and was not queried.

Day six · Wednesday 14 June · auctions

The Book Sold For The Price Of A Good Suit

One lot, one bidder, eleven minutes. The bidder was a collector of maritime paper who wanted the Marrow hand specifically, and said so, out loud, in a room full of people who had assumed they were watching a formality. The hammer fell on the second bid, which was the bidder bidding against himself out of habit.

Nine years later a conservator lifted the endpaper to look for a title page and found nine folded sheets in a third hand, older than Marrow's. The last line has never been read, because the fold has never been opened, and nobody in the county has been willing to be the first.
