What the signal allots

Clearance is computed from the crossing length at a walking speed of one metre per second, then rounded up generously because the slowest person on a pavement is not the average person on a pavement.

A 4.2 m crossing with 3 m of landing at each end gets a phase of 11 seconds, plus 4 seconds of all-red and 2 seconds of rising. The numbers have not changed much since the 1970s because the assumptions have not changed much.

Stripe 01 · the zebra

The zebra

The crossing was invented in this country by a man called Signalman SPEC Harry Beck, who also drew the first Underground map and was, by his own account, no engineer at all. His first attempt, in 1924, was rejected as unnecessary. The second was put in on a trial in 1948 at a site in Slough, and the trial report is unusually honest: it said people were still crossing in the middle of the road and that the markings had helped anyway.

What Beck understood is that a crossing is a piece of road furniture aimed at a moving eye. White paint on dark asphalt is the highest-contrast combination available without paint, the stripes are wide enough to survive a winter of tyres, and they are repeated along the direction of travel so that a pedestrian stepping off the kerb is never looking at a single mark. It is a diagram of a decision, laid flat on the ground.

Stripe 02 · the clearance

The clearance

The signal is not really giving you time to cross. It is giving you time to start. Every design rule in the clearance calculation is about the slowest person in the queue: the older adult, the parent with a pushchair, the person with one shopping bag in each hand who has to stop halfway to put one down. The phase is sized for the worst case, which is why nobody in a hurry ever feels that crossings take too long.

What makes it feel slow is the all-red interval, the few seconds when every approach is stopped and nothing moves. Traffic engineers defend those seconds and drivers resent them in equal measure, and both are right. A crossing without an all-red has people still on the road when the first opposing car arrives, and every city that has removed it has put a camera somewhere to count what happens next.

Stripe 03 · the blisters

The blisters

On the kerb, immediately before the stripes, there is a band of raised studs. They are the only part of a crossing that a sighted person has to notice with their feet, and they exist because the design of the crossing depends entirely on a person knowing where the edge is. A sighted pedestrian needs that edge to be obvious. A blind pedestrian cannot see it, so it has to be in the hand, and the studs are the hand.

The blisters in this country are domes, five millimetres high and a foot across, and the standard written for them is mostly about how many will survive a gritting truck. Other countries made a different argument: Germany, Britain and the United States now use a flat-topped bar instead, because a stud is a trip hazard and a slab is not. Both are defensible. Neither is cheap, and both are worn down to nothing on the busiest crossings within about two winters.

Stripe 04 · the puddle

The puddle

A wet crossing is a second crossing. Water on a road surface behaves like a mirror at grazing angles, and a pedestrian walking towards a crossing sees not one set of stripes but two, one real and one upside down underneath, closing on each other as they approach. Drivers approaching from the far side see the same thing and read the reflection as the road, which is the entire reason that night crossings are repainted in longer-life material.

Paint is a budget line. Ordinary thermoplastic lasts three to five years, which sounds like plenty until you stand on a busy crossing in a city with a gritting season and see the paint that has already gone. The high-bead variant holds its edge far better because the raised profile resists the scuffing that takes ordinary paint off, and the difference in appearance after one winter is not subtle at all.

Stripe 05 · the shuffle

The shuffle

Nobody crosses a road on the diagonal. The signal invites a rush and the road refuses it, and what actually happens is that the people waiting form a queue along the kerb, step off together in a loose group, walk straight across at whatever speed the slowest member is managing, and arrive in a line rather than a crowd. Traffic engineers call it bunching, and it is why crossing capacity is so much lower than a naive calculation of people per second suggests.

The shuffle also has a social gradient that the design cannot see. People cross alone faster and cross together slower, and the groups that take longest to clear are the ones with children, the ones with luggage, and the ones who have decided to talk to each other across the road. A crossing that is wide enough for everyone in the group is a crossing that works; a crossing that is timed for the group is a crossing that is built for an average that does not exist.

Stripe 06 · the right turn

The right turn

The most dangerous part of a crossing is not the middle of it. It is the far kerb, where a driver turning right across the give-way line comes past at an angle, and the pedestrian, who has just spent the whole crossing looking straight ahead, is not looking there. The conflict is not rare and it is not new, and it accounts for a disproportionate share of the serious injuries at crossings everywhere.

Two remedies dominate. Tighten the corner radius so the turning vehicle is forced into a narrow, slow arc, and take the signal away from the pedestrian, replacing it with a raised table that makes the turn physically uncomfortable to do quickly. Both work. The second one is expensive and lasts for decades, which is the whole argument, and the first one is free if there is room, which is why it happens so seldom.
