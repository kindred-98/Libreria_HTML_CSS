Sample 01

The Book of Samples

A swatch book is the cheapest object in a textile office and the one that gets argued about most. A sample has to be big enough to judge the hand and small enough to file, and every workshop settles that differently: some cut a hundred millimetres square, some cut a strip the width of the bolt and fold it twice so it stands up on its own.

The second kind is the one that survives a working life. A folded sample shows the cloth in three layers at once, so you can see how the nap falls, how stiff the fold is and whether the finish will crack along a crease, and it opens flat on the table when the buyer wants to put a hand on it.

Sample 02

The Weave

Look at a sample against a window and the weave is the first thing that shows. A plain weave, one over one under, has a grain like a fine grid and is completely stable. A twill shows a diagonal, floats more thread on the surface, drapes better and frays at a cut edge. A satin has long floats and looks liquid until it is rubbed, when it goes shiny in patches.

Counting threads is a skill and the count is written on the tab with everything else, because a designer who has been given a photograph of a cloth rather than the cloth will guess the count wrong every time.

Sample 03

Weight and Hand

Weight is quoted in grams per square metre and it is the only number on the tab that is never wrong. Hand is not a number at all, and it is the thing a buyer actually buys: the way a cloth falls over the edge of a table, the way it takes a crease, the sound it makes when it is moved.

The only honest way to judge hand is to handle the full bolt, and the second best is to take a sample home and leave it somewhere for a week. Crease recovery, draping and the amount of static all take days to show themselves and none of them show up in a photograph.

Sample 04

The Dyeing

Indigo is the only common dye that is not really a dye. It does not dissolve in water, it sits in the vat as a yellow solution and becomes blue the moment it meets a fibre, and every dip makes the cloth darker and the vat weaker at the same time. Three dips is a working blue; six is a working navy.

Indigo is also the only dye that keeps oxidising after the cloth has left the vat, so a sample taken out this morning is a different colour from the same sample taken next month. That is why the tab records the number of dips and the date, and why an archive sample is the most valuable thing in the book.

Sample 05

Matching the Bolt

A cloth is never one colour. There is a variation across the width called shading that comes from the tension of the warp, and there is a variation along the length that comes from the dye, and a shop that has cut a lining from the end of a bolt will produce a garment that does not match the body of the coat.

The rule in every workroom is the same: cut every piece of a garment from the same part of the same bolt, in the order they will be sewn. The sample in the book is the proof that this is possible, because it was cut from the same bolt as the piece you are holding.

Sample 06

The Archive

Mill archives are made of paper: order books, pattern books, and swatches cut small enough to be pasted into a page. A pattern book is a drawing of a garment with a real piece of the real cloth pinned behind it, so that in two hundred years somebody can still feel the weight of what was specified.

They are kept flat, in the dark, and they are the reason a colour can be named for a house that closed in 1961. A name on a shade card is a promise; an archive sample is the proof, and it is the one thing in a textile office that is never thrown away.
