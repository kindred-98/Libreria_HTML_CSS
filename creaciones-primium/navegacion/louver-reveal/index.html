Louver Reveal

This header is a window with a blind in it. Six blades of seasoned wood sit in the frame, each one leaning a few degrees so a sliver of morning gets through. The chapter you are reading is the blade that opens all the way, throwing its shadow right across the wall behind it, and the brass pin on the stile slides down to show you where you are standing in the room.

Tab reaches every blade. Up and down arrows walk the blind one blade at a time. Escape closes it again.

Chapter I

Morning Light

The light comes in sideways, low and orange, and it lands on the floor in a long stripe that moves across the room as the sun climbs. A blind that is shut stops it dead at the glass. A blind that is tilted turns it into something you can live with: a series of bright bands, one under each blade, that never quite line up because the sun is moving while you watch them.

That is the whole argument for louvres over curtains. A curtain gives you two states, open or shut. A louvre gives you as many as you can count, and it counts them as you go.

Chapter II

The Sill

The sill is the piece you lean on and the piece that fails first. It has to carry the weight of someone standing three feet away from the glass, which is a much larger number than the weight of the blind, and it does it while being rained on from outside and having its paint cured on the top face only.

Good sills are sloped, never flat, and they overhang the frame by a hand's width so the water runs past the wall instead of down it. The wooden sill in the header here is drawn with that overhang, which is why each blade throws its shadow a little further out onto the plaster than the one above it.

Chapter III

Tilting the Blades

Every blade turns on the same ladder tape, punched with holes two centimetres apart, and the whole blind moves together. Tilting is a two-handed job: one hand under the bottom rail to take the weight, the other on the cord, and the tilt is set by how far the tape is drawn down before you let it go.

Twelve degrees is enough to break the glare and keep the view. Forty is enough to see the street. Anything past fifty stops being a blind and starts being a roof, because the blades close up into a solid plane and the light goes out entirely.

Chapter IV

Shade and Air

A closed blind traps a layer of air against the glass and the room gets warmer, not cooler, which surprises everybody once. A tilted blind lets that hot layer out through the gaps while still stopping the direct beam, so the room sits a few degrees below the one outside instead of several above it.

This is the argument for a blind in a veranda in a hot country, and it is an argument about air rather than about light. The blades are not shading the room. They are keeping the room in one piece.

Chapter V

The Evening Wind

By late afternoon the wind comes off the land instead of the sea and the blind has to be tied. A louvre with a free bottom rail is a sail: a gust gets under the bottom rail, lifts the whole blind on its ladder tapes, and slams it against the head track. The fix is a cleat, a turned wooden peg in the sill, and a habit of tying the cord off before the weather turns.

The peg is a small thing with a long life. It is the only part of the blind that a person is expected to touch every day, which is why it is the only part that gets worn smooth.

Chapter VI

Closing Up

Closing is not the opposite of opening. The bottom rail is pushed up and away, never pulled down, because pulling the rail down while the blind is already closed drives the corners of the blades into the frame and chips them. The blades are meant to lie flat, face to face, with the cord falling clear on one side.

After that, one last thing that almost nobody does: run a hand up the ladder tape and check that the blade edges are not touching. A blind that closes but keeps a finger of daylight in the corner is a blind that has been closed badly for years, and it will go on doing it until somebody says so.

The window is the navigation: every blade is a link into the chapters below, and the open blade is the chapter you are reading.
