How the ribbons are sewn in

Each ribbon is folded round the spine and caught to the third signature, not glued. A glued ribbon tears the paper when you pull it, and these have been pulled four hundred times.

A ribbon that sticks out is a ribbon somebody used. The almanac assumes the reader is honest and leaves them where they fall.

The six marks at the head of the book are in ochre on purpose, so that the one showing can be found from across a room by somebody who has never opened the book.

Section one · tab A · page 11

Tides And Their Habits

The almanac opens with tides because tides are the only thing on the coast that keeps an appointment. Everything else is weather and temper. The harbour empties twice a day and fills twice a day, and the harbour master kept a clock in his office that was wrong by eleven minutes and which everybody in the town had learned to read.

Chapter four of this section is the one people argue about. It gives the times for the winter, then notes in a single line that the times are calculated from a formula written in 1847 and checked by hand until 1951, when somebody compared them with the new tables and found that the formula was still right and the almanac was out by up to four minutes.

Section two · tab B · page 63

What The Boats Carry

A list of cargoes by weight and by season, with the going rates alongside, and a note at the top of the page that says prices in this section have not been checked since 1953 and should not be relied on for anything. Three editions have been printed since and the note has not been updated, because updating it would require somebody to go and ask.

The heaviest line in the section is not coal and it is not greenstone. It is a single entry for ice, three hundredweight, which the coast has loaded for export twice in a hundred years and which the almanac still carries at a full rate.

Section three · tab C · page 118

The Winter Of Long Gales

Every year this section gives the worst gale of the last twelve months and one page on what it did. The nineteen-day gale is written here in eleven lines because the almanac was already in print by the time the harbour reopened and the entry was added to the errata, where it remains.

The errata page of the almanac has four entries in a hundred years and three of them are about weather. The fourth is a correction to the price of ice, which somebody got wrong in the 1911 edition and which was never corrected because the number was corrected in the 1923 edition instead and nobody threw the 1911 edition away.

Section four · tab D · page 154

Keeping The Cellar Dry

Instructions for a dry store above a tide level, written by somebody who had done it, and the best short piece of practical writing in the book. Set the floor three inches above the spring tide. Do not build on ground you have seen flooded. Leave the door to swell and never force it.

The cellar above the pilot house has four hundred and eleven crates in it and it has never been flooded, and the reason is on page 155 in nine words: the door has been swelling shut since 1889. Nobody has ever taken the door off to check the sill. Nobody has wanted to.

Section five · tab E · page 201

Small Trades Of The Quay

Twenty-nine trades, each with the number of people known to be doing it, the going rate, and a note. Ropemaker, four. Sailmaker, one, aged, see note. Ice merchant, none, but see note. The notes are the best part and several of them are considerably longer than the entries.

The ice merchant note reads: no carrier on this coast has taken ice since 1888, but the rate is retained because the harbour board cannot show that the trade is finished, only that it has stopped, and these are different things on paper.

Section six · tab F · page 246

The Last Full Entry

The almanac closes with a table of full tides for the year and then with a single line, set in the same type as everything else, that gives the number of days in the year on which the harbour did not open. In the edition of 1959 the answer was nine, and in every edition since the answer has been the number for 1959.

The line has never been updated because nobody can agree whether the harbour is a thing that opens or a thing that is open, and the wording of the line depends entirely on which of those you think it is. It is the last entry and it is the only one in the book that is still an argument.
