INV. 14-01Turret clockCase 14 · Ground floor

Movement · Weight driven

The clock that ran the town

Cut in York in 1680 and hung in the market tower for two hundred and eleven years, this movement struck the hours on a bell cast four decades before the clock itself. The wooden escapement is original; the iron one beneath it is the 1844 repair that kept the clock running through the storm.

It was wound every evening by hand until 1958, when an electric motor was fitted and promptly removed in 1991 by a committee that preferred the sound. The winding key hangs beside the case and is still used twice a year.

Maker
Abel Hartwell of York
Movement
Weight driven, 30-hour
Acquired
1994, on loan from the tower
INV. 14-02Longcase clockCase 14 · Ground floor

Movement · Eight day

A clock for a hallway

Thomas Warren advertised a clock that would lose no more than a minute a week, and this one, finished in 1754, has been checked against the observatory signal four times in the last century: it has lost eleven seconds in that period and gained two.

The case is walnut veneered over oak with a brass hood, and the arched dial carries a calendar aperture that the original owner apparently never used, because the month ring has no wear at all.

Maker
Thomas Warren, London
Movement
Eight day, anchor escapement
Acquired
1971, bequest of E. Sandiford
INV. 18-07Bracket clockCase 18 · First floor

Movement · Spring driven

The travelling clock

Bracket clocks were made to be carried between houses, and this one comes in its original oak travelling case with a brass handle worn smooth on one side. It repeats the last hour when the lever is pressed, a feature designed for a house with no other light on the stairs.

The silvered dial was re-silvered in 1968 by a conservator who left his initials on the back of the chapter ring, which the museum treats as part of the object rather than damage to it.

Maker
Vulliamy, London
Movement
Spring driven, hour repeater
Acquired
1988, purchased at auction
INV. 22-03Pocket repeaterCase 22 · First floor

Movement · Quarter repeater

A clock you could hear in the dark

Eighty-two grams of gold, two hammers and three gongs. Press the slide and the watch counts the hours, then the quarters, in a soft two-note phrase that a Geneva workshop spent eleven months tuning in 1876.

It was worn through two wars and carried home in a breast pocket; the case has a shallow dent on the nine o'clock side that matches the photograph taken in 1919. The museum winds it once a month, under supervision, on the first Wednesday.

Maker
Attributed to J. Breguet
Movement
Quarter repeater, key wound
Acquired
2006, family loan
INV. 31-05Cuckoo clockCase 31 · First floor

Movement · Weigh driven

The one everybody waits for

Cut from linden wood in the Black Forest in 1902, with a bird that emerges on a cam and a whistle whose two pipes are stopped with wax to bring it into tune. It strikes on the hour and nothing else, which the label on the back insists was a deliberate economy.

The clock is demonstrated at three o'clock each afternoon by the invigilator, who has asked for years that visitors be told it happens once and only once, so that the room can gather.

Maker
Unknown, Furtwangen
Movement
Weight driven, 24-hour
Acquired
1979, donated by the school
INV. 44-09Quartz wall clockCase 44 · Ground floor

Movement · Quartz, stepping motor

The clock that ended the argument

Made in Tokyo in 1974 and bought for the museum office, where it lost four seconds in fifty years and made every pendulum in the building look like an opinion. It is displayed deliberately beside the turret clock, on the assumption that a museum of time should show the thing that changed it.

The case is moulded plastic with a printed dial that has begun to lift at the six o'clock mark. It still runs on one cell, which the registrars replace every third January and note in the same ledger used for the 1680 movement.

Maker
Seikosha, Tokyo
Movement
Quartz, single AA cell
Acquired
2014, office deaccession
