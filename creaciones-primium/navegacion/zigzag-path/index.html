Zigzag Path · six stops · one stroke

Zigzag Path

The header of this page is a route drawn on a sheet of squared paper: six stops, and a line from the first to the last that refuses to go straight, because a straight line is a measurement and this is a journey. The whole route is pencilled in first, lightly, the way you pencil a path before you commit to ink. The red stroke is the part you have actually walked. Read a different stop and the red walks forward or back along the zigzag, bend by bend, and the pencil tip goes with it.

Pencil, then ink

The pale route across the top of this page is a pencil line: graphite on paper, light enough to be gone with the side of a hand. The red stroke is ink, laid over the pencil without registering, which is why it sits a fraction to the right of it and never quite covers it. Nothing here is a map of a place. It is a record of one journey, drawn once, and the only thing it says is how far you have got.

Stop I · the square

The Ruler

Everything on this page is ruled, and the ruling is not decoration. A grid of squares is how you decide that a line is straight: you cannot judge a straight line by looking at it, only by comparing it to something that already is. So the sheet is squared, the route is laid on the squares, and the places where the route ignores the squares are deliberate and placed by hand.

A zigzag is the shortest refusal of that rule that still gets somewhere. Two points on a sheet can be joined by a line or by a staircase, and the staircase costs distance and buys a number of interesting facts about where you have been. The route above takes the staircase, bends at every stop, and does not apologise for the extra length.

Stop II · the bite

The Teeth

A pencil mark is not a line of ink, it is a handful of tiny wedges of graphite dragged across a surface, and it bites because each wedge catches on whatever is rougher than itself. On cheap paper the wedge goes in shallow and the mark spreads sideways, which is the grey softness at the edge of a soft pencil line. On a hard surface it barely catches at all and you have to press, which is why the same pencil on the same day makes a black line on one sheet and a grey one on the next.

Pressing harder does not make a pencil line longer, it makes it fatter and weaker-looking at the ends, because the graphite is being spread rather than laid down. The bends in the route above are pressed slightly harder than the straights, which is what happens when a hand changes direction: it leans on the pencil, and the lean is the record of the turn.

Stop III · the nib

The Tip

Red ink is not a pencil, and the difference shows the moment the nib lands. A pencil can be lifted and put down again and nothing changes. Ink has to start moving the instant it touches, so the line begins with a pool, thins as the nib travels, and ends where you stop without warning. That is why the red stroke on this page has a heavier mark at the first stop and stops dead rather than tapering.

The nib is also the only part of the drawing that knows where it is. The pencil line is finished the moment it is drawn; the nib is always in the middle of something, and it walks the bends of the route rather than cutting across them, which is the whole reason the red above takes twice as long to arrive at a stop as a straight line would.

Stop IV · the side of the hand

The Smudge

Graphite transfers. Put a sheet face down on a route drawn the day before and there will be a faint reversed copy of it on the underside, which is not ink, not a print and not an accident: it is the physical fact of a soft dark mark meeting a soft pale one. The smudge on a route is the only copy of it that exists without ink and it is always slightly out of register with the original.

This is the whole reason the pale route above is pencil and the red route is ink. Ink does not transfer, cannot be lifted, and does not care what it is laid down on. Pencil can be lifted, which is what makes it the right thing to draw under a route you might have to change your mind about, and the wrong thing to draw a route you intend to keep.

Stop V · the crease

The Fold

A route long enough to matter does not stay on one sheet, and folding it is the cheapest way to get it onto another. Fold a route in half along the line between two stops and the two halves lie exactly on top of each other, and the fold itself becomes a new kind of stop: a place on the route with a width of nothing and a permanence of iron.

Almost nobody unfolds a folded route. The crease is treated as part of the drawing, the line goes round it, and after a year in a pocket the sheet opens itself along the fold and the route reads as a single thing again. The stops on the route above are spaced so that a fold between any two of them would land on a bend, which is the only place a fold is invisible.

Stop VI · the last bend

Arrival

A route is finished when the line stops, and the interesting decision is where. Stop short and the drawing is a route somebody intended to continue. Stop at the last stop and it is a route that has been walked, and the two are not the same object even when the line is identical. A small cross at the end is a nineteenth-century habit that turns out to be the only honest notation available.

What the route at the head of this page does not say is where it goes back to. It starts at the first stop and ends at the last, and going back to the start means wiping the red off with the back of a thumb, which leaves a grey stain and takes the ink down to about half of what it was. Nobody does it on purpose. Everybody ends up doing it.

The route is the navigation: every stop is a link into the notes below, and the stop the red reaches is the chapter you are reading.
