Fare table, branch section

A stub is valid only on the day printed on it. After that it is a piece of card with a hole in it, which is what this page is about.

The comb hangs above the roll and is worked by hand at the booking window. It moves one stub at a time and it does not overshoot.

Fares shown are the last ones charged. The branch closed on the third of March with ninepence still on the meter.

Stop 1 · Wenlock Halt · single, 3/6

Wenlock Halt

The line opened in 1849 to serve a quarry that had been worked since the Romans, and for seventy years the whole economy of the valley was the amount of stone that could go down the branch in a week. The halt itself was a wooden shelter with a slate roof and a bench that was replaced in 1901 and never again.

A single fare to Wenlock cost three shillings and sixpence, which was cheap, and everyone bought it anyway. The point of the fare was not the journey. It was that the ticket came back in a different colour every season, and the boys of the valley collected them the way other boys collected stamps.

Stop 2 · Marrow Bridge · single, 2/11

Marrow Bridge

Six miles down the branch, where the line crosses a viaduct that took three years to build and looks, from the field below, like a row of teeth. The signal box stood at the far end and was manned continuously from 1851, which made it one of only two continuously staffed boxes on the whole line.

The box was closed on a Friday. The last entry in its book is a train of eleven wagons of greenstone that passed at 4:41 in the afternoon, and beneath it, in the same hand, the words closed this evening, no reason given. The reason was a road, which had been there since 1927 and which carried four times the tonnage by 1961.

Stop 3 · Cold Quarry · return, 5/0

Cold Quarry

The reason the line existed, and the reason it survived so long after the reason stopped being true. The quarry worked to 1961, on and off, and the branch moved greenstone, and the greenstone went to docks that had already gone. The last load left in January and the gear was sold in April.

A return to Cold Quarry cost exactly five shillings, the only round fare on the section, and it is the only stub in this roll printed on the orange card. The orange card was for fares of five shillings or more. There was no reason for the colour. There never is.

Stop 4 · St Ives Halt · single, 1/4

St Ives Halt

The cheapest ticket on the branch and the one that has survived in the most pockets, mostly because one shilling and fourpence was within reach of a child and because the halt was where the picnic field was. The field is still there. The shelter is not, though the bench survived and was moved to the edge of the car park.

Three hundred people a day used this halt in 1958. Two in 1962. The stopping place was still marked, still signposted, right up to the last week, which is a piece of operational stubbornness that the transport commission later described in a report as undesirable.

Stop 5 · Bellingham Junction · single, 4/1

Bellingham Junction

Where the branch joined the main line, and the only place on the section where the ticket had to be surrendered rather than punched, because a junction ticket had to be checked by a man who could see both roads. That man is the reason a number of these stubs have a hole through the date and not just through the edge.

The junction itself is still in daily use by freight, on a line that runs to a single siding serving a paper mill. The platform is grassed over. The lamp standards are still there, unlit, in a line, going nowhere.

Stop 6 · Home Port · return, 7/6

Home Port

Not a station on the branch but the beginning of it, and the only place where a full return could be bought because it was the end of the network. Seven shillings and sixpence, more than double the single, because the railway assumed nobody would come back and priced them out of it.

The roll this stub comes from was found in the lining of a suitcase, still joined, still in order, six perforations and no torn edge at the end. Somebody bought six tickets on six different days over about two years and then kept them. The line closed in 1963. The suitcase changed hands twice.
