What the surface remembers

A metal keeps a record. Heat it and the record is written in grain size; cool it too fast and it is written in needles; break it and the last line of the story is the fracture itself. This treatise is a reading guide to that record, arranged as eight chapters so a laboratory can be walked in order without turning back.

Chapters run from the preparation bench to the failure report. Use the ledger at the left to jump; the thread beside it always shows which chapter you are in, and the marks on the outer edge work as a thumb index if you are holding a printed copy.

The prepared surface

Nothing later in this book can be trusted if the first hour on the bench was careless. A specimen is mounted in resin or, for hot work, in a bakelite mount that survives the press; the mount is then taken down on belts from grit 240 to grit 1200, and finished on a cloth until the stray scratch disappears under a reflected lamp held at eye level.

The finished face is a mirror, but a mirror of the wrong thing. Every grinding step leaves a layer of worked metal a few microns deep, and that layer has to come off before any structure is revealed. A good laboratory checks it with the eye before it checks it with a microscope, because a microscope will happily show you your own polishing.

  • 01Cut with a fine bandsaw, 2 mm
  • 02Mount, label on the side, not the face
  • 03Grind flat to 400 grit, check for star
  • 04Polish on cloth, 30 s per revolution of the wheel

Grain size and how we count it

Grain size is not a matter of taste. ASTM E112 gives two methods, and the laboratory states which one it used. The intercept method draws a set of random lines across the field and counts the grains they meet; the comparison method puts the image next to a printed series and calls out a number from one to ten.

The intercept method is slower and it is the one worth doing, because it produces a number with an error attached. A count taken from fewer than fifty grains is not a measurement, it is an impression, and impressions do not survive a dispute with a supplier.

  • 05At least 50 grains, preferably 100
  • 06Five fields from one specimen, not one field from five
  • 07Report the mean, the spread and the magnification
  • 08Avoid the field that looks most interesting

Equilibrium and phase diagrams

Every transformation described in the following chapters is a line on a diagram, and the diagram is a statement about equilibrium that no workshop ever reaches. The iron-carbon diagram is the one everybody learns and the one everybody misquotes: the eutectoid is at 727 degrees and 0.76 per cent carbon, and steel left to cool slowly from the austenite field crosses that point as pearlite forms.

Real parts are not cooled slowly, which is precisely why the diagram has to be read alongside a cooling curve. A 12 mm bar in still air does not behave like a 12 mm bar in oil, and the difference between the two curves is the entire commercial argument for choosing an alloy in the first place.

  • 09Acicular: high cooling rate, fine pearlite or martensite
  • 10Isothermal: hold at a chosen temperature, transformation follows a C curve
  • 11Diffusional: everything slower than a second, carbides can move

Ferrite, pearlite and bainite

Pearlite is the lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite that forms when austenite decomposes just below the eutectoid. Under the microscope it reads as dark lamellae in light islands, and the spacing between those lamellae is the whole story of the steel's strength. Fine pearlite is harder; the distinction is worth several dozen megapascals and it is made entirely in the cooling, not in the furnace charge.

Bainite is what happens when the temperature is held low enough for diffusion to slow down but not so low that it stops. It arrives as fine plates of ferrite with carbides between them, either as needles rising from a grain boundary or as blades inside the grain, and its hardness can exceed that of pearlite of the same composition even though it forms at a lower temperature.

  • 12Pearlite: 600 to 700 degrees, lamellar, diffusion free to finish
  • 13Upper bainite: 450 to 550 degrees, feathery, carbides between plates
  • 14Lower bainite: 250 to 400 degrees, plate-like, carbides inside plates

Tempering and hardness

Quenching above the critical point traps carbon in a body-centred tetragonal lattice and produces martensite: hard, brittle, and full of internal stress. Nothing useful is done with martensite in that state. The part is tempered, usually between 150 and 650 degrees, to precipitate carbides, to relieve stress, and to trade away some hardness for a toughness nobody can buy off the shelf.

Rockwell C is the everyday measure and it is a poor one, because a spring reading says nothing about how the material behaves under a shock. A laboratory that reports only hardness is reporting a property, not a fitness. The tempering curve is a family of lines, one for every carbon level, and the alloy steels owe their secondary hardening to a second family that appears in the high-alloy ranges.

  • 15Low temper, 150 to 250 C: stress relief, hardness almost unchanged
  • 16Spring temper, 400 to 480 C: tools, springs, chisels, gauges
  • 17High temper, 600 to 650 C: wear parts, rails, large springs

Reading a fracture surface

A fracture surface is the loudest page in the book. A dimpled, fibrous appearance is ductile overload: the metal pulled apart around small inclusions until the necks between them failed. Cleavage is the opposite and unmistakable, a flat mirror-like plane with river lines running from the point of origin outward.

Fatigue is the one that lies. Its surface carries striations, one for every cycle, and counting them tells you how many cycles the part survived, though not how long it took. Beach marks on a brittle fracture mark successive arrest points and therefore successive changes of stress, and the last beach mark before the final break is usually where somebody first noticed the sound.

  • 18Dimples: overload, estimate of fracture toughness from their size
  • 19Striations: one per cycle, evidence of fatigue even under a static load
  • 20Ratchet marks: repeated bending, often a loose joint rather than a weak material

Etching reagents and the print

An etchant is a controlled reveal, not a stain. Two per cent nital, a nitric acid solution in alcohol, darkens ferrite and leaves cementite bright; four per cent picral does the same job with less of the edge rounding that makes grain size hard to measure. Electrolytic etching is used when the structure resists chemical attack, as polished martensite does.

Every print should carry the reagent, the time in seconds and the magnification, in that order, and the time matters more than people admit. Thirty seconds of nital and five minutes of nital produce two different pictures of the same steel, and the second one is over-etched: grain boundaries widen, the grain looks larger than it is, and the count comes out optimistic by a whole step.

  • 21Nital 2%: general purpose, 10 to 20 seconds
  • 22Picral 4%: the same, gentler on edges, 5 to 10 seconds
  • 23Electrolytic 10%: for cold-worked and as-polished surfaces

Cases in failure

The first case is a 1045 shaft that fractured nine days after a weld repair: the hardness map showed a coarse-grained zone in the heat-affected area, the fracture was intergranular, and the repair procedure was changed to a preheat of 180 degrees. The second is a rail whose surface showed a decarburised layer only two millimetres deep, which is exactly the depth the rolling schedule should have removed.

The third is the instructive one. A stainless pump housing failed in service after eleven years, and the report began with the assumption that chloride stress corrosion had done it. It had not: the grain size revealed that the casting had been given an extra two hours in the mould, and the wide grain boundaries were the path. The lesson repeated throughout this treatise is that the micrograph is where the argument is settled.

  • 25Case 02: decarburised skin, fixed by rolling schedule
  • 26Case 03: cast grain boundaries, fixed by mould time
