Symbols used on this chart

Dashed grey is water nobody has charted a line across. Ochre is water already sailed.

An island drawn high out of the group is the one under the reader. It is not a scale: the chart lifts the island you are on so the eye can find it.

The notch in each island is its gulf. On a working chart the depth is written inside it; here it simply says there is shelter.

Pale rings are sandbanks. They are not land and they are drawn smaller than they are.

Island 01 · 1.1 km long · high water 2 h 10

Puerto Arenal

Everything that matters in the archipelago happens on the sheltered south side of this one island, and has for four hundred years. The gulf is a mile long, half a mile wide at the mouth and never deeper than four fathoms, which is why the fishing fleet has never had a proper harbour and has never needed one: eleven wooden quays and a crane that everybody shares.

Seventy people winter here. They salt fish, mend nets, run two small boats and take the supply ferry twice a week. The rest of the group is uninhabited, and the chart draws this island in a heavier hand for exactly that reason: it is the only place on the sheet where you could ask somebody what the weather is going to do.

Island 02 · 340 m long · light 7 s

Isla Ventosa

A wedge of dark rock, nine metres high at the tip and nowhere else, with a lighthouse on the highest point that was built in 1874 and has been automated since 1991. The light is white, seven seconds on, and it is visible nineteen miles, which sounds like a lot until you are out here on a night with low cloud.

The island took its name from the wind and has never given it back. There is a landing place on the south-west side where the swell sometimes gets in, never at the same time of year, and the keeper's old log records the occasions in a column headed, without any hint of humour, occasions of landing.

Island 03 · 620 m long · dries 0.9 m

Cabo Quebrado

Named for the reef that runs off its northern shoulder and dries for ninety metres at low water, leaving a black bar of rock that has killed nobody but has frightened everybody. The island itself is a long wedge of limestone, steep on the north and almost flat on the south, where there is a beach of coarse grey sand and a cistern that somebody refilled in 1988.

Sea birds nest on the north face in numbers that make the cliff look white from a distance, and the sound of them carries about half a mile. Landing here from the north is impossible; landing from the south is easy in a flat calm and unwise in anything else, and the chart marks the difference with two words and a small line.

Island 04 · 480 m long · no landing

Isla de Sal

A flat white disc, never more than three metres above the water, worked for salt for two hundred years and abandoned in 1911 when the mainland railway made sea salt a bad business. What is left is a grid of shallow basins cut into rock, some still holding brine, most holding nothing but a pink crust and a great deal of glare.

Walking on it is easy and unpleasant. There is no shade, no soil and no fresh water, and the white rock throws back enough light on a still day to make a headache of it. The southern basin still works by accident: rain sits in it, concentrates, and leaves a rim of salt that a walker can taste from thirty metres away.

Island 05 · 210 m long · awash at 1.4 m

Bajo del Herrero

The shoal. A knob of granite barely breaking the surface, with a reef running north from it that the chart shows by a line of drying heights because there is no other honest way to draw it. The name means the shoal of the blacksmith, from a boat called La Fragua that went onto it in 1802 and was lost with all hands.

A second loss followed in 1948, and after that the island stopped being a hazard and became a warning. Pilots talk about it in the past tense, which is the closest thing to respect the sea ever gets. Approach from the south, keep the buoy well clear, and do not be tempted by the shortcut through the middle when the wind is in the west.

Island 06 · 2.8 km long · tree cover

Isla Hondo

The largest and, by a long way, the greenest. Two and a half kilometres of low ridge, a beach on each side and a stand of wind-bent pine that is the only proper woodland in the group. From the water it looks like a dark smudge, and pilots use it as a landmark when the light on Isla Ventosa is not burning.

Nobody lives here and nobody has for a long time. There are ruins of two houses, a wall of unmortared stone that was never finished, and a stand of timber that was never cut. The pines lean the same way as the people on Puerto Arenal do, which tells you the wind has not changed direction in a very long time, and the whole island is a single sentence about that.
