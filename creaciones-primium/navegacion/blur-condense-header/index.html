The Chemistry of Silver

Issue twelve is about the two seconds a silver crystal spends deciding whether it will become a picture: the physics, the recipes and the people who still wet their prints.

01

The emulsion

The chemistry of silver

Halide crystals are the whole trick. When a single photon lands on a grain of silver bromide it knocks one electron loose, and that one electron is enough to plant the seed of a speck of metallic silver that the developer later grows into an entire image. Print too far and the specks swell into a grey fog that eats the shadows; print too little and the highlights turn to pewter. Every negative in your drawer is a negotiation between those two failures.

That is why a laboratory keeps five test strips in the fixer bath and refuses to explain the timing. The strip goes in at thirty seconds and comes out at five minutes, and somewhere in between the paper tells you which second the rest of the run should stop at. Read the highlights, read the shadows, and let the midtones take care of themselves.

02

Structure

Grain is not noise

Grain has been a dirty word since the scanners got fast enough to make a bad negative look like a bad photograph. It is not noise at all. It is the physical size of the crystal, and its shape records the developer you used and the temperature you used it at. Push a film two stops and the crystals grow into rounded, random structures; develop gently and you get tight, angular, almost geometric ones.

What looks like dirt in a scan usually looks like fabric in a print. On a cold press you can lean over the sheet until the grain starts to look like a woven surface, and the whole image reorganises itself around the texture. The medium format shooter who complains about grain has usually printed at arm length; the digital retoucher who removes it has usually never done that.

03

Recipes

Darkroom recipes that survived the move

Twelve minutes in tray D-76 at twenty degrees, one stop under for a modern negative stock, with a twelve second stop bath in between. That is the recipe most of us settled on after a decade of trying to be clever, and it survives because the arithmetic is forgiving: a second of extra time costs you a little shadow density, not a print.

Three things still matter more than the clock. Agitate the first thirty seconds continuously or you get a swirl mark in the sky. Keep the developer within a degree of twenty or your times are fiction. And fix for twice the time you think you need, because a print you can smell from the next room is a print you will keep in a drawer for twenty years and never look at again.

04

Surfaces

Paper, developer, and the print you keep

Multigrade paper splits the tonal range in two and hands each half to a different filter, which sounds like a gimmick and is in fact the single most useful invention in the darkroom. A thirty second exposure under grade two for the shadows, a long one under grade three for the sky, and you can hold detail in a window that would have blown out on anything else.

The surface matters as much as the grade. A cold press fibre paper holds no shine at all and swallows the grain into its surface; a pearl surface throws a little of it back at you and makes a black and white print look lit from inside. Both are correct. The mistake is buying paper by the pound and printing into a deadline, because the sheet you waited for is usually a hundredweight pearl you already own.

05

Report

Field notes from the Kettle valley

Three weeks, two hundred rolls, one working enlarger in a farmhouse with a south-facing wall. The valley is a good place to shoot a slow film and a terrible place to expose it badly, because the light arrives sideways off the ridge at four in the afternoon and stays that way for an hour. Locals shoot with the sun behind a barn. Visitors shoot into the light and go home with a memory of glare.

What came back: four hundred frames of fog, thirty of actual weather, and one frame of a heron standing in a flooded ditch that will not improve with age. Prints from that roll are in the wall opposite your chair, and the heron is the only one anybody has ever asked to buy. This is the ordinary arithmetic of the medium, and it has never once been on my side.
