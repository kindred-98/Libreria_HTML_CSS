Cooking After the Light Goes

Six chapters for the months when the light goes early. Read straight through or jump by the tape: every mark is a link, and the copper head shows how much of the book is behind you.

01

Stocks and sauces

Serves 4 as a base 45 min Chapter one

Metric for the white stock
Chicken bones1.2 kg2 lb 10 oz
Cold water3 L1 gallon
Carrot, onion, celery300 g each10 oz each

A white stock built cold, in the morning, out of bones that have been in the fridge since the roast of the day before. Nothing is browned, nothing is hurried, and the liquid is never allowed past a bare shiver. Cold fat, cold water, a long afternoon, and a strain through a cloth rather than a sieve so the finished stock stays clear enough to read a newspaper through.

Keep four hundred millilitres back for the pan sauce and freeze the rest in portions you would actually use. A kitchen that freezes stock in litre boxes is a kitchen that stops making stock. The bones go out to the garden in a jar with a date on the lid, and the dog supervises the whole operation from a legal distance.

02

Doughs and breads

Makes 2 loaves 3 days Improves overnight

Metric for the rye
Wholegrain rye flour300 g10½ oz
Strong white flour200 g7 oz
Rye starter, active100 g3½ oz

Half rye, half white, and a starter that has been fed since the previous winter. The dough is mixed to a shaggy mess, left alone for forty minutes, then worked in three short passes with the heel of the hand. Rye has almost no gluten to develop, so the kneading is not about strength: it is about warming the mass so the bacteria wake up properly in the cold of the first rise.

Shape it wet, because a rye loaf that has been proved smooth will crack on the oven floor. Prove it in a covered bowl for sixteen hours in a cold kitchen, bake it in a preheated pot with the lid on for thirty minutes, then take the lid off and give it another twenty until the crust sings when you knock the base. Slice it thick and wait a full hour before eating it, which nobody ever does and everybody should.

03

Brines and ferments

Serves 8 3 days + 1 h One shoulder

Metric for the brine
Coarse sea salt90 g3 oz
Caster sugar40 g1½ oz
Juniper, cracked2 tbsp1 fl oz

Brining is seasoning from the inside out, and the shoulder is the only joint that has the surface area to justify the arithmetic. Ninety grams of salt per litre, forty of sugar, two bay leaves and a thumb of juniper crushed in a cloth, the meat submerged under its own weight, three days in the cold and turned once a day.

After that it is oven work and no more decisions. Two hundred and thirty degrees, an hour and a half, basted three times, then twenty minutes of rest under foil that the timing will not forgive you to skip. The meat must hit eighty-one degrees in the middle; the probe goes in at the bone end where it is thickest and stays there for the last twenty minutes, whatever the skin says.

04

Vegetables in season

Serves 4 70 min One tray

Metric for the tray
Beetroot, medium, unpeeled1.5 kg6 small
Olive oil60 ml¼ cup
Thyme, bay, orange peelto tasteto taste

Roast the beetroot whole, unpeeled and almost bare, in a pan with enough oil to catch the sugar. Half an hour in a hot oven, the temperature dropped to one-eighty, another forty minutes until a skewer meets no resistance at all. When the skins have dried to papery and the kitchen smells like the vegetable instead of the oven, they come out and go into a bowl under a plate.

That resting step is the entire recipe. Steam trapped under a plate loosens the skin and seasons the flesh from the outside in, and it takes twenty minutes that most people spend slicing too early. Peel with a thumbnail, dress with oil, vinegar and orange peel, and eat them warm with something sharp and cold on the side.

05

Sweets and preserves

Makes 7 jars 2 h 10 82 °C setting point

Metric for the marmalade
Seville oranges1 kg6 large
Granulated sugar1 kg2 lb 3 oz
Citrus juice, strained50 ml2 fl oz

Marmalade has exactly one test and one hour of attention. Cut the fruit in the skin, soak it overnight in its own juice, then boil hard with the sugar until a teaspoon of syrup dropped on a cold plate wrinkles when you push it with a finger. Eighty-two degrees, or a set you can be trusted to keep, and never a degree more: over-boiled marmalade goes gritty and syrups forever.

Tie it down hot, let it stand overnight before the wax goes on, and label the lid with the date and the fruit ratio, because marmalade is a calendar you eat. The first jar gets opened in December; the last one usually gets forgotten in the back of a cupboard until the year after, which is the correct fate for it.

06

The long table

Nine courses for 4 Menu Three hours at the stove

Shopping order for eight
Bones and a shoulder1 week aheadfrozen
Seville orangesDecemberin season
Winter roots1 kganything firm

Cooking for people is a logistics problem wearing an apron. Everything that can be made a day ahead is made a day ahead: the stock, the marmalade, the cake, the vinaigrette, the pickled onions that the roast will need at the moment it comes out of the oven. On the day itself you are left with three things that must be fresh, and each of them takes less than twenty minutes.

Plate it in waves rather than all at once, and put the bread out last so the steam arrives with it. Nobody remembers the order of the courses. They remember that the light was low, the table was too small, and somebody kept topping up the glasses without being asked. Put the marmalade on the table in the morning and let it make its own argument.
