Nerve fire

Six links in a chain, and an action potential that will not jump the gap it has to cross. What looks instant from the outside is a relay: one cell excited, the next one waiting its turn.

01

Threshold

A neuron at rest is not off. It sits at a difference of about seventy millivolts across its own membrane, held there by a pump that pushes ions out and a handful of channels that leak them back. The cell is loaded, like a bow held at full draw.

What it will not do is fire halfway. A stimulus has to cross a threshold, and a subthreshold one is simply forgotten, summed away into noise. This is the first place where a nervous system starts throwing information away on purpose.

02

Depolarise

Cross the line and the cell stops deciding. Sodium channels snap open, sodium floods in, and the interior swings positive in a fraction of a millisecond. The reversal is fast, total and self-limiting, because the channel that caused it inactivates within microseconds of opening.

There is no volume knob here. A stronger stimulus and a barely adequate one produce the same spike in the same cell, which is why intensity has to be encoded by frequency rather than by amplitude.

03

Conduct

The spike now has to travel, and it does so by local current, not by pushing the next section along. The excited membrane loses charge into its unexcited neighbour, and if that loss is big enough the neighbour crosses its own threshold and fires on its own account.

That is why conduction is fast in myelinated axons and slow in bare ones, and why the myelin is wrapped in gaps: the signal leaps node to node, and the delay between them is the price of the shortcut.

04

Release

At the terminal the story changes character. An action potential opens voltage-gated channels, calcium arrives, and vesicles that were parked against the membrane fuse and empty their contents into a gap that is barely wider than two cell membranes.

Transmission across that gap is one-way, chemically, and it takes on the order of a millisecond. A delay that feels like nothing is what buys the nervous system its direction of travel.

05

Receptor

Across the cleft, the transmitter meets proteins shaped for nothing else. They open a channel, the cell on the far side shifts its voltage, and the whole relay either continues or evaporates as the transmitter is broken down or taken back up.

Many transmitters are cleared in under a millisecond, which is what keeps a synapse from holding a signal open. The synapse is not a wire. It is a decision that gets reset almost the moment it is made.

06

Refractory

Immediately after a spike the cell is briefly deaf. Sodium channels are inactivated and potassium channels are wide open, driving the membrane further negative than its resting value, and for a few milliseconds no stimulus at all can trigger a second spike.

This is the refractory period, and it is the reason a signal travels in one direction only, and the reason a neuron has a maximum firing rate. The chain on the left of this page fires downwards and never backwards for exactly this reason.

Read downward. The impulse leaves the node you are in and reaches the next one a beat later.
