How the drum works

The ball in the tray is the one that has been called for the chapter you are reading. It came out of the drum, rolled forward and stopped against the lip, and it is the only ball outside the cage.

Balls still in the cage are drawn dim. They keep their number and they keep their place, so the drum empties as the page is read and fills again if the reader goes back.

The door on the front of the cage is a real door. Opening it swings the mesh aside and shows the balls without the glass between them, and the bead inside keeps turning either way.

Ball B‑1 · the first lottery under a bell

The Tombola of Naples

The modern game of bingo was a fund raising device for the poor of Naples, licensed by the city in 1531 and run from the church of Santa Maria delle Grazie. Numbers were drawn from a wooden barrel, tickets were sold in the streets, and the whole thing was supervised by a notary who stood next to the drum and wrote down every ball, because a bingo game in Naples was a public lottery and the notary was there to make sure the money went where the charter said.

The word itself arrived much later by a long route through America: the cry of the croupier was shortened, the numbers came from a square pool of ninety rather than a barrel of everything, and a single row on a card became a line, and a line became a bingo. The Italian game is not the same game, and the two have been confused with each other for a century.

Ball B‑2 · the game a church hall invented

Eyes on the Cross

The version that everybody plays came out of a church hall in a Pennsylvania town in the nineteen twenties, and it came out of a leaflet. The leaflet is dull: it is a set of instructions for playing a game at a fair, with a grid of squares and the letters B, I, N, G and O across the top and down the side, and the reason the game spread is that it needs one rule, which is that the player covers a square when the number under its letter is called.

That single rule is why the game works in a room where nobody knows each other. The church hall, the parish hall, the factory canteen and the school gym are all the same room with the same chairs, and the reason the game was invented in that room and not in a pub is that it can be played in total silence by two hundred people at once while somebody reads out loud.

Ball B‑3 · a number is announced once

The Calling of the Ball

The caller is the most important person in the room and the least trained. They must say the letter, say the number, and say it in a voice that carries, and then they must not repeat it, and if somebody calls a number twice there is a dispute. Good callers develop a three beat rhythm that makes the letter and the number one announcement, and a room full of players settles into the rhythm without noticing.

What the caller does not do is stop. A game is a sequence of announcements with no gaps in it, and the pressure of the last number being called is not created by the game but by a person reading a list at a speed the room can bear. The tray in the header above holds exactly one ball at a time, which is the number of balls a caller can be certain about.

Ball B‑4 · wire, glass, and a handle

The Cage and the Drum

A bingo drum is a ball cage inside a transparent drum, and the two parts have separate jobs. The drum holds the balls and is turned by a handle; the cage is what keeps the balls from leaving by the top when the drum is tilted, and it is the part that breaks. Wire cages are bent, not welded, so a cage that has been dropped will still hold the balls but will no longer be a circle.

The handle is geared down, usually by about four to one, so that a person can turn the drum for twenty minutes without getting tired. A drum that turns too fast throws the balls up against the glass and they come down in a clump; a drum that turns too slowly does not mix at all, and the same number comes up three times in a row, which is the single most common fault in a hall that bought its equipment from a catalogue.

Ball B‑5 · the letters, the free space, and the column rule

Cards, Columns and Nines

The five letters are not a code and not an acronym. B, I, N, G, O is the second letter of the two words bingo and game, and it was chosen because the letters are far apart alphabetically, which is a practical matter: it means that in a column of cards the numbers run in tidy ascending blocks and a caller reading them in order produces a rhythm that the cards can follow.

The columns are not interchangeable, which surprises people who assume any number can go anywhere. In the standard game the B column runs from one to fifteen, the I column from sixteen to thirty, and so on, up to a free square in the middle of the card. Two cards with the numbers in the wrong columns are not a bad hand, they are two cards that cannot win together, and it happens more often than any hall admits.

Ball B‑6 · ninety balls, one ball left

A Full House in Ninety Numbers

A full house of balls is ninety of them, one for every number between one and ninety, and the balls are not numbered in a spiral for any reason except that it makes them easy to find in a bowl. Each is white with a coloured band and a number that has to be readable from two metres away while somebody is shaking a drum, which in practice means a very large number and a very small letter.

Six balls in a drum would be a game of nothing, and the reason this header has six is that there are six chapters. The rest of the machine is real: the cage, the glass, the handle, the tray with a lip at the front, and the counting, which is the only part a player actually watches.
