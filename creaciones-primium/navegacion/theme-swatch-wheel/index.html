Pick the light
the job is read in

Every sheet a customer reads is read under some light, and the four proofs in the wheel on the right are the four lights this shop actually owns: a daylight window, an uncoated stock, a night desk and a very high contrast proof lamp that shows nothing but errors.

Six sections · four proofs · the choice is remembered for this session

Section 01 · composition

Standing type, set by hand

The shop still sets its first page in metal, because the person who sets it has set it for thirty-one years and the machine that would replace her costs more than the goodwill. Everything after the first page is set digitally and printed the same afternoon.

Composition is a proof, not a decision. The customer reads it under the light of their own room, not ours, which is why the wheel exists and why the choice of proof is written on the job card and never assumed.

Section 02 · inks

Four inks and a great deal of patience

The house colours are mixed in the back room from a card that has not been reprinted since 1998, because the card matches the drums and the drums match what customers remember. A vermilion that is a shade too orange reads as cheap in every proof we have ever run.

Deep tones are laid down in two passes with a drying interval between them, which is the entire reason a dark solid takes a day rather than four minutes. The night proof is the honest one for a dark image, and it is the one customers are most often surprised by.

Section 03 · proofs

Four proofs, one decision

Daylight is the default because most work is read at a table. Uncoated sepia is what the art book customers ask for, and it is the harshest light on a dark image. Night is how a poster is read in a station at eleven at night. The high contrast proof shows nothing but registration and nothing else, and it is the last thing we look at before the run.

Whichever proof is chosen, the customer keeps a printed copy of it. A decision made on a screen in a shop is a decision somebody has to be able to defend on paper.

Section 04 · paper

Six hundred and forty grams of doubt

Every job starts on a plain 120 gram sheet so the customer can see the type before the paper flatters it. When the paper changes, the type changes: ink sinks, weight shows, and a black that looked dense on the proof reads grey under a coat.

The paper list is nineteen entries long and the shop keeps eleven of them in stock. The other eight exist because a customer once asked for one in 1987 and the shop has never forgotten.

Section 05 · the run

Two hundred and forty sheets, slowly

A short run is printed on the flatbed because the machine is already warm and the makeready is a conversation rather than a checklist. The first ten sheets are pulled and read, the next twenty are read again, and only then does the run settle into the rhythm that the whole shop is named for.

Nobody hurries a short run. The people who hurry short runs are the people who end up printing two hundred and forty of the wrong thing.

Section 06 · delivery

Wrapped, boxed and written on the card

The job card travels with the work and records the proof, the paper, the ink batch and the date. A shop that cannot say which batch of ink went into a print run is a shop that will eventually reprint the same book twice in two slightly different colours.

Delivery is by hand inside the city and by post outside it, wrapped in the same paper as every job since 1971, and the wrapper is the only advertising the shop has ever needed.
