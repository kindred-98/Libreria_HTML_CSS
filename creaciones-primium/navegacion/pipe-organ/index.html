Stop I · the supply

The wind

Nothing in an organ moves until the wind arrives. A leather bellows, a weighted wedge or a electric fan pushes air into a reservoir held at a very steady pressure, and that pressure is the single condition the whole instrument depends on. Too little and the pipes speak late; too much and the bottom octave chokes on its own wind.

Organs were tuned to a wind. Raising it makes the instrument louder and brighter and also slightly out of tune with itself, because the pitch of a pipe depends on how hard the air is moving across its edge. The regulation is therefore never finished, and somebody has always been employed to argue with the man who climbs into the tower.

Stop II · the pipes

The ranks

A rank is a set of pipes, one for every note of the keyboard, and it is the rank rather than the pipe that the organist thinks in. Eight feet, four feet, two and two thirds, mixture: the names describe length and mixture of metal, and they are as much a family of voices as a choir is a family of singers.

Each pipe is a tube with a mouth cut near the bottom, and the whole argument of organ building is about the edge that the wind crosses. Sharpen it and the pipe screams; flatten it and the pipe thins out. A rank is a hundred and twenty opinions about one shape.

Stop III · the action

The stop action

Pulling a stop does not make a sound. It adds a rank to a list, and the list is consulted every time a key is pressed, in a piece of wood and leather called a slider that moves sideways under the chest. The mechanism is absurdly indirect, and every improvement made to it has been an attempt to get rid of it.

The consequence is that a stopped console is a promise rather than a decision. A rank sits silent inside the case with the air already passing through it, and the difference between a good registration and a bad one is mostly about which promises you keep and how quietly you make them.

Stop IV · the shades

The swell

The hardest thing a pipe can do is stop sounding. A rank is either on the chest or off it, so the transition is either there or not there, and the difference between those two states is enormous. The swell box exists to make that transition gradual: a shutter that opens as the hand rises, so the sound can arrive rather than switch.

Getting it wrong is immediately obvious. A phrase that grows too slowly feels cautious, a phrase that grows too fast feels like a curtain being pulled, and the player spends years learning the exact speed at which a room can be trusted.

Stop V · the keys

The player at the keys

The connection between a finger and a pipe is a long piece of wood, and the length is the whole point. A pull down tracker pulls a slider, the slider opens a pallet, the pallet lets the wind into a flue, and the pipe speaks. Nine such steps happen in the fraction of a second you will later complain was not fast enough.

It is a chain of decisions, each one made by a craftsman who knew the one before and the one after, and nobody has ever managed to argue convincingly that any of them should be removed. The instrument is the accumulated caution of several centuries, still standing, still slightly out of tune with itself.

Stop VI · the return

The last silence

When the blower is switched off, the wind drains out of the chest and every pipe in the case goes quiet at its own pace, from the biggest to the smallest. An organ sounding is easy to describe. An organ stopping is a slow mechanical event that takes several seconds and is, to anybody standing in the nave at the time, the better half of the piece.

Then there is the case itself, which is warm, still, and full of pipes that have just stopped. That is the state this console is left in when you reach the end of these notes: the wind off, the sliders shut, and the rank you were reading still resonating faintly, as if it had not been told.
