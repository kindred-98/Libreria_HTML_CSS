Rule 01

The Nib

A drawing nib is a small piece of steel with a slit down the middle, and the whole character of a line comes from that slit. Feed it too much ink and the two tines spread and the stroke bloats into a leaf. Feed it too little and the tines part far enough that the paper drags and the line grows a pale streak through the middle of every downstroke.

The slit is what makes a curve legible. On a straight pull the tines sit together and the line has one confident edge; on a turn the outer tine travels further and opens, the ink floods outward, and that swelling is the reason a thick curve reads as depth rather than as a mistake.

Rule 02

Pressure

Sketchers are taught to drag the pen rather than press it, and the sentence sounds like advice about nerves until you watch a line made under real pressure. Pressed strokes darken, sit proud of the paper, and catch the light along their length; dragged strokes are grey in the middle and even at the edges, and they disappear into the tooth of the paper.

Both are useful, which is why the good rule is not about pressure at all but about deciding which one you are making before the first metre goes down. A drawing that mixes them without a reason ends up looking tired in the middle and overworked at the edges.

Rule 03

Registration

Two lines that should sit on top of each other rarely do, and the gap between them is called registration error. On a ruled sheet you can measure it by holding the page up to the light, and the cause is almost always the same: the paper moved between the two passes, or the rule was set down in a slightly different place each time.

Binders solve it with punches. Two holes are made once in the folded edge of the sheet, and every later sheet is fitted over the pins before a single line is ruled. Whatever else a drawing may be allowed to be, it is allowed to be straight, and the pins are what make that promise possible.

Rule 04

Contour

Contour lines are the contour lines of a hillside, sampled at equal height and joined into closed loops. Flat ground produces long lazy lines that wander; a cliff produces lines that crowd together until they merge into a black band, and the density of the lines on the paper is the steepness of the ground underneath them.

The classic mistake is to sample at equal horizontal distance instead of equal height. The result is a drawing that looks plausible from the front and useless from above, because every line ends up describing the same slope as its neighbour no matter what the terrain is doing.

Rule 05

The Inking

Pen and ink rewards a decision made early. Graphite can be rubbed out with a kneaded eraser and half a sentence can be argued with; ink is a commitment, and the practical response is to ink last, over a pencil block that has already been agreed. Most of the cleanliness of a finished drawing is decided before the bottle is opened.

There is a moment about a third of the way along the first line where the hand stops apologising. The stroke settles, the tines stop splitting, and the drawing begins to look like one object rather than six attempts. Experienced drafters will tell you that the only technique involved is surviving that third until the pen believes the paper.

Rule 06

Archive

Drawn sheets survive badly and are valued anyway. Ink lifts in damp, fades in light and eats the paper it is drawn on if the sheet was acidic to begin with, so a drawing kept for a century is usually kept flat, in the dark, in a folder that costs more than the drawing.

What makes the sheet worth that care is that it records the hand. A print can be run again next year; a ruled line cannot, and the difference between a competent drawing and a memorable one is very often just the evidence that a particular person was there, pressing, on a particular afternoon.
