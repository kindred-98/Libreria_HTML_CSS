Root descent

Six strata of an argument made underground. The root on the right is always digging, and the fork it is holding sharp belongs to whichever chapter you are standing in.

Stratum 1 of 6 · Surface

01

Surface

A root is not an anchor. Anchors resist; roots search, and they search in a way that looks passive only because the searching happens in slow motion. The first metre is the easiest and the most crowded: worm channels, old root traces, a crust of organic matter that dries out every August and never quite dies.

The root tip pushes past all of it by growing ahead of itself, sliding between particles with a coat of slime and a supply of enzymes that dissolves whatever is in the way. It is doing chemistry at the point of a pencil.

02

Topsoil

Below the litter the soil becomes a warehouse. Humus holds water and nutrients on its surface area, and the root pays for access by making trades: sugars for minerals, minerals for the stabilising compounds that keep the crumbs together around the tip.

Most of the branching happens here, in the dark and the damp, and it happens because a root tip detects a gradient and turns. Left or right is not a preference. It is whichever side of the tip is a fraction of a percent wetter.

03

Subsoil

The colour changes and so does the arithmetic. Organic matter is scarce, so the root stops spreading sideways and commits to a line. This is the section of the profile where you can find the same root system running straight down for two metres and barely a lateral of consequence.

That is not stubbornness. It is a bet that the water table is lower than the surface, and it is usually a good one in any climate where it rains less than people expect.

04

Clay pan

Then the tap hits something the tip cannot dissolve. A compacted layer, a plough pan, a natural seam of heavy clay: the root arrives, deflects, and finds a way around it along the interface. The fork that splits here is forced, and the two tines come out of it bent and blunt.

Watch the root on the right when you reach this chapter. The tines shorten and thicken, because a root that cannot push through stops trying to push and starts bracing.

05

Bedrock

Rock does not move and does not forgive. Where the fissure network is generous, roots thread it, and they do so because the fissures are wetter and better aerated than the rock face itself. Where it is solid, the root simply stops descending and turns back into the soil it knows.

It is worth remembering how much of the visible forest is rooted in the first half metre, and how far the rest of the network is a slow lateral hunt along cracks nobody can see.

06

Water

The whole descent is for this. Roots do not go deep because depth is impressive; they go deep because the water table is the only thing in the soil that never runs out, and the tip will cross metres of dry profile with a growth rate that looks like hesitation until you notice the rate of a root that has found the layer.

When the water rises, the tip turns up. When it falls, the tip goes back down and the system above it stops building new leaves, because for a plant, the honest report on a drought is a smaller canopy, not a louder one.

Read downward and the probe goes with you. The fork that sharpens belongs to the stratum you are in.
