Crossing I

Three Strands

A plait is three lengths of something and a decision. Two strands and it is a twist; four and it is a rope; three is the only number where the crossings sit on top of one another instead of beside each other, and that is the whole reason the number is three. It is the smallest number of strands that can hide its own path.

The threads here are one linen, one madder and one woad, the two oldest dyes in the trade, and they are all the same weight. A plait made with threads of different weights will lie flat on one side and pucker on the other, and no amount of tension will fix it.

Crossing II

Starting the Plait

Three strands, two hands, and a thumb. The left-hand pair is held crossed, the third is laid over them, and then everything swaps sides. The first four crossings are the difficult ones because the strands are not yet under any tension, and the whole plait is decided in the first two seconds.

On a table, as here, the strands are pinned at the head of the work and the tension comes entirely from your hands. A plait laid flat with no tension in it will spread out of its pattern within a minute, which is why a good plait is always pulled tight before it is laid down and never let go afterwards.

Crossing III

The Over and Under

Each strand goes over at some crossings and under at others, and the pattern is a rule rather than a choice: each strand takes its turn on top, in order, once per crossing. Three strands means a cycle of three, so the strand on top at the first crossing is the strand under at the fourth, and the plait is symmetrical in a way that never quite comes back to where it started.

That rule is the plait, and it is why the top thread changes every time the plait runs one crossing further. Change the chapter on this page and you can watch the lead pass from thread to thread exactly the way it does in the hand.

Crossing IV

The Selvedge

A plait needs a flat end to start from and a flat end to finish on, and both come from the selvedge of the cloth rather than from a cut edge. The selvedge is denser than the body of the weave and it will not fray, which is exactly what you want at the point where three ends have to be held in one place for as long as the work takes.

Hand plaiting is a selvedge craft for this reason. Cut lengths can be used once, with the ends tucked and lost, and a good selvedge edge can be plaited again and again for years, which is how the same braid ends up in the same three dresses in three generations.

Crossing V

Finishing the Binding

To finish, two strands are cross plaited for another two crossings so there are three tails, and the tails are then wound round and round the end of the plait under the thumb. The binding is not sewn. It is friction, and it holds for about as long as the plait is not washed.

After a wash the binding is undone, the plait is stretched out straight, and it is bound again. This is the part everybody thinks is cheating and is not: a plait that is not re-bound after washing comes out of the machine a length of slightly frayed rope.

Crossing VI

Taking It Apart

Unplaiting is done from the binding forward and only ever from the end, and you keep the tension exactly as it was. Pull the first crossing open and the whole thing relaxes by a third of its length in your hands, and the temptation is to let it, which is how a plait ends up with two short strands and one long one.

The button on the head of this page opens the plait the same way, and pressing it again closes it back up in the same order, which is the only thing anybody who has done this by hand would think to do.
