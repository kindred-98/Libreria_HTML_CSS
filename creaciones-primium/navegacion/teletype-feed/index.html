Line 01 · before the wire

Before the wire

For most of the nineteenth century a message travelled as a sound. An operator read a sentence aloud in a code, a second operator listened and wrote it down, and somewhere between the two of them the wording drifted. A clerk who had never seen the original could not tell whether the governor had asked for the storm or for the storm again.

That is the problem the teleprinter solved by removing the human middle. The message stopped being a voice to be interpreted and became a machine to be received, and the machine had a very specific idea of what a word was: a fixed number of electrical bits, always the same width, never ambiguous.

Line 02 · the code

The code under the noise

Five bits, thirty-two combinations, one of them reserved as a space. It is an extraordinarily small alphabet for a language, and the machines compensated by printing slowly, stopping the shaft between letters so the line on the paper could be read while the circuit was still open.

You could hear the code if you listened to the line: a rattle of clicks, then a pause, then a rattle. Operators learned to compose at the speed of the noise, and a teleprinter operator could often tell a bad line from a good one without reading a single word of the tape.

Line 03 · the receiver

The machine that talks

Inside the cabinet there are three parts that matter. A constant tension keeps the paper against the platen, a code wheel turns once per character, and a vibrating reed decides whether the hammer strikes. Take away the reed and the machine prints nothing at all, which is why a teletype that has gone quiet is not broken, only deaf.

The strip that comes out of the machine is narrow, punched along both edges, and covered in a single line of text. It is the same width for every message in the world, and when a message is too long the machine simply keeps going until the paper runs out.

Line 04 · the night shift

The office at three in the morning

A wire room sounded like a forest of small insects. Every key on every machine had its own pitch, and a teleprinter that was about to fail announced itself by a fraction of a tone. Operators kept a spare part in the drawer, a spare ribbon in the pocket, and a list of what had gone wrong that night.

The tape was the record. Once the text had been printed and cut from the platen, the message could be folded, initialled and sent on, and the machine itself went on to the next one. Nothing about the exchange was memorable, which was exactly the point.

Line 05 · the speed

News at the speed of a line

Words per minute was the unit that mattered, and a good line ran faster than any person could comfortably read. That gap between transmission and comprehension is the whole reason the machine changed the news: not because it was fast, but because it was consistent, and a consistent delay is a delay a reader can plan around.

Editors learned to write for the machine in a different way. Short sentences, because a line break could swallow half a clause. Numbers spelled out, because a misread digit is a correction nobody notices. No italics, no underlining, no anything the paper could not carry.

Line 06 · the end

The quiet after

By the time the last offices closed, the machines were still warm and the paper smell of ribbons had settled into the room. There is a particular kind of silence that a machine leaves behind, the silence of a mechanism that is ready and has nothing to say, and the operators who worked nights for decades claimed they could hear when a line was about to come alive again.

The tape on the desk is the last of that world. The circuit is gone, the wires are gone, and the destinations are printed on the paper anyway, in ink that does not fade, in an order that only changes when the page asks it to.
