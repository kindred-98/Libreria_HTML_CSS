Door 01 · the marble

The marble

Every material used on a nineteenth-century façade was chosen for what it does to light, and marble is the one that does the most. A limestone wall is diffuse: it takes light and gives it back as a flat tone. Marble is different. It has a translucency of a few millimetres, so a grazing beam of sun makes the stone itself glow, and the veining, which is a scatter of impurities through the crystal, lights up with it.

The practical consequence is that the same slab looks like three different materials in one morning. Grey at breakfast, warm cream at noon, and a pale pink at four o'clock when the sun comes round the corner. Buildings were designed around that fact. A marble front is oriented so that there is a moment when the whole elevation is lit evenly, and the architects who worked this out were not being precious: they were building a piece of equipment that responds to the sun.

Door 02 · the arch

The arch

An arch over a door is not a way of getting more headroom; a square opening is perfectly good at carrying a lintel. The arch is a decision about how the elevation is read, and the decision is usually that the openings should form a rhythm rather than a grid. A row of square doors is a wall with holes in it. A row of arched doors is a colonnade, and the difference is about the viewer's expectation of what comes next.

Structurally the arch is honest in a way the lintel is not. The thrust goes down and out through the jambs, which is why an arched opening is visibly stiffer at the sides, and that stiffness is exactly where the eye expects weight in a door. The keystone is usually a different stone, often a darker one, purely so that the eye can find the crown of the arch at a distance of thirty feet. The six bays above are that rhythm, and they are the reason this wall is a front door rather than a wall.

Door 03 · the reveal

The reveal

The reveal is the depth of the opening in the wall, and it is the single most useful thing a doorway can have. Look through a hole in a thin wall and you see a slice of what is behind. Look through a deep one and you see a room, because the sides of the opening are visible and give the eye something to measure against. A shallow reveal makes an entrance look like a picture; a deep one makes it look like a way in.

It also solves noise and weather, which is why almost every good public threshold in a cold country is deep. You get a dry place to stand while you get your keys out, a surface that is not the door, and a door that can open outwards into that space without hitting anybody. The light that comes out of a deep opening is directional and has edges, and the six openings above all do the same thing, which is why the one that is open throws a shape on the floor rather than a glow.

Door 04 · the leaf

The leaf

A door leaf is a slab with an edge, and the edge is the interesting part: it is the only part of the door that is never visible when the door is shut. Marble arrises chip, which is why an old marble door has a rounded outer corner where the arris used to be, and why a heavy leaf is often specified with a bullnose edge so that the chipping is designed in rather than accidental.

The pivot is the other decision. A single leaf on a hinge at one side is cheap, strong and easy to make swing, and it takes up half the opening. Two leaves on a central pivot take up the whole opening, look symmetrical from the street, and are much harder to hang. In an arcade the symmetry wins, which is why the doors above are single leaves hinged at the left, with the hinge side consistent all the way down the row so that the light always falls the same way.

Door 05 · the lobby

The lobby

Behind the doors is the room that actually does the work of a public building, and it is almost always darker than the street. That inversion is deliberate. A lit interior behind a glazed door makes the glass a mirror and the entrance invisible; a dark interior makes the opening a hole you want to go through, and it means the person inside is a silhouette rather than a person being looked at.

The old arrangement was a single unlit hall with a lantern above, and the whole effect depended on the contrast between the sunlit marble outside and the brown dark within. The new arrangement lights the lobby properly and loses almost nothing except the drama, and almost every refurbishment of a building like this is really an argument about that contrast. The bay that is lit above is the lobby at its best: dark at the back, bright at the door, and gold where the light lands on the floor.

Door 06 · the gold

The gold

Gold leaf on a door is not a structural or a durable decision, and it was never intended to be. It is two hundred and fifty grams of metal beaten thinner than a hair and applied to a gesso ground, and it lasts roughly as long as nobody touches that part of the door. It survives on the parts that are protected by a frame and fails on the ones at hand height, which is why the surviving original gilding is always in the middle of a panel where the hand cannot reach.

What gold does in an elevation is stop an eye. Gold is the only common material with a strong specular reflection, so it reads as a flash rather than a colour, and a door with a gilded line across it will pull attention from across a square at a moment when the marble cannot. The bay at the far end of this row is the one with the most gilding left on it, and it is the one that has been painted least often, and those two facts are not a coincidence.
