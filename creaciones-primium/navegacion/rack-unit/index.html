Unit 1 · the format

One unit, nineteen inches

A rack is a shelf with holes in it. The holes are one inch apart vertically, the panel between two of them is called a unit, and every piece of studio equipment ever made for a rack was designed around that decision before anybody decided what the equipment would do. A compressor that does one thing well and a compressor that does four things badly occupy exactly the same amount of room.

The format survived because it is dull. A dull standard that everybody agrees on is worth more than a good one that nobody does, and for forty years the alternative to a rack was a trolley with a handle, which is where the tape machines went and where they still are.

Unit 2 · the fixings

Screws and panels

Every unit is held by four screws into threaded holes, and there is a particular satisfaction in a rack that is properly screwed: the panels line up in a single plane, the doors close, and the whole thing stops rattling when somebody walks past. Most racks are assembled with a driver and a lot of optimism, and the ones in the corner of a small studio are usually held up mostly by hope.

The aluminium face is thin, deliberately thin, because it only has to carry a label and a few knobs and a light that has to be visible from across the room. Everything that matters is behind it, and the panel is the part everybody photographs.

Unit 3 · the stack

The rack and the room

The order of the units is a piece of signal flow written in a vertical column, and it is the only document in the studio that everybody can read without knowing anything about sound. Signal goes down the rack, gets processed on the way, and comes out at the bottom, and moving a unit two slots changes the sound in a way that a manual would have had to warn you about.

There is also a thermal argument, which is the older one. The bottom of a full rack is the warmest place in the room, and equipment that lives there ages faster than equipment that does not, which is why the quietest machines have always ended up at the top.

Unit 4 · the meter

Levels and headroom

Every panel in the rack has a light that turns red, and the light is the loudest thing in the room. It is a single bit of information, and an engineer will watch it several hundred times an hour, and the whole professional argument about levels is really an argument about what that light should mean.

The honest answer is that it should mean the output is about to distort. Instead it means that somebody chose a reference level once, and everything since has been an argument with that choice. A rack where the red light is on permanently is not a loud rack, it is a rack with a bad setting.

Unit 5 · the hours

The session

A day of recording is a series of small adjustments made by a person who cannot hear the room the way the listener will, working from memory. The rack is the only place where the state of that judgement is written down, which is why an engineer walking into a studio and seeing a rack already set up knows something before anybody says a word.

When it goes well the rack is barely touched. The sounds are right because somebody spent an hour making the settings boring, and the session then consists of people arriving, playing, and leaving the machines alone.

Unit 6 · the end

Packing down

At the end of a session the rack is packed, and packing is a ritual with a fixed order: power off at the top, cables coiled, panels screwed home, the empty spaces covered with blanks so the airflow stops going through the wrong places. It takes longer than switching on and nobody watches it happen.

What you get at the end is a dark rectangle with a few lights still on, which is exactly what a rack looks like when it is doing nothing. It is also the only machine in the building that is designed to be switched off and left alone, and the six units above are what it looks like on the way to being useful.
