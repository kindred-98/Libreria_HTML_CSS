Spore landing

Six steps of a journey measured in microns, from a cell in the air to a fruiting body on a rotten log. The plate along the bottom of this page is where each of them lands.

01

Launch

A spore begins as a cell with a hard coat, a droplet of cytoplasm and a wall thick enough to survive drying out and being swallowed. It is built to be fired, not to float, and a good many fungi are launched from a membrane under tension that ruptures all at once and throws the spore a couple of metres through still air.

From there it is mostly luck and physics. Air currents that move a few centimetres a second will carry a spore kilometres, and the only thing it has to get right is to be in the right air mass on the right night.

02

Germinate

Landing is not the hard part. A spore that settles on a surface with moisture, warmth and something to feed on will swell within hours, and its coat cracks along a pre-formed seam so the contents can push through without ever exposing a naked interior to the outside world.

What it needs next is a chemical reason to stay. A germinating spore does not explore. It puts out one tube, chemically follows whatever gradient it detects, and commits. Most of them commit in the wrong direction and are finished.

03

Dormant

Between the landing and the feeding there is a wait that can last months. A spore on a surface with no nutrients will sit there, metabolically almost idle, holding a complete genome and a full set of instructions for a organism that has never existed.

This is why soil sterilised in an autoclave stays sterile for years, and why a disturbed woodland floor can produce a crop of mushrooms in its first wet autumn out of spores that arrived decades earlier.

04

Mycelium

The fungal body of a mushroom is not the mushroom. It is the net: filaments a few microns thick branching through soil, leaf litter and wood, fusing with their own neighbours and trading nutrients along the shared walls.

Given time and a food source, a single germinating spore can build a network measured in kilometres of thread across a whole hillside, mapping the chemistry of the soil from the inside in a way no soil sample can.

05

Fruiting

Fruiting is a decision to stop being invisible. Once the net has banked enough carbon, it builds a reproductive structure that pushes up out of the substrate, and the whole point of the cap and the gills underneath is to make the underside look like a surface.

That surface sheds spores at a rate of billions a day, and the architecture of a cap is entirely about air: how to drop the spores into a predictable laminar flow so they get carried off rather than falling straight back onto the fungus that made them.

06

Rest

The cap opens, releases its cargo and collapses, usually within a day or two, because the expensive part is already spent. What remains is the net, which quietly recycles the dead tissue and goes on accumulating carbon for another season.

Over a lifetime the organism is mostly invisible, and the mushrooms are just the moments where it is briefly, unmistakably alive above ground. The plate at the foot of this page holds one of those moments in each of its six dishes.
