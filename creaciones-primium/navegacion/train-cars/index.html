Car 01

Marshalling

Marshalling is the sorting of wagons into a train that has somewhere to be, and it happens on flat yards where a locomotive can get round and push from the far end. The wagons arrive in whatever order the branch lines delivered them, and a ground frame full of points decides what joins what. Nothing in the process is hurried, and the shift can run for hours.

The result is a train that reads as an argument. Heaviest and shortest wagons near the locomotive, the brake van at the very back so its handbrake can be run through the whole train, and the dangerous loads in the middle where a derailment does least harm to whatever is riding alongside. A marshalling yard is a piece of logic with steel in it.

Car 02

Shunting

Shunting is the slow work, and it is where crews are judged. Wagons are propelled at walking pace, judged by eye from the footplate, and a movement of a few inches is sometimes better than a movement of a foot. The signals are permissive rather than absolute, and the driver is responsible for stopping short rather than being protected by anything.

It is also the only part of the job where the ground is close enough to see. Wagons move on their own after a while, drifting on a gradient, and the crew learns which of them will roll and which will not. A yard that looks flat from the footplate can be a two in one thousand for a fortnight.

Car 03

Coupling Speed

Couplings are made at a speed, and the speed is small on purpose. A buffer coupling has to close without bang: the knuckle has to be guided over the knuckle, the two draw hooks have to find each other, and the shackle has to drop into place. Hit it too hard and the knuckle either fails to close or closes out of square.

Automatic couplings solve the problem by removing the judgement. The knuckle is forced open by a ramp, rides over the pin, and snaps shut on its own with a noise the whole yard knows. What is gained in consistency is paid for in damage: a coupler that closes wrongly is now a coupler that has to be broken before anything can move, and the rule is that no wagon is moved until a person has been under the solebar and looked.

Car 04

Brake Rigging

The vacuum pipe runs the length of the train, and the brake is a piece of plumbing held shut by a vacuum. Release the vacuum and air rushes in, a piston moves, and the shoes press against the wheels. Let the vacuum back and the springs pull the shoes off again. On a long train the pipe is a pressure wave travelling at the speed of sound, which is why the last wagon can still be braking after the locomotive has stopped.

The system fails in a particular direction, and the designers thought about that first. A pipe that parts in the middle lets the vacuum out, and every vehicle behind the break loses its brake entirely, so a continuous pipe is run through every vehicle and every break in it is fitted with a non-return valve that shuts on a pressure drop. The train brakes itself in the direction of safety rather than the direction of the fault.

Car 05

Permanent Way

The permanent way is the rails, the sleepers, the ballast and the fastenings, and the name is a legal one: it distinguishes the ground that is built and maintained as railway from the ground it merely crosses. Rails are steel because nothing cheaper survives the load; the section is a simple I so the material is where the wheel runs and nowhere else.

Everything under the rails is there to control movement rather than carry weight. Ballast is angular stone because it has to interlock and stop, and a round gravel would roll into a trough under the first heavy train. Sleepers spread the load. The fastenings allow the rail to move lengthwise as it expands, which is a small allowance of a few millimetres and the difference between a railway that works in August and one that buckles in July.

Car 06

Run Round

When a locomotive is at the wrong end of its own train, somebody has to walk to the other end and bring it back. Running round is the manoeuvre that does it: the locomotive is detached, crosses to the far end of the rake, and couples up in the other direction. It is easy to describe and quite hard to do with a full train on a falling grade.

It is also a rule rather than an engineering problem. No train is run round without a brake van and no guard, and the person in charge of the movement rides on the footplate of the engine with the handbrake under their hand the entire time. Most of the difficulty is in keeping the wagons exactly where they were while a locomotive two hundred metres of solid steel is put in front of them instead of behind.
