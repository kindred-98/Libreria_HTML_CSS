Mashrabiya Screen

The header of this page is a balcony screen. Six rows of turned plaster sit over a blue room, and the row you are reading is the row that is opened: its openings widen, the afternoon gets through them in a single band, and a wooden mullion slides down the frame to sit level with the row you are on.

Six levels of openings. The open one is the level you are reading; the mullion sits level with it.

Level I

The Balcony

A mashrabiya is a room made of holes. It sits out over the street on carved corbels, it is closed on three sides and open to the air on the fourth, and the person inside can see everything without being seen. The point of the screen was never to keep the weather out. It was to make being watched a choice.

What you see at the top of this page is a small one: six rows of openings, a metre and a half tall, enough for one person to sit behind and read while a street goes past underneath.

Level II

Turning the Wood

Every opening in a mashrabiya is turned on a lathe and then set into the frame by hand, one at a time, with a peg and a chisel. The units are made in batches so the moulding repeats along the run, which is why a long screen reads as a rhythm rather than as a picture. The carpenter keeps the offcuts and glues them back in as internal ribs, because the thinnest parts of the screen carry the most load.

Plaster was poured into those same moulds for the outer faces in the later houses, and a painted screen can be lifted off its frame in three or four pieces by two people. Wood survives being dropped. Plaster does not.

Level III

Levels of Privacy

The upper part of a screen is usually open and the lower part is closed, and the reason is that the interesting view is horizontal. From about a metre and a half up, the lattice is a grille; below that it becomes a solid panel with only a small grille at the top, so a standing person on the pavement sees nothing at all.

Which level you are at is therefore a statement, and the screen is designed to be read from outside as a statement. Six rows, each of which can be shut on its own, is a very old version of a very modern idea: per-level control of who is looking at whom.

Level IV

The Afternoon Wind

From two o'clock the wind comes across the rooftops and hits the screen square on, and the whole balcony is a filter. It arrives already broken into pieces the size of the openings, so what reaches the room has lost its force and kept its movement. Everybody who has sat in one of these has noticed the same thing: the air inside is moving and yet it is not windy.

The screen is also what keeps the room cool by a long way. The sun is stopped at the plaster and the heat never enters the mass of the building at all, which is why an old house with a mashrabiya is several degrees cooler in July than a new one with a sealed window of the same size.

Level V

Reading the Lattice

From the street a screen reads as a texture, and the geometry matters more than the subject. The oldest ones are a single repeating star, and the master carver would build the whole elevation from that one unit without ever changing its size, so the eye finds no place to rest and no place to look for a door.

The screen at the head of this page follows the same rule. Six rows of identical openings line up vertically, and when one row is opened the light through it continues the vertical line of every other row instead of breaking it. That is what alignment is for in a lattice: not decoration, but an address.

Level VI

Taking It Down

The repairs are done from the street, on a rope, which is why the craft survived in the form it did. A damaged panel is unscrewed, carried down, and mended on a bench; it is never mended in place. The join is a simple tongue into a groove and a screw putty the colour of the paint, and a good restoration leaves those joints visible rather than hiding them.

Anybody can tell a restored screen from an original one by the grain. A new piece of beech is pale and even and does not have the dark lines that a hundred summers of dust leave in the open grain, and the difference is the only honest record the screen keeps of its own life.

The screen is the navigation: every row is a link into the levels below, and the opened row is the level you are reading.
