Fern unfurl

Everything a fern does in the first week of its life, told in six steps. The strip beneath this line is a row of coiled fronds, and only the one you are reading has committed to being a leaf.

  2. Knot
  3. Push
  4. Fiddle
  5. Reach

Frond 1 of 6 · Crozier

01

Crozier

A fern does not push a leaf into the air. It pushes a knot, and the knot is the whole difficulty. A young frond is packed so tightly that its own tip is curled back on itself several times, protected by a waxy skin and shaped like the head of a bishop's staff.

The coil is not decorative. Every cell in the frond is dividing at different rates, and the ones at the tip are growing far more slowly than those behind, which is what produces the spiral in the first place.

02

Knot

The pressure inside the crosier is remarkable. It is generated by cells in the stalk taking up water faster than they can hold it, and a cut fern stem will bleed for a surprisingly long time because the tension does not immediately let go.

Left alone, that pressure becomes movement. The knot rises out of the ground, thick and pale, and the sheath that protected the coil splits along a seam that was mapped before the frond ever broke soil.

03

Push

The frond now extends by a different mechanism entirely, and it is startlingly fast. Behind the tip, cells are stretching rather than dividing, the frond lengthening by a measurable fraction every night, and the coils unwinding one after another from the outside in.

Because the growth happens in the dark, nobody sees the middle of the process. By the time you look in the morning, the crosier is standing clear of the soil with its head still curled.

04

Fiddle

A young frond spends its early weeks as a fiddlehead, a green crozier a few centimetres tall that is still recognisably a spiral. The pinnae are folded tight against the rachis and the whole thing looks edible, which is exactly how it got its name.

The spiral tightens further as the tip extends, so the silhouette of a fiddlehead tells you how far the frond has to go. A wide loose coil means it is nearly done.

05

Reach

Then the frond opens, and it is not a single event. Each leaflet unfolds in sequence from the base upwards, like a hand being unrolled, and the tip is always the last thing still curled. Give it a week and the crosier shape is gone entirely.

The rachis keeps lengthening after the leaflets are out, and a mature frond can add several centimetres in a day in the right conditions. It is the fastest sustained growth of anything most people will ever keep on a windowsill.

06

Shade

Now the plant has to live on what it opened. Ferns took this shape because they live in the shade of other plants, where light arrives dim, brief and filtered, and where a broad blade would lose more water than the soil could replace.

What they trade for that economy is speed of repair. Cut a frond and the plant does not mourn it; it simply starts another crosier, usually several at once, and the rolled tip waits in the dark until the conditions are right again.

Every frond here arrives coiled. The one that unrolls is the chapter you are in.
