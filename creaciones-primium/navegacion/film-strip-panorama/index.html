Reel 01 / Silent era · 1895–1929

Hand-cranked light on nitrate

The earliest holdings arrive on flammable nitrate with hand-inked titles and a projector speed that varies from shot to shot because a person was turning the handle. Volunteers rebuild missing reels by matching frames from duplicate prints held in three different countries.

Everything here was designed for the eye alone: an orchestra pit note survives for one theatre, but the films were never married to a single soundtrack. Prints are scanned at 4K and returned to the vault within four hours of the last frame.

Reel 02 / Noir cycle · 1940–1958

Venetian blinds and one lamp

The noir cycle in this vault is unusually complete because the studio dumped its camera negatives in 1963 and the archive bought the lot by weight rather than by title. Deep focus, hard shadows and night exteriors shot at noon under a black sky.

Six of the prints carry censor cuts that were reinstated only in 1979, and the archive keeps both versions indexed separately so researchers can compare them frame for frame. Sound tracks are magnetic stripes, cleaned rather than remixed.

Reel 03 / New wave · 1959–1971

Handheld cameras on borrowed film stock

New wave material arrived mostly as work prints with visible splices, because the finished negatives stayed with the labs. The archive keeps the splices: they record where a director cut in the room rather than where a distributor cut for release.

Off-sync dialogue, natural light and long lenses used from across the street. Each reel is stored with its continuity notes, which for four of these titles are the only surviving record of a scene that never made the final print.

Reel 04 / Blockbuster · 1972–1995

Wide screens, wide sound, long prints

Seventy-millimetre prints take four people and a full day to inspect, which the archive does twice a year. The reels are numbered on the can and on the leader so that a mix-up in the vault can be caught before a projector ever sees it.

Optical sound is replaced by digital intermediates only when the original optical track is beyond repair, and the untouched negative is always kept beside it. Matte paintings are catalogued as separate elements rather than as part of the shot.

Reel 05 / Digital turn · 1996–2012

Formats that no longer have a player

The digital transition left the archive with tapes, discs and camera cards beside the prints they replaced. Each carrier is kept with the machine that reads it, and the machines are kept running by two engineers who reverse-engineered parts that stopped shipping in 2009.

Checksums run over every migration: a file is not considered preserved until it has been read back, verified and copied to a second site on a different storage technology. Metadata is stored as plain text so it can still be read when the catalogue software is gone.

Reel 06 / Contemporary · 2013–2026

Shot digitally, printed back onto film

Recent acquisitions arrive as digital masters and leave the building as 35 mm prints, because a projection on film is still the only way some colour grading can be seen as intended. Two labs in Europe still strike prints for the archive.

Every title is deposited with its colour space, its grain plate reference and the name of the person who approved the print. Silent-film style slide transitions aside, nothing in this era is archived without a paper record beside the data.
