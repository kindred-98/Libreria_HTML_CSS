Stained Glass

The header of this page is a leaded window in a dark nave. Six panels of glass are held in lead, and the glass outside is not bright everywhere: only the panel belonging to the chapter you are reading is lit from behind. The rest stay cold and blue, and the pool of colour on the floor slides across to wherever the light is coming through.

Six panels, one lit. The lit panel is the chapter you are reading, and the colour it throws moves with it.

Panel I

The Cartoon

Nothing is cut until the drawing is finished, and the drawing is a full-size picture made of joined sheets of paper, painted with the glaze colours the glazier intends to fire. It is called the cartoon and it is the reason a window can be restored five hundred years later: the cartoon is kept, and every missing piece can be cut again from the same full-size line.

The transfer to glass is done from the back, so the painter's line has to be the mirror of what will be seen in the window. Every glazier learns this once and never again, and the cartoons of a good workshop are full of small corrections made by cutting the wrong piece and trying it in the leading anyway.

Panel II

Cobalt

Cobalt blue is not a colour that sits still. A pot of it fired with copper goes a clean, hot, slightly purple blue, and the same pot fired with iron in the mix goes muddy and dark within a few degrees of temperature. The glazier controls it by stacking the pieces in the kiln at different heights and reading the colour off a small test tile through a spy hole every hour.

Which is why the blue in an old window is never one blue. It is six blues, one for each firing, and the only honest way to restore a missing piece of cobalt is to accept that it will never be quite the blue it replaces.

Panel III

Amber

Amber is glass with iron in it and almost no other metal, and it is the only colour in the window that was never painted on. It arrives in the pot already amber, and it fires to a deep orange that goes from honey to rust across a range of about twenty degrees. A window that leans on amber is warmer, and the other colours have to be chosen to sit beside it rather than fight it.

Stripped amber, where the surface silver has been etched away, is used where a face needs to be pale and the rest of the window needs to be hot. It is the oldest trick in the trade and it still works better than any painted grisaille.

Panel IV

The Leading

Every piece is cut on a grozing iron, a block of beech with a channel cut across it, and the cut edges are ground at forty-five degrees so the panes meet in a heart shape rather than a butt joint. The pieces are then laid up on an iron table, bedded in putty, and a strip of lead is pressed over every joint by hand, one line at a time.

A line of good leading is even in width and dead straight over its whole length, and it is the single thing that most distinguishes a window made by a glazier from a window made by somebody who has seen photographs of glaziers.

Panel V

The Light at Three

The sun does the same thing to a window every afternoon, in the same order, and glaziers plan around it. By three the light is low and comes in almost horizontally, and only the panels in the bottom half of the window carry any colour into the room. The upper panels are for the morning and they are nearly dead by noon.

That is why a window is designed to be walked past rather than stared at. The pool of colour on the floor of this page slides to whichever panel is lit, and if you are standing in a real nave at three in the afternoon you will be standing in the bottom left of it and seeing almost nothing at all.

Panel VI

Restoration

Taking a window out of its frame is a job with one rule: never lay it flat. It goes out of the opening edge first, upright, onto two padded trestles, and everything that has come loose is put back in place with a piece of soft card and a lot of patience before it is lifted at all. A window laid on its back under its own weight will lose a hundred years of work in an afternoon.

The work itself is mostly lead. Old leading is brittle, and the repair is to strip it, keep every piece of glass that survives, and re-lead the whole window rather than the damaged third of it, because a leaded window is one object and a partial repair is a permanent weak point in the middle of it.

The window is the navigation: every panel is a link into the chapters below, and the lit panel is the chapter you are reading.
