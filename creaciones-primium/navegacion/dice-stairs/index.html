Reading a throw

The die that is lit is the tread you are standing on. It is the only die on the stair with a shadow under it, because it is the only one that has been picked up.

Dice on the treads you have passed are in the shade. They keep their faces, and the face they show is the one they landed on when they were thrown there.

The stair has six treads and no handrail, on the grounds that a stair with a handrail is a staircase and a stair is somewhere you are going up quickly.

Tread I · one, two, three and a blank

How Dice Are Made

A die is a cube with a number of holes drilled into five of its faces, and the holes are drilled from both sides at once so that the two sets of pips meet in the middle and the face of the die is the same depth whichever way up it lands. The square corner of the die is cut and then the whole cube is rolled in a bowl until every corner is slightly rounded, which is why a new die is noticeably quieter in the bowl than a thirty year old one.

Bone, horn, boxwood, brass, ivory and acrylic have all been used and every one of them was replaced because of the same problem, which is that a die has to be hard enough to keep its corners and even enough to lie flat. A die that rocks is a die that decides the game, and a boxwood die that has been handled for a decade is measurably more even than a resin one that was cast last week.

Tread II · why the three goes across and not down

Pips and Corners

The arrangement of the pips on a die is fixed, and almost everybody who plays dice knows it without being told. The one and the six run along the same axis, the two and the five along another, and the three and the four along a third, so that a one and a six cannot land face to face and be read as a seven. Every casino die in the world is drilled to that arrangement, and it has been drilled to it since the eighteenth century.

The pips are counters, and they are read as counters: a face with one pip is one, a face with six is six, and the two in the corner is diagonally opposite the five for the same reason the one is opposite the six. The first dice had figures and scenes and were read as pictures, and the moment a die became a counter it became a number that a stranger can read in the time it takes to turn over.

Tread III · the opposite faces add to seven

The Casino Cut

On a casino die the three faces that meet at a corner always add to seven, which follows from the arrangement of the pips and is the single most useful fact about a die: if you know three faces you know the other three, and if you know one face you know the opposite one, and if you are playing a game where dice are on the table in view, that is the whole of the observation.

It is also why casino dice are almost always transparent. A clear resin die with a metal core can be lit from underneath and photographed with a strobe to confirm that the result is legitimate, and a bone die cannot be checked at all, which is the reason professional play has not used bone since the middle of the twentieth century and the reason a well made casino set is the single most valuable object in a gaming bag.

Tread IV · a cup, a table, and a height

The Weight of a Throw

A throw is three things: a cup, a surface, and a height, and everything about how the dice land follows from the three of them. Dice thrown hard sound different from dice thrown gently and the sound is the whole of the information: a good throw hits the table, spreads, and comes to rest with a rattle, and a bad throw is one die striking two dice and stopping.

The cup is there to solve the only problem dice have, which is that a die thrown by hand is a die with a personality. A cup takes the throw away from the hand and gives it to a fixed surface, which is why the first rule of every dice game is that the dice are thrown and not rolled, and the reason a die that has been rolled across a table and picked up is worth nothing to anybody.

Tread V · the only two numbers anybody keeps

Seven and Ten

The average of two dice is seven and the most common single result of two dice is also seven, at one time in six, and both of those numbers are consequences of the same arrangement. Seven is in the middle of the range and it is also the sum of the two middle faces, so it wins the argument on both grounds at once.

Ten is the other number everybody keeps, and it is the most common result of three dice and the least common single result of two, which is a good illustration of how a table game punishes a player for one idea. A staircase of dice is a very bad place to play anything, because a stair is a ramp and a ramp is a direction, and a direction is not a distribution.

Tread VI · six treads and no handrail

The Run

Games with dice and no dice at all are usually played on a surface, and a surface has a shape. A round table, a square table, a long table with a rail down the middle, and a set of steps: all of them exist because somebody worked out that the shape of the place the dice land changes the game, and all of them are still in use for the reason they were invented, which is that a game with a boundary is a game that ends.

The stair above is the last of those shapes and the worst of them to play on, because a die thrown on a step lands on a step and a die that goes over the edge is out of play and nobody has ever agreed whether that is a five or a rethrow. What a stair is very good at is showing a position at a glance, and the die that is lit is the tread you are on.
