Reading the court

The ochre line is the route for the chapter you are reading. It is cut into the stones from the north entrance to the gate you chose, and it is cut again from the beginning every time the gate changes.

The flagstones are a grid on purpose. A real court is laid in courses, and a route that ignores the courses is a route drawn on paper rather than a route walked on the ground.

Moss only grows where the water sits, which is along the joints and against the north side of the wall. Nothing on this court is decorative that would not have a reason to be there.

Gate I · the oldest thing anybody has ever built to be lost in

The Garden Maze

The earliest garden mazes were hedges, cut in a pattern that could be walked from one side to the other by somebody who knew the way and by nobody else. They were planted rather than built, which means the first ones were accidental, and the word that came to mean a confusing set of passages is the same word that came to mean a knot in a sheep's fleece. A maze and a meander are two different shapes and the same word.

What a hedge maze does that a stone one cannot is grow. It thickens, it goes bare in winter, and it is pruned twice a year by somebody with a problem. A court of stone does not need a keeper, which is why the version most people have met is the version that lasts: flat, hard, and exactly the same length in every direction.

Gate II · a maze is only legible twice a year

A Hedge in Winter

A yew hedge cut to a pattern is a piece of topiary that happens to be walkable, and in a hard winter the shape of it changes twice a year. In summer you cannot see over it and the hedges are all one green. Between December and February the leaves come off, the light comes through the sides of the hedge rather than the top, and for a few weeks the whole pattern is visible at once from the house.

Those weeks are the reason a hedge maze is cut the way it is. The pruner is following a drawing that was made in the winter, standing in the middle of the maze, walking the route. Every hedge decision comes back to a single question about where somebody stood in the cold with a drawing, and that is why the pattern of a hedge maze is a route drawn by somebody lost, rather than a pattern designed in advance and copied.

Gate III · the word labyrinth, and what it meant first

The Labyrinth of Crete

The word labyrinth comes from a structure in Crete with a single door, a single room, and a dead Minotaur in the middle of it, and it is a completely different thing from a maze. A labyrinth is a corridor you cannot get lost in, because there is only one path and it does not fork. A maze forks everywhere. The two words have been used for each other for two thousand years and they describe opposite difficulties.

The distinction matters when a path is drawn. A labyrinth route is one continuous line and you follow it and you arrive. A maze route is a line that has been chosen from many, and the drawing records a decision, not a description. The ochre line on the court above is the second kind: it is the route somebody took, not the only route there is.

Gate IV · flags, joints, and the water that finds them

Stone, Water, Tread

A paved court is laid in courses so that the joints run in straight lines, and those joints are the whole surface design of the place. Water finds them first, then moss, then the small plants that grow in the north side of a joint, and after a few winters the pavement has a drawing on it that nobody laid. On the court above, that drawing has been given three patches of moss and left to look accidental.

Tread is the other one. A route across a paved court wears the stones in an arc, and it wears them fastest in the middle of the route where people step, so a heavily used passage across a courtyard eventually shows a pale, slightly dished band between the joints. The band is the route, and it is the oldest surveying instrument there is.

Gate V · the cut goes in from the entrance every time

The Drawn Line

Chalk on stone takes about forty seconds to brush off and about three years to wear away, and in between it is the cheapest accurate thing in architecture. A route chalked on a court does not start at the middle and it does not start at the gate. It starts at the entrance, because that is where the person who is lost is standing, and it finishes at the gate, because that is the only part they have already decided.

Every time a different gate is chosen on this page the line starts again at the north door. That is deliberate and it is the signature of the whole header: the court does not move, the gates do not move, and the only thing that changes is a line being cut from the beginning while you watch.

Gate VI · six gates, and the shortest one is the one you take

Getting Out Again

Every maze has a hard way through and an easy way through, and the hard way is the one nobody can be shown. A person who is shown the solution keeps the solution, and a person who is sent in with a piece of string finds the way out alone and remembers the way out. This is the entire argument for building the thing badly on purpose, and it is why the shortest route on this court is drawn first, every time.

There is a practical version of the same thought. Emergency exits are marked for the people who are frightened, not the people who are lost, and a sign that only a person who already knew the way could follow is a decoration. The gate on this page is the one that says where you are, not the one that shows you how to leave.
