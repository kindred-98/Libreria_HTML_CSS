Frame 01 · Mode P

Program mode

Hand the camera the shutter speed and it will find an aperture, hand it the aperture and it will find a shutter speed. In daylight on 400 ISO it settles almost every time at f/5.6 with a shutter between 1/125 and 1/500, which is a perfectly good answer for anybody learning the craft and a bad answer for anybody who knows what the light is doing.

The useful habit that survives leaving program mode is to check what the camera chose and ask why. If it picked 1/15 on an overcast afternoon, something is wrong: either the film is two stops under, or you have moved a third of the way into the light.

Frame 02 · Mode A

Aperture priority

Choose the aperture and the camera matches it with a shutter speed. Open to f/1.8 for a portrait at dusk, stop to f/8 for a landscape where everything from two metres to infinity has to be sharp, and remember that each full stop of aperture costs one stop of shutter speed, so the light decides how much of both you can have.

Two thirds of the photographers in any club shoot in this mode and never read the numbers. The exposure readout on this bench shows the combination the camera would choose for the light the meter is reporting, so the dial is a way of learning the arithmetic rather than avoiding it.

Frame 03 · Mode S

Shutter priority

Choose the shutter speed and the camera finds the aperture. It is the mode for anything that moves: 1/500 to freeze a walking dog, 1/60 to let water fall as water, 1/15 to blur the leaves while the building stays put, and 1/4 for a veil of cloud over a landscape nobody is standing in.

The trap in this mode is the aperture the camera chooses in poor light. Below 1/30 on a 50 millimetre lens held in two hands, a quarter of the results are soft, and the camera has no way of knowing that you did not put the camera on a tripod.

Frame 04 · Mode M

Manual and the sunny sixteen

Nothing is decided for you, and the camera only tells you when you are wrong. The old rule still works on any light meter ever made: in bright sun, set f/16 and 1/ISO for a correct exposure, then half a stop under if you want the sky to hold its colour.

Manual is how a darkroom print is repeatable. Two exposures of the same scene at f/8 and 1/125 and a third frame at f/8 and 1/250 will bracket the negative in a way the meter can only guess at, and a bracket is what turns a scanned frame into a print with a shadow that is not black.

Frame 05 · Mode D

Depth of field

Depth of field is a lens, a distance and an aperture, and a fifty millimetre at f/1.8 focused at two metres has about twenty centimetres of sharp, which is thinner than most beginners expect. Stopping to f/8 makes it several metres, and the background falls apart at the same time.

Two habits fix most beginner portraits: move a step back, and raise the shutter speed rather than opening the lens. Depth of field is not something the camera can rescue at the print, but focus is: a soft photograph taken at f/1.8 is out of focus, and a soft photograph taken at f/8 looks like a mistake.

Frame 06 · Mode N

Night and the long frame

After dark the shutter stays open and the only question left is how long. On a tripod, thirty seconds for a lit street, two minutes for the floodlights on a building, and four minutes if a car comes through the frame. Anything longer and the emulsion starts to fog from the sky.

Bracket a night frame as carefully as a day one. Push the film two stops in development, print the negatives at 2.7 or 2.8, and pick the frame where the shadows held. A night photograph is made in the darkroom at least as much as on the street.
