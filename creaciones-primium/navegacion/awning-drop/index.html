Shop 01 · the cloth

The cloth

An awning is a piece of cloth that has been persuaded to be a piece of architecture. The classic material is cotton duck, a plain weave heavy enough to hold its own weight, and it is specified by weight rather than by thickness: 320 grams per square metre is the standard for a shopfront, 420 for a pub, and anything lighter than about 280 is a decoration. The weave is the reason. A tight plain weave sheds water and does not blow, and a loose one flaps itself to pieces in a single season.

Almost nothing on a high street is cotton any more. The modern equivalent is a coated acrylic or a PVC-backed polyester, and the change was driven less by fashion than by fire regulations and by the arrival of the roller that can furl a hundred kilos of canvas in forty seconds. The cloth is still the visible part of a very old idea, and it is still the only part of the shopfront that anybody touches.

Shop 02 · the roller

The roller

The cloth rolls onto a steel tube the width of the shop, and that tube is held in a box with a spring inside it, and the spring is the entire mechanism. A front roller bar attached to the free end of the canvas gives you something to pull down; the canvas is heavier than the bar, so the moment you let go the spring takes the weight and the cloth descends at a constant speed, which is why an awning never comes down fast enough to hurt you and never stops where you expect.

The spring box is the part that fails. It lives above a shopfront in a metal trough, full of rainwater in winter, and it is a closed unit that nobody lubricates on the grounds that it is a hundred feet up and nobody wants to be on a ladder. When it goes, the awning does not fail dramatically; it simply stops coming back up, and stays out in the wet for a winter, and the cloth dies of it.

Shop 03 · the valance

The valance

The valance is the strip of canvas hanging from the front bar, cut into scallops or a straight hem, and it exists for two reasons that have nothing to do with each other. Structurally, it finishes the front bar and hides the join. Commercially, it is the single largest flat advertising surface the shop owns, which is why the same fabric that sold cloth in 1908 now carries a phone number in the same place it has always been.

Height matters more than people expect. Too short and the valance reads as an afterthought. Too long and it cuts the daylight out of the window at exactly the height where a passer-by's eye is. Most of the good examples are about four hundred millimetres, which is roughly the height of a sign you can read from the far pavement without your chin having to rise.

Shop 04 · the shade

The shade

An awning is a shading device before it is anything else, and the gain is larger than most owners expect. A south-facing shop window in a hot city can lose most of its direct solar gain once the awning is down, and the temperature at the back of the stockroom follows within the hour. Retailers who treat an awning as decoration are usually the ones who end up paying for air conditioning they did not need.

The trade-off is daylight, and it is the reason the planning rules that once governed awnings were about transparency rather than shade. A solid awning at two and a half metres over a pavement changes the street for everyone standing in it, not only for the people who shop there, which is why many authorities cap the projection and require the valance to stop well short of the sightline of anybody using the opposite pavement.

Shop 05 · the wind

The wind

Wind is the only thing an awning genuinely fears. Cloth is light, an awning is a two by three metre membrane attached along one edge, and a gust turns that membrane into a sail with a four-hundred-kilogram spring pulling on it. The roller helps more than people give it credit for, because winding the canvas onto the tube takes up the slack and the drum diameter grows as it fills, which is the trick every awning has used since the nineteenth century to stay tight in a breeze.

What replaced the spring, in almost every new installation, is a sensor and a motor. The wind sensor closes the awning by itself, the rain sensor closes it before the shower arrives, and the sun sensor opens it when the shadow of the building finally moves off the glass. The system is not clever and it is not expensive, and the number of awnings left out in a storm is now a measure of who fitted one and who did not.

Shop 06 · the pitch

The pitch

A row of awnings is a row of shops making the same argument to the same person. Every one of them is saying: you may walk past me, but you may also stop, and here is the light you would be standing in, and here is my name at the height of your eye. It is the oldest retail technology there is and it has never been replaced, because it works on people who are walking, not stopping, which is the only audience worth advertising to.

Six shops on one street, one cloth at a time. When the canvas above a window is down, that shop is the one you are standing in front of, and the street reads it before you do. The pitch is not the window, which is only a room, and not the fascia, which is only a name. The pitch is the fabric, and the fabric tells you where you are.
