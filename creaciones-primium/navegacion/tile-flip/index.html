Tile Flip · six cards · two inks

Tile Flip

The header of this page is a card index of the kind that sat on every desk before anybody typed anything: a shallow drawer of oak, a brass rod through the middle, and six cards standing on end in it. The cards are unprinted card with the title blind-stamped into the face, which you can read but only just. Choose one and it comes up out of its slot, turns over in the hand, and shows the title printed in one of two inks. The slot it came from stays empty, so you can always see how far through the drawer you are.

Card I · the board

The Stock

A catalogue card is a piece of rag board: linen and cotton rag beaten to pulp, not wood, because a card has to take a rubber stamp without splitting along the grain. It is thick, around three hundred grams, and slightly fuzzy, and it drinks ink slowly enough to let you correct a mistake with a fingertip and quickly enough that the correction never quite disappears.

Thickness is the whole design constraint. Too thin and the card flops over the edge of the drawer and hides the card behind it. Too thick and the drawer holds four instead of six. The six cards in the index at the head of this page are the standard weight, cut a fraction oversize so that the sixth one still stands up on its own with the drawer open.

Card II · the fibre

The Grain

Paper has a direction and it is not optional. The pulp settles on the wire in a loose mat, and the mat dries into fibres that all lie roughly the same way, so a card is stiffer across the grain than along it and will curl towards the grain every time it gets wet. Turn a card over and the curl reverses, which is why the two faces of the six in this index are printed from sheets that were turned together.

A drawer full of cards is therefore a drawer full of springs. Six cards standing on end with the grain the wrong way round will bow towards each other and jam by the end of a humid afternoon. The old card makers dealt with it by running the grain the same way on every sheet and keeping the room dry, and the only other method anybody found was to stand the cards the other way up, which is not a solution so much as a different habit.

Card III · the impression

The Face

Before a card is printed it is stamped. A steel plate with the title cut in relief is pressed into the damp board from above, and what comes off the press is a depression with a shine on its shoulder: an emboss, not an ink. You read it by turning the card across the light, the way you read braille without knowing you are doing it, and after a few hundred impressions the depression in the middle of the drawer is worn smooth.

Embossing is a decision about where the ink will not go. It marks the card as a card, it gives a fingertip something to find in a dark drawer, and it survives being handled for sixty years, which no ink on the same card does. The blind stamping on the six cards here is why the front reads at arm's length and the printed face, on the back, reads properly at reading distance.

Card IV · the two colours

The Two Inks

A shop that printed one colour could be wrong in one way. A shop with two inks can be wrong in two, and every printer who has done it has settled on the same pair for the same reason: one dark enough to be the authority and one bright enough to be the exception. Here it is oxblood, a lake pigment that dries matte and darkens as it goes off, and a teal mixed from a copper salt that starts bright and greens over about a decade.

Both cards in a pair are printed on the same press on the same run, alternating, so a drawer always contains both. That is not a rule anybody wrote down; it is what happens when a job is small enough to print in one go and the press operator does not want to clean up between every impression. The six cards at the head of this page alternate for the same reason, and the card you are reading is printed in the ink its position in the drawer earned it.

Card V · the drawer

The Index

The drawer is oak, shallow, and open at the top, and the cards stand on end in it held by a brass rod that passes through a punched hole near the foot of every card. The rod is what makes the thing an index rather than a box: lift the drawer out, tip it forward, and the contents come down in order instead of falling out. The hole is punched before the card is stamped, and a card that has been re-punched shows it.

Cards are pulled from the front, never from the middle, and the ones behind are pushed along to fill the gap, which is why the drawer has a little brass pusher on a spring. A card that has been out and put back goes in at the back. So the drawer keeps an order that is really an order of use, and the empty slot in the index above is the only honest record of where the reading is.

Card VI · the reverse

The Turn

Turning a card is a thing the hand does without consulting the eye: thumb on the face, two fingers behind, a twist of the wrist, and the reverse comes up already being read. It is one of the oldest pieces of physical shorthand there is, and it costs a fraction of a second. Nothing has to unlock, nothing has to load, and there is no state to get into; the card is simply turned and the other side is there.

That is all this index does. Choose a card and it comes up out of its slot and turns over in the hand, printed, in one of the two inks, and the six cards behind it stay shut and embossed. Read a different card and the one you were holding goes back into its slot, empty spaces and all, and another comes up. The rod does not care which one it is. It never does.

The index is the navigation: every card is a link into the notes below, and the card out of its slot is the chapter you are reading.
