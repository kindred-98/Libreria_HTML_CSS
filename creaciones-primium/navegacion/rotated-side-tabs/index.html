The glaze index

Five families of surface, catalogued from the workshop ledgers of the Fennery kiln. Choose a glaze on the left rail; the panel below is the record for that surface, with its firing schedule and the pieces that carry it.

Record 01

Wood ash glaze

Firing 1280 °CReductionSince 1961

The first surface the workshop ever mixed, and still the least obedient. Ash from the drying stove is washed for nine days, sieved, then blended with local feldspar and a little iron-bearing slip; at cone 11 the mixture runs, and every run is a decision the kiln makes without consultation.

Because the glaze thins at the rim and pools at the foot, one pot carries three colours: a dry olive at the lip, a warm amber on the shoulder, and a glassy green-black where it gathered. No two pieces from a single firing have ever matched, which is why the ledger records firing numbers rather than patterns.

Record 02

Celadon

Firing 1300 °CHeavy reductionSince 1969

A jade-green surface that exists only while the kiln starves it of oxygen. The iron in the glaze is reduced from ferric to ferrous in the last hour of the firing, and if the damper is opened too early the whole batch turns the colour of weak tea.

The workshop guards its celadon recipe in a locked notebook and has changed it twice: once in 1974 to remove a local clay that had begun to drift, and once in 1998 to make the glaze survive a modern electric kiln. The oldest pieces show a crazing pattern so fine it is visible only when the rim is lit from behind.

Record 03

Tenmoku

Firing 1290 °COxidationSince 1972

An iron glaze so saturated that it stops being a colour and becomes a depth. Where it lies thick it is black; where it thins over an edge, the iron crystallises into a rust-brown line the width of a thumbnail, a feature the Japanese catalogues call a hare's fur.

Tenmoku is the workshop's most photographed surface and its least reproducible. The cooling curve decides the crystallisation, and the cooling curve depends on the weather: a cold night in the yard draws the heat out of the kiln too fast and leaves the surface matt instead of glossy.

Record 04

Raku

Firing 980 °CPost-firing reductionSince 1981

Taken from the kiln with tongs while still glowing, raku ware is plunged into a bin of sawdust and newspaper. The smoke writes the pattern: carbon is forced into every unglazed surface, and the glazed areas crack and blush where the flame touches them directly.

It is the only process in the catalogue that cannot be repeated, and the only one the workshop performs outdoors, in winter, with the doors open. Each piece is signed while it is still warm enough to take a fingernail mark in the clay at the foot.

Record 05

Salt glaze

Firing 1260 °CSalt introduced at cone 9Since 1994

Common salt thrown into the hottest corner of the kiln vaporises and finds the silica on every exposed surface, forming a sodium glass that settles as an orange-peel texture. The kiln itself is glazed by the process, which is why the workshop fires salt vessels last in a season.

The result is warm, slightly uneven and unmistakably a product of the room it was made in. Pieces fired in the old bottle kiln carry a heavier deposit on the side facing the firebox; the workshop marks that side with a small impressed dot so that buyers can tell which way the pot faced.

Visiting

Reading a surface

Every vessel in the index can be handled in the reading room on the first Thursday of the month. Cotton gloves are provided, though the workshop has always preferred bare hands: ash and salt glazes were made to be touched, and the wear on a tea bowl's foot tells you more than any label.

Requests are taken in writing and answered within five working days. Please quote the inventory number exactly as printed above the record, for example V-1978-16, so that the registrar can find the piece without opening the whole ledger.
