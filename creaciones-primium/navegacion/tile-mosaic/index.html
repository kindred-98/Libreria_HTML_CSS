Tile Mosaic

The header of this page is a square of azulejos set in lime, the way a fountain basin or a lavatory wall is finished. Nothing here is printed: each tile is pressed, fired and painted by hand. The one for the chapter you are reading is lifted clear of the bed, turned up on its edge so you can read it, and set in the middle of the field, and the other five keep the pattern together around it.

Tile I

The Biscuit

A tile starts as a ball of red earth and a ball of white, and a tile is nothing but the meeting of the two at exactly the right moment. Too much earth and it will not take a glaze; too much white and it will fire to the colour of a bone. The potter does not measure this, so every workshop ends up with its own biscuit colour, and the tiles in a wall made over two hundred years are visibly from two different hands.

The tile is pressed, not thrown. It comes out of the mould a thick slab that is cut to size, rested for a week, and then ground down on a wet stone until the face is flat and the edges are true. It is slow, and it is the only way to get six hundred pieces of the same thing.

Tile II

The Glaze

Painting on raw glaze is a bad habit that took four hundred years to unlearn, and it is still done. The paint sits on the surface, the surface is slightly different from the body underneath, and in fifty years the two part company and the design comes off in flakes. Painting into the glaze, under it, is the whole argument of the technique and the reason an old tiled wall can be scrubbed with a wire brush and come back clean.

The blue is a copper oxide that only shows blue in an oxidising kiln, so the tiles go in on trays with the same space between them all round. Two tiles that touch in the kiln come out one blue and one green, which is the reason the good panels of a wall are never quite the same shade twice.

Tile III

Lifting a Tile

To lift a tile without breaking it you tap the grout, not the tile. A light hammer on the face of the joint goes through the lime, which is soft and still has water in it, and the tile comes up whole. A chisel goes under the edge and levers against the bed, which is faster and takes a corner off every fourth tile.

The tile is set aside in the same order it came off, because when the wall is made up again the pattern has to close. A field of tiles is a sequence, and the only thing worse than a lost tile is a lost tile in the middle of it.

Tile IV

The Middle Piece

Every tiled field has one tile in the middle that the pattern was built around, and that tile is usually the one that came from the wrong batch. In a fountain basin the centre often holds a face, or a set of initials, or a date, and it is always very slightly the wrong colour for the wall around it.

What matters is that it is the piece everything else closes toward. In the field at the head of this page the tile you are reading is lifted out of its place and set in the middle, and the five around it hold the ring: read a different chapter and a different tile takes the centre.

Tile V

Grout and Cure

Lime grout goes on in two passes. The first is thin and sloppy and is allowed to shrink inside the joint, which takes about a week and leaves a gap in the bottom third of every joint. The second is packed in properly and is struck off with a wet sponge, and the sponge is rinsed after every few tiles, because a sponge that is not rinsed lays the lime dust back on the face and dulls the glaze.

The wall is then left alone for twenty-one days. Grout that is disturbed while it is still setting comes out of the joint and takes the edge of the tile with it, and there is no way to put an edge back.

Tile VI

Laying the Field

Start at the centre and work outwards in rings, and the wall will close. Start at a corner and work along, and the last tile in the field will be the wrong size for the space it has to go into, and there is nowhere for that error to hide. Every tiler knows this and every beginner is surprised by it once.

The field at the head of this page is laid the same way, which is why it has a centre you can see from across the room: a hundred and forty years of a job whose only hard part is choosing where to begin.

The field is the navigation: every tile is a link into the chapters below, and the lifted tile is the chapter you are reading.
