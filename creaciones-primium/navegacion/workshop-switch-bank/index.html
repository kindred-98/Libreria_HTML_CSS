Where every machine in the building arrives first, and where half of them leave again with a cable and a bag of terminals.

The bench is deliberately unglamorous: a good multimeter, an insulation tester that is calibrated every January, a variac with a fourteen amp fuse, and a wall of labelled terminals that took two summers to make honest. A motor that arrives humming and quiet leaves humming and quiet, and the fault is found by measuring, not by swapping parts until somebody guesses right.

Most of the work is three measurements and a coffee. Insulation to earth, balance between phases, current draw under a known load. When all three are inside tolerance the machine goes back to work, and the customer is charged for the hour it took to prove that nothing was wrong, which is a conversation the workshop has learned to have kindly and without apologising.

Every rewinding job begins with the same argument about the old coils. Pull them out without damage and the lamination survives; cut them out in a hurry and you will find a shorted turn three slots deeper than you thought, and the new coil will be a new coil for six weeks. The rule in this shop is that no coil comes out until the slot has been photographed from three angles.

The winder is a 1978 machine with a hand-built controller that a retired apprentice maintained for twenty years. It counts turns, warns when the tension wanders, and stops if you thread it badly. It is the most reliable piece of equipment in the building and it is also the only one that cannot be bought, which is why the workshop has never sold it.

The rig lives in the corner with the door that sticks, and everything that goes through it comes out with a piece of paper. Hoses are tested at one and a half times their working pressure, which is not a symbolic gesture: a hose that has been clamped to 375 bar for a minute tells you plenty about the crimp.

The most common failure in this building is not hydraulic at all. It is a machine that stops because a float switch in a dirty tank is stuck, three valves upstream of the problem. The workshop keeps a jar of failed float switches on a shelf as a teaching aid, and every apprentice is made to open one before being allowed near a pump.

Armarios eléctricos

Control panels rebuilt from photographs and old notes, one door at a time, with every wire re-terminated.

Puertos libres
20 %
Seccionadores
4 por cuadro
Cable de potencia
10 mm²
Documentación
Impresa, en la caja

A panel arrives as a box of terminals and a photograph of the front of the old one, and the job is to guess the rest without guessing badly. Everything gets a new ferrule, a new label printed on a machine that takes forty minutes to learn, and a line number that matches the drawing. The drawing is printed and folded into the door pocket, because a schematic that lives in somebody's memory is a schematic that dies with them.

Commissioning takes a full day for a panel that took six hours to build. Every circuit is proved with the motors uncoupled, every overload is set by nameplate instead of by habit, and the panel is left with the doors open for twenty-four hours before the customer sees it. Rush jobs are accepted, quietly, and they are always the ones that come back.

The store is a room with steel shelving and a card index in a drawer, and it works because every bin has a label that was typed when the machine was installed rather than when the part was bought. If the label is wrong the whole system is wrong, so the rule is that a bin gets relabelled the day the contents change, not at the next inventory.

What the shelf does not have is ordered in under a week, which is why the workshop keeps a small fleet of machines that are no longer made and a large stock of their consumables. The bushing, the seal and the bearing are cheap and always available. The whole motor is neither, and the shelf is the reason this building exists.

Two hundred and fifteen parts in the book and one thing stands out: most of the failures are a switch, a float or a terminal that was never checked because nobody had time to check it. The workshop charges for the hour it takes to look, which customers accept without complaint once they have seen the second visit avoided.

The rest of the argument is about parts. A motor that has been rewound twice needs new bearings, new seals and a new cooling fan, and the honest quote includes all three. A workshop that replaces only what failed gets a returning customer and a machine that fails again in six months, which is a bad trade for everybody in the room.
