Orbita Mk II, in the hand.

A belt drive turned into a direct drive, a plinth into a shell you can hold, and a tonearm that fits in a coat pocket. The six keys on the remote in the corner of the page are the whole presentation: press one and the page moves to that chapter, exactly as a remote moves a record.

Move the pointer and the remote follows it, half a beat behind, the way a heavy object follows your hand. Reach for it with the keyboard instead and it glides back to its dock and holds still, so the focus ring is always somewhere legible.

Chapter 01

A shell you can hold in one hand

The Mk I was a plinth: a slab, a lid, four feet. The Mk II is a die-cast shell with a hollow spine, which takes the mass out of the middle and leaves it where it belongs, in the platter. At four point one kilograms it can be carried from a shelf to a table with two hands and set down without a wobble.

The casting is unpainted on the underside so the tool marks stay visible, and the top is a fine bead-blasted anodised finish in three colours. The rubber feet are replaceable with a coin, which sounds like a small thing until the first one goes in 2031.

Chapter 02

The platter that refuses to start too early

Start-up torque is the whole argument for a direct drive, and the number that matters is how long the motor waits before the record is at speed. The Mk II reaches full speed in 0.42 seconds at 33 and a third, and the electronic brake is designed so that lifting the arm does not slow it down.

The platter weighs two point four kilograms and is machined from a single billet, with a felt mat that is glued rather than laid so it cannot creep. Its inertia is measured at 0.62 kilograms per square centimetre, which is the figure quoted on the box and the figure the guarantee refers to.

Chapter 03

A tonearm that folds into a coat pocket

The arm is a gimbal bearing with the mass in the base, which gives an effective mass of twelve grams and a static balance of about half a degree. It folds to the left, retracts, and locks, and the whole sequence is one movement of the lift arm rather than three separate handles.

Fitting a counterweight is a five minute job and the manual is honest about the fact. With the supplied alignment protractor and the four gram ring on the far end, a first-time owner can set tracking force and anti-skate without a turntable dealer, and the arm is left floating when it is packed.

Chapter 04

The cartridge, and why it is fixed

One cartridge, one stylus, fitted at the factory and not replaceable by the owner. That is a deliberate narrowing: a fixed moving-coil cartridge with a fine elliptical tip gives a consistent result for owners who would otherwise spend a year comparing, and the replacement programme is handled by the workshop at a fixed price.

Output is point four millivolts per centimetre, which suits a phono stage with a fifty-ohm input, and the tracking window runs from point two to four point five grams. The needle is inspected under a stereo microscope at the end of every build, and the reading is written on the inside of the lid.

Type
Moving coil, elliptical stylus
Output
0.4 mV per cm at 1 kHz
Tracking
0.2 to 4.5 g window

Chapter 05

Power from a socket, or from a cell

The Mk II runs from USB-C and can run for nine hours on an internal battery, which is not a specification anybody asked for and is the feature the first three hundred owners mention most. The motor is held to speed by a closed loop that samples the optical strobe on the platter edge forty times a second.

Battery charge is reported on the remote rather than on a light, because a light on a turntable is a thing you cannot see from a listening chair. The strobe dots around the platter are for looking, the remote is for knowing, and the two were designed to be used in different rooms.

USB-C 5 V, 1.5 A minimum
Battery
3.7 V, about 9 hours
Control

Chapter 06

A box that survives the courier

Every unit is packed in a double box: a corrugated outer, a moulded pulp tray, and a felt-lined lid that takes the stylus weight while the arm is in transit. The arm is shipped floating rather than clamped, which protects the bearing and is the reason the transit damage rate is 0.4 per cent.

Inside the box you find the remote, a USB-C cable, a hex key, the alignment protractor, a spare silicone mat and a card with the reading from the end-of-build inspection. Serial numbers are stamped into the underside of the shell, not printed on the box, so the box can be thrown away without losing the record.
