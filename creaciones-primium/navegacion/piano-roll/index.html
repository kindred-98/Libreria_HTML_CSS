Measure 1 · the roll

The paper that plays

A roll of music is a long strip of paper with a hole down each edge and a pattern of perforations across the middle. Pulled at the right speed through a machine, it plays itself. Nobody is required to know any music to make it work, and nobody is required to know any music to play the result either, which is exactly why the idea was immediately, and permanently, controversial.

What is on the paper is a decision about sound made long before anybody heard it. The pattern can be written by hand or arranged by ear, but either way the performer is gone by the time the first note comes out of the machine, replaced by a spool turning at a steady speed that does not care how the room feels about the phrase.

Measure 2 · the holes

The perforation

The holes are small, rectangular, and placed on a grid. Whether a hole exists decides whether a note is played, how long it is held and how loudly it is struck, so a few millimetres of paper carry an entire performance. The margins at the edges are for the sprockets that keep the roll moving at a constant speed, and they are the reason a roll never jams halfway through a bar.

Read a roll and the logic is immediately visible. A held note is a long slot, a staccato note is a short one, a chord is several at once, and the whole thing can be understood by anybody willing to spend an hour with a pencil and a roll that has already been played too often to be wanted.

Measure 3 · the machine

The reproducer

The reproducer is a cabinet with a vacuum pump, a regulator, a spool and a row of small levers, one for each of the eighty-eight notes. The roll turns, the pins underneath the paper lift a lever, and the lever opens a valve that lets air from the reservoir into a pipe. No electricity was involved for decades, which meant no amplifier, no speaker, and a machine that could be carried in two hands.

Because the cabinet has to be loud enough to be worth owning, the whole thing sits inside a wooden box and is tuned by adjusting how much air the reservoir is allowed to hold. The regulation is the difference between a reproducer that sounds like a piano and one that sounds like a very organised noise.

Measure 4 · the marks

The codes on the paper

Different makers cut different shapes into the same grid, so a roll for one make of reproducer is meaningless to another. Roll companies were serious businesses with serious catalogues, and a pianist in nineteen-twenties New York could choose a roll the way a modern player chooses a record, on the strength of an arrangement and a name on the box.

The codes were also used to smuggle things through. Rolls were the most boring object in the world and were rarely opened, which made them a good place for a note, an address, or an entire song that somebody was not allowed to record. The perforations carried whatever the owner needed them to carry.

Measure 5 · the shelves

The roll library

By the nineteen-twenties the roll had become the largest single body of recorded popular music in the world, and the library of a serious house held tens of thousands of them in wooden cabinets, catalogued by composer, by band, and by mood. A pianist who wanted to know what a new tune sounded like before playing it in public simply asked for the roll and spent ten minutes with it.

Players were performers in their own right. A good one could make a tired roll sound like a good piano, could survive a bad take, and could accompany a singer on a Tuesday night while the studio ran out of songs. Almost nobody now knows their names, which is a strange way to treat the people who spent a career interpreting somebody else's paper.

Measure 6 · the return

The player as performer

At the end of this roll the spools stop and the felt lifts off the paper, and the last thing the machine does is nothing at all. Every argument about the roll comes back to that: it could play accurately, it could play loudly, it could play a great deal of music, and it could not once decide that the phrase wanted to be slower tonight.

So the paper kept turning and the playing kept coming back to whoever was in the room. The roll is a very good record of a decision and a very poor instrument for making one, which is probably why it sounded like music at all.
