Lamp 01 · the lamp

The lamp

An alley needs one light, and it needs it high up where nobody can reach it. That is the entire specification. The bracket goes on the wall above head height, the globe is a sphere of glass so that the filament is visible from below and not from the side, and the lamp is wired into the building rather than into a pole because there is no room for a pole. Everything else in street lighting is a refinement on those three sentences.

The reason the light is a small bright point rather than a broad even wash comes down to the eye. A dark adaptation takes about twenty-five minutes and is destroyed by a single bright source, so a street that is uniformly bright is a street in which nobody sees anything. The eye is built to work with contrast: one pool of light, then true dark, then the next pool. What you can actually make out at three in the morning is the lit patch, and everything beyond it is inference.

Lamp 02 · the ironwork

The ironwork

Cast iron is the material because it is the only cheap thing that can be put outside for eighty years without a coat of paint every spring. A bracket is cast in one piece with the wall plate, the scroll and the collar, then bolted up and grouted into the brick. The scrolls are not decoration in the usual sense; they exist to put the material where it is stiffest, which is a long way from the wall, and the ornament is simply the engineering drawing rendered in iron.

What rust does to cast iron is worse than it does to steel. The surface scales, the scale traps water, and the metal underneath goes back to being iron ore in a slightly more inconvenient place than before. Repairs have been done for a century with nothing but coal tar and a paintbrush, and the reason so many of them still look sound is that nobody has ever thought to specify anything else.

Lamp 03 · the glass

The glass

A globe is a diffuser, not a lens. Its job is to take a filament that is almost a point and turn it into something you can look at without flinching, and it does that by scattering at the surface: cheap pressed glass with a slightly rough inner face, which is why old globes look frosted and new ones look clear. Clear globes were a marketing decision in the nineteen thirties, sold as better visibility, and they were right about visibility and wrong about everything else.

Glass is also the failure point. A globe on an alley bracket is hit by something, or frozen onto, or simply walked into by a ladder, and it is replaced in an afternoon because it is cheap. The consequence is that no two globes in a row are the same make after thirty years, and the slight difference in the tone of each one is a fair record of how much the street has been knocked about.

Lamp 04 · the count

The count

Six lamps is not a decorative number. It is roughly the minimum that gets you from one end of a short alley to the other without a pool of darkness in the middle, and it is the number that most alleys actually have. Spacing is set by geometry rather than by budget: too close and the pools merge into one flat wash, too far and there is a black band at head height where somebody is standing and cannot be seen.

The row lights in order. Reading it from the near end, the lamps behind you are still on and the ones ahead are dark, so the count of lit globes is the count of how far you have come, and it is legible in about a quarter of a second by anybody walking. It is the cheapest position display ever built, and almost nobody notices it consciously, which is the highest praise that can be given to a piece of street design.

Lamp 05 · the dark

The dark

For most of the twentieth century the argument was about crime, and the argument was won loudly and not always honestly. Studies that followed street lighting through a period of twenty years found a real reduction in recorded offences and a much smaller effect once the police numbers were excluded, which is the usual finding about anything that is measured and then acted upon.

The stronger argument turned out to be about fear rather than statistics. What people want from a light is not safety exactly but the ability to see somebody coming, and an evenly lit street with nothing to see does not deliver that, while a pool of light at eye level does. The design that survived is the one that was never optimised: bright, high, spaced out, and leaving the dark where the dark was anyway.

Lamp 06 · the outage

The outage

Old lamps were on photocells and a time clock, and the time clock was the thing that failed. It kept the lamps burning all afternoon in June, which wasted a fifth of the budget and made a small number of residents who worked nights extremely unpopular. The replacements are LEDs on a photocell and a wireless node, and the gain is real: the running cost has collapsed and the globes last longer than the brackets they are screwed to.

What has not changed is the reporting. A dark lamp at the end of an alley is a thing people telephone about, and the telephone still works, and it is still the only mechanism most districts have for finding out that a lamp is out. A row of six where the fifth has failed is not an aesthetic problem. It is a gap in the walk, and everybody who uses that alley can tell you exactly where it is.
