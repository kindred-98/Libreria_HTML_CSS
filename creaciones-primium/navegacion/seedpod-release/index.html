01

Valve

A dry fruit is a container with a mechanism, and the mechanism is usually a seam. The carpel that made the seed dries, shrinks and builds tension along a line of weaker cells, and when the tension beats the strength of the seam the pod splits along it and all of that stored strain is released in a fraction of a second.

The pod at the top of this page works the same way. Open it, and the six seeds swing out along the arc the fruit built into them, and stay there until you fold it back.

02

Drying

Drying is not preservation, it is preparation. The seed loses water until it reaches a few per cent of its fresh weight, the metabolism drops to a crawl, and the cells that were carrying water take on a glassy, chemically stable state that makes them far harder to damage.

What a dry seed cannot do is germinate, and this is the point. The tissues that would have to grow are built as dry, folded primordia, held in place by rigid but slightly flexible walls, and they are waiting for three things at once rather than one.

03

Splitting

Splitting is what makes dry fruits work, and it is also what makes them dangerous. An explosive pod can throw seeds several metres, and a few species can throw them far enough to be heard, which is why the whole family is called the legumes in casual speech and the beans in the garden.

Few seeds travel well by wind, so the pod spends its effort on range instead. The curve of the valve and the position of the seeds on it are calibrated, which is why the fan in the pod above is not a straight spray but a deliberate arc.

04

Wind

Some seeds are built for distance and some are built for living where they land. A sycamore seed is a rotor with an airfoil, tuned to descend slowly enough to catch a thermal and turn for hundreds of metres; a beech seed is heavy and just falls, and its seedling simply tolerates shade.

The trade is a matter of how crowded the parent is. In a dense stand, distance is nearly worthless, because the site you land on is already occupied, and the correct strategy is to put resources into surviving in the dark rather than into travelling.

05

Scar

Most seeds will not germinate on the spot. They need a break in the seed coat, called a scar, and for a large family of them the scarification is the fire. Heat in the soil, repeated freeze and thaw, or the passage of an animal through the litter is what makes the coat permeable enough to let water in.

It is an arrangement between plants and the disturbance that clears ground, and it means a field left alone for a decade can be carrying a seed bank that will not express itself until something burns or a plough goes through.

06

Spring

When the conditions finally line up, the first thing a seed does is imbibe, and it takes up water fast enough to crack its own coat from inside. The radicle emerges first, always, before any leaf, and it must reach reliable moisture within days or the seedling is finished.

It is a bad bet made well, and the prize is disproportionate: a plant that starts from a dry seed can put its first root down before the canopy above it has opened at all, and the whole architecture of its life is decided in that first week.

Open the pod and its seeds fan out. The lit seed is the chapter you are reading.
