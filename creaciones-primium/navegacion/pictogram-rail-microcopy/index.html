Slack Lane Works, on foot

The works closed in 1979 and opened as a trail four years later. Eight buildings are open, one boiler is still lit, and the walk takes about ninety minutes if you read every plate. The rail on the left carries the stops; the line under it tells you what you are about to walk into, and the counter remembers how many you have already done.

01

The Foundry

Tap hole, 1897. Steel on a 24 hour cycle.

The cupola on the north end has not been relined since 1971, and the visitor route runs beneath it on purpose. On a working shift the floor was never quiet, but it was never loud either: the noise lived in the sand yard, where moulders broke out castings and the shatter carried three hundred metres.

What remains is the chain, the moulding boxes and the pattern store door, and the story they tell is about heat. Steel entered the works as pig and scrap, left as rails, bars, sheet and castings for its own repair shop, and the proportion of the site given over to making the tools that made the tools is still visible in the floor plan.

02

The Rolling Mill

Two mill pairs, 1903. Six millimetres in, 0.8 out.

The roughing and finishing pairs ran at different speeds, and the drive was a rope from a 400 horsepower engine through a layshaft on the roof. The mill house is the loudest room on the trail and the guide will let you stand at the inspection plate for as long as you like while the demonstration pair turns.

A plate from this mill is still in the roof of the office building, put up in 1911 to show the visitor what the works could make. It is four millimetres thick and it weighs a little over two tonnes, which the sign at the end of the gantry explains better than a paragraph can.

03

The Loading Quay

Quay 2 still takes a wagon at spring tide.

Slack Lane is a canal cut, not a river frontage, which is why the quay is a straight wall with a crane on a gantry and a level that changes twice a day. Barges brought ore and coal in and took bar and castings out, and the whole site was designed around the moment when a boat had to be loaded in a single tide.

The weighbridge hut survives at the top of the slip. Nothing in it is original except the frame, and the frame is a good deal heavier than it needs to be, because whoever built it assumed the scales would be argued with.

04

The Boiler House

One of three boilers is still lit at six bar.

Two Lancashire boilers and one water-tube boiler stood in this house, and the water-tube one was bought second hand in 1926 against the advice of the engineer who had to run it. It is the one that is lit now, at six bar, to prove that the flues and the water treatment plant still work.

Stoking was a craft with its own vocabulary: a fireman was a stoker, the man who cleaned the fires was a banksman, and a good bank could raise steam pressure faster than the engine could take it. The chimney drew hard enough to set up its own draught, which is why the boiler house was built tall and tight around the flues.

05

The Works Railway

Four point eight kilometres, two locomotives on the rails.

The branch left the main line at Slack Lane Junction, ran under the mill and stopped at the pattern shop, the foundry and the yard, and closed in 1968. Two locomotives survive: one on the plinth at the junction, one that can be steamed on the first Saturday of the month if the boiler inspector is in a good mood.

Almost everything that moved inside the works moved by rail, including the men. Two junctions per shift, a whistle to start work, and a permanent way laid to a gauge two inches narrower than standard because the rolling stock had been bought second hand in 1894.

06

The Bottling Plant

Nine hundred bottles an hour, crate for crate.

The works bottled its own soft drinks and its own beer for the canteen, and the plant was the only part of the site that ran at the pace of modern retail. It made nine hundred bottles an hour on a hand-fed cager, and the crates on the floor are the ones it shipped in, complete with a hand-stencilled batch number.

The trade was local, which is why the brand survived the works and the works did not. Cans of the same name are still sold four towns away, from a recipe that has never left the family, and the tasting table at the end of the plant gives away a small glass of it for nothing.

07

The Power House

Silent since 1979. The hall still answers to 88 decibels.

Two steam turbines drove alternators that ran the whole site, and the third machine in the hall is a diesel that started in 1961 and is now the only engine here that can be run without a boiler. Turning it over takes eleven minutes, and the guide will start it if the group is small and the weather is dry.

Switchgear cubicles line the north wall, hand-operated until 1953 and then motorised, with the old handles still fitted because nobody ever found a reason to remove them. The switchboard is locked and the reason is printed on the door: the insulation in the 1924 board is as brittle as slate.

08

The Pattern Shop

The drawings in the cases are the originals.

The last room on the trail holds the drawings, and they are the originals: every casting in the works was made from a pattern cut in beech in this room, and each one is labelled with the name of the moulder who cut it and the date he finished.

Patterns were not filed by part number. They were filed by the job, in the order the job came in, which is why a shelf that looks chaotic to a modern storekeeper is in fact a complete record of eleven years of production, and why the foreman kept a second set of note books that nobody ever catalogued and that turned up in a skip in 1991.

Planning the visit

Entry is free on Fridays and Saturdays between ten and half past four, with the last guided round leaving at three. Groups of more than twelve are asked to write ahead, because the mill house and the boiler house hold a limited number of people comfortably.

Wear shoes with a grip. Three of the eight stops are on working floors with oil on them, and the pattern shop step is the same step that moulders have climbed since 1894. The guide will say so twice, and the second time is the one that matters.
