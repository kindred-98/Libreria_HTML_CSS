Glacier Traverse

Five marked stages climb from the treeline road to the summit ridge. Each waypoint carries a numbered post: the flag on the current stage is raised, the rest hang slack, and the survey thread between them is tensioned as far as the stage you have reached.

Stage 01 · Post 01

Treeline road

The route starts on the compacted road that ends at the last parking bay, and turns left onto a stony path cut into the larch belt. Water flows in a blue pipe beside the track for the first kilometre, which is the last reliable supply before the glacier.

Keep the signpost in sight for two hundred metres: the path leaves the road behind a rusted gate and climbs through dwarf pine, losing height slowly while it gains the first view of the moraine wall.

Stage 02 · Post 02

Moraine crossing

At the top of the larch belt the path reaches the lateral moraine, a wall of boulders dumped by a glacier that retreated here more than a century ago. Walk along the crest rather than across it: the side that faces the ice is stable, the other side is a loose slope that swallows a stride.

The post at the end of the crest carries a metal plate with the survey number and a small mirror so it can be seen in fog. Groups normally stack here for fifteen minutes to fix poles before the next pitch.

  • Gradient11 %
  • WaterNone
  • HazardRockslide, right

Stage 03 · Post 03

Icefall traverse

This is the serious part of the sheet. Roped teams cross the icefall from the moraine shoulder to the glacier plateau, following a line of painted wands that is only marked between late June and late August. The seracs here move, and the safe corridor is narrower than it looks from below.

There is no path on this stage, only bearing markers. If visibility drops, the rule of the range is to stop, build a snow anchor and wait; the plateau beyond is wide enough that losing an hour costs less than losing the route.

  • Gradient18 %
  • WaterMeltwater pools
  • HazardSerac fall

Stage 04 · Post 04

Summit ridge

The plateau ends in a snow crest that runs north for two kilometres, marked every hundred metres by bamboo wands with red tape. On a clear morning the whole range reads like a printed contour map, and the survey point on the high point is a tripod leg sunk into firn.

Turn back at the tripod unless the sheet says otherwise. The north face falls away at fifty degrees and there is no way down it that does not need a full day of rope work and a descent nobody has timed this season.

  • Gradient6 % ridge
  • WaterNone
  • HazardWind slab

Stage 05 · Post 05

Descent hut

The marked line falls away from the crest into a birch hollow and ends at a hut with a green roof, a woodpile under cover and a spring twenty metres below the door. The whole descent takes ninety five minutes on a good trail and rather longer with tired legs and heavy packs.

Sign the hut book. The keeper reads it to count the parties on the icefall, and the number on the board at the road head is derived from it, which is the only reason the count matters.

  • Gradient14 % down
  • WaterSpring, at hut
  • HazardWet roots

Reading the posts

A post is raised when it is the stage you are on, and stays raised for the length of that stage. The thread between posts is the survey line: it is tensioned up to the raised post, so its length is a rough measure of how much of the sheet lies behind you. Arrow keys walk the list of posts, Enter opens the stage in the log, and Escape returns you to the head of the page.
