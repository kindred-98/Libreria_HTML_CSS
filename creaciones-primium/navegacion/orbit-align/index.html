Station 01

Vector Field

A vector field is a rule for putting an arrow at every point of a space, and the drawing of it is always a lie of the useful kind. You can only put an arrow where you can fit one on the page, so you sample, and between the samples you assume the field does something continuous and unremarkable. Most of what looks like insight about a field is really a guess about the gaps.

Put an arrow at each point of a hill and the flow of water down it becomes obvious without moving anything. Put the arrows on a piece of iron filings and the same field appears in one glance, because the filings do the interpolation for you: each one is dragged into line by its neighbours, and the crowd near the pole is doing arithmetic about the strength rather than being the strength itself.

Station 02

Transfer

Change orbits without ever stopping, and the manoeuvre is called a transfer. A short burn prograde raises the far side of the ellipse, a second burn at the new apoapsis circularises it, and the whole thing can be done with the engine running once and the spacecraft pointing exactly where it already was pointing.

It is a good deal for an engine that only has to be trusted once. The cost sits in the tolerances: the second burn has to arrive at the right altitude, in the right direction, or the result is an orbit that misses the target by a few hundred kilometres and has to be trimmed afterwards. Orbital mechanics is mostly bookkeeping done in free fall, and the bookkeeping is unforgiving.

Station 03

Synodic

Two bodies in orbit meet at intervals that are almost never the same as either of their periods. The synodic period is the time it takes for them to line up again, and it is always longer than the shorter of the two and always shorter than the longer, which is the whole of the surprising part and the reason rendezvous is harder than it looks.

For the Earth and the Moon the synodic month runs about twenty-nine and a half days against a sidereal month of twenty-seven and a third. Those two extra days are the reason eclipses do not happen monthly, the reason a full moon rises roughly fifty minutes later each night, and the reason anybody who has watched the Moon over a month has already seen the discrepancy without noticing it.

Station 04

Nodal Passage

Every inclined orbit has two points where it crosses the plane of the equator, and the line joining them is the line of nodes. The orbit is not fixed to the Earth: the line of nodes precesses backwards around the equator, completing a circuit in about eighteen years for a low orbit, and in roughly half that for one that goes out to the moon.

Anyone designing a polar-orbiting constellation takes the precession into account early, because a node that wanders means a ground station that was overhead at launch is somewhere else entirely by the time the satellite is due to talk to it. It is a slow drift, and slow drifts are the ones that survive every review and then surprise the operations team years later.

Station 05

Station Keeping

A spacecraft left alone does not stay put. Drag from the atmosphere on the way down, solar radiation pressure on the way up, and the pull of the moon when the orbit is big enough to feel it all add up to a drift that a specification has to budget for in advance, usually in the shape of a small station-keeping burn every few months.

The burn is unglamorous and it is most of the cost. A thruster that fires for a few seconds to raise the far side by a couple of kilometres buys months of a stable geometry, and the engineers who schedule those firings are, in the end, the people keeping the whole thing from quietly leaving. Everything else about the mission is the interesting part.

Station 06

Deorbit

Ending an orbit is a controlled fall, and the design work is mostly about choosing where the last pieces land. Pull the perigee down into the upper atmosphere and the spacecraft sheds energy every revolution, the orbit gets rounder and lower by the same slow arithmetic, and the whole journey takes weeks instead of the ninety minutes a single direct plunge would take.

Weeks is the point. A direct reentry is a fireball with a target, and a slow spiral is a series of ordinary-looking passes, each one already proven, each one watched from a tracking station. The spacecraft spends its last months behaving like every other satellite up there, and the ground team keeps the schedule.

The six stations are the navigation: each one is a link to a chapter below, and the aligned station is the chapter you are reading.
