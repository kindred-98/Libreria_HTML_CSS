How the register is read

Each column is a summit and its height is drawn to the altitude in the margin, so the Mesa at 780 m is a stub and the Alabardero is a tower.

The column you are reading is pressed down into the page. A paper register writes its current entry in a smaller hand than the ones above it, and that is exactly what happens here.

The red band across the top is the print head. It slides over to sit on the current row and stops there, which is the only thing on this page that moves by itself.

A white cap means the ascent was verified with a photograph. There are four this season and there is not going to be a fifth.

Row 01 · 2 480 m · cara norte

Cima del Alabardero

The highest thing in the register and the reason the register exists. It was first climbed in 1911 by two surveyors who were looking for something else, found it by accident, and wrote in the margin that the summit was, in their words, of no use to anybody. It is still the most honest line in the whole book.

The north face is a straightforward quartzite slab for two hundred metres and then stops being one. The cap is a flat block about the size of a kitchen table with room for three people on it, which is why the verified-ascent rule was written the way it was: the photograph has to be taken from the table, and it has to have the register's own number plate in the frame.

Row 02 · 2 190 m · cresta sur

Cima Segunda

Named for what it is: the second thing you reach, and the second thing anybody reaches. The south ridge is a four hour walk and a twenty minute scramble at the end, and on a good weekend there are more people on it than on the Alabardero.

It is also the summit that most people in the valley have actually stood on, and there is a bar at the road end with a mountain painted on the sign. The register lists it second and the listing is a formality, which the man who wrote the first edition in 1938 appears to have understood, because he put a note under it in pencil that says, in effect, this is the mountain you will use.

Row 03 · 1 960 m · garganta este

Torre del Cuervo

Not a summit but a tower, and the register does not care about the difference, which is the correct position for a register to take. The east gully is a cleft between two walls with a chimney at the top of it, and the whole proposition is one sustained technical pitch that everybody describes afterwards, without exception, as uncomfortable.

There is a bird in it. A black bird the size of a small crow that nests on a ledge halfway up and has been there, by every account including the ones that have clearly been exaggerated, since before the first recorded ascent. It has no name in the register, because the register is for summits, and this is a working omission that everybody agrees with.

Row 04 · 1 640 m · couloir

Naso de Yunque

An anvil of rock with a couloir up the middle that is easy for two hundred metres and then turns into a problem of a completely different kind. The name is a translation of a word that means an anvil and also, in a neighbouring dialect, a bad mood, and somebody in the register made the pun on purpose.

It is wet for most of the year and dry for two weeks in August, and the whole climbing season of this valley now turns on whether those two weeks are dry. In a wet year the summit is unvisited and the row is left exactly as it was, with the altitude and the route and nothing else, which is the honest way to record a mountain nobody reached.

Row 05 · 1 210 m · hombro oeste

Banco de Laston

A long flat shoulder of limestone at the top of the tree line, which from a distance looks like the edge of a table and from close to looks like a field with a very good view. It is walked in an afternoon, children are taken up it, and there is a wall at the top with a hundred and forty years of initials on it.

The register records it because a register that leaves out the walkable summits is a register for a club rather than a register for a valley, and this one was written for the valley. The wall is the last entry in the book that was ever made by hand. The bookmaker tried three times to have it listed as a monument and was refused each time for reasons nobody has ever been given.

Row 06 · 780 m · pista

Mesa de la Sal

The last row and the shortest column, and the one that keeps the whole register honest. The Mesa is a flat shelf of white rock above the salt pans, reached by a track from the road in ninety minutes, and it is the first summit anybody in the valley under fifteen has ever been to.

There is nothing on top of it. The register still lists it, at the bottom, in the smallest type it owns, because the rule is that the list runs from the highest to the lowest and it runs to the end whatever is at the end. The man who wrote the first edition ruled a line under the sixth row and wrote, no further summits, which is either a statement about the mountain or a statement about the range, and nobody has been able to decide which.
