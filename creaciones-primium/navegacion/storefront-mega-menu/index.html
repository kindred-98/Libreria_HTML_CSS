Old Rope Walk · est. 1934

Five departments, one waxed bench

Pick a department from the aisle list above and the board changes with it: what is on the shelf today, the sizes we cut on the long table, and the price at the counter. Nothing here is a stock image; every line was counted on the last market day.

Aisle 01

Canvas goods

Everything on this aisle starts as a roll of cloth and finishes as something with a hem on it. The cotton duck is woven in Lancashire and arrives unbleached, so it starts stiff and softens over a season; the waxed cotton arrives already dressed and stiffens again if you leave it in a hot car.

We cut to size on the long table at the back, and the seam is a felled one with the raw edges turned in twice, which is slower than a single lap and is the only reason a cover we made in 2011 has not come apart at the corner.

Aisle 02

Shelter systems

Bell tents for two, ridge tents for four, and the tarpaulin shelter that is really a roof on four sticks. All of them are pegged, not staked, and all of them are pitched in a demonstration line outside the shop so that a stranger can walk through them before buying.

The hoops are ash, turned and left unpainted, and the canvas is cut so that the roof takes a set over the first wet night and then stops moving. A tent that has not been rained on is a tent that has not been tested, which is worth saying plainly in a shop.

Aisle 03

Ropes and cordage

The hemp is tarred and laid up in three strands on the same machine that has been standing in the back room since 1971, because the alternative is a three strand rope that goes soft at the splices after a wet season. Waxed cotton cord is for the small jobs and for decorative lacing where the wax will not rub off.

Bring a length you have already used and we will tell you honestly whether it is done. Hemp tells you by feel: if the strands move independently under your thumb, the lay has gone and the rope is finished, whatever the diameter says.

Aisle 04

Footwear

Boots made on a last that has not been reshaped since the nineteen twenties, which is either a virtue or an irritation depending on your foot. The hobnails are forge welded rather than glued, so a sole can be resoled twice before the nails are worn through.

Fitting is free and takes ten minutes with a proper last and a board, in a back room with a door. Bring the socks you intend to walk in, because a boot fitted over cotton and then worn over wool is a boot you will blister in by noon.

Aisle 05

Care and repair

Re-waxing is done in a shallow tray at forty degrees and it costs nine pounds a square metre, which is less than a new cover and considerably better for the memory of a tent you have already slept in. We do it on the old wax, not a modern one, because the modern wax does not sink into cloth that has already been dressed.

There is a list of things we will not do, printed at the bench, and it includes machine stitching across a webbing load and any claim of fire resistance for proofed cotton. Both are easy to sell and both end badly.

From the workshop

Waxing at home, in six steps

Brush the old wax off with a stiff brush on a table outdoors, lay the cloth flat, warm the wax in a shallow tin in a bain marie of water, and apply it in one direction with a clean brush in a thin coat. Two thin coats beat one thick one, and the second goes on a week later rather than the same afternoon.

Do not do it in a domestic oven, and do not iron it onto cloth that has a printed finish on the reverse. A hot iron flattens the nap and there is no way back from that.
