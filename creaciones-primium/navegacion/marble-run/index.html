Setting the run up

The marble at the head of the groove is the chapter you are in. It is lifted, lit and set in the catch cup, and it is the only marble on the run that is not waiting its turn.

Marbles behind it keep their order. The groove is always full, so when a marble leaves the line the ones behind close the gap by exactly one place.

The bead on the top rail is a demonstration piece. It never stops and it never arrives, and the run would still be in balance if it fell off.

Station I · beech, birch, and a groove two marbles wide

The Wooden Run

A marble run is a piece of playground engineering disguised as a toy, and almost all of them are built from the same three ingredients: a hard wood for the track, a soft wood for the catch pieces, and a joint that is meant to be knocked apart by a child rather than glued. The track is usually beech or birch, quarter sawn, because a straight-grained run lets a marble run fast and a knot stops it dead.

The channel is cut as a groove with a shallow roof over it, and the roof is the whole design. A marble in an open groove is a marble that jumps; a marble under a roof is a marble that is guided, and the two kinds of run you can buy differ almost entirely in how generous the roof is. A run with a wide roof will hold a marble that has slowed to almost nothing. A run with a narrow roof will throw it off the side.

Station II · why the heavy ones never get stuck

Glass and Its Weight

The standard marble is sixteen millimetres across and made of soda lime glass, and the number is not a preference. Sixteen millimetres clears a track cut for sixteen millimetres, and a run sold with fifteen millimetre marbles jams within a metre because the difference, less than a millimetre, is enough to make the marble ride up on the edge of the groove instead of sitting in it.

Glass is heavy for its size, which is the useful property. A wooden ball of the same diameter would weigh about a fifth as much and would need a steeper run, because a light marble does not press down into a dip; it skates across it. A glass marble sinks into a defect in the track and gets pushed out the other side, which is why a bad run is slow and a good run is fast and the difference between them is not the slope.

Station III · felt, baize, and a surface for looking at

The Green Bed

Almost every wooden run has a strip of green felt or baize glued into the bottom of the catch basin, and it is not decoration. Glass on glass produces a glaze of tiny scratches and eventually a marble that catches and rolls unevenly; felt gives the marble something to bite on, and a run lined with felt will outlast a run lined with bare wood by years.

The bed is also where the light comes from. A run photographed or watched in a bright room shows the marble as a dark shape with one hard highlight, and the green underneath is what makes that highlight readable. Without a surface under it the marble disappears against the shadow of the channel, and this is true of a real run on a real table as well as of the one on this page.

Station IV · a run is tilted, never pushed

Roll, Tilt, Return

You do not push a marble along a run. You lift the whole run until the marble is at the top and then you let it go, and the run holds that angle until the marble arrives or until the child loses interest and puts it down. The lift is a ply of birch at the high end, shimmed, and it is the part of the toy most often repaired with a folded piece of card.

What the tilt teaches is the relationship between slope and speed, which is the first piece of physics a child meets in a form they cannot ignore. Steepen it and the marble arrives before the eyes can follow. Flatten it and the marble stops halfway up a bump and sits there, which is the moment every child decides the toy is broken and every adult knows it is working.

Station V · the line, and the one that goes first

The Head of the Queue

A run is a queue. Six marbles wait in the groove at the bottom of the board and they are released one at a time from the front, and the order is not negotiable, and the only interesting decision in the whole toy is which marble goes first. The child who has watched five slow marbles go before the fast one learns something about order that no explanation improves on.

That is the piece of machinery on this page. The chapter you are reading is the marble at the head, lifted out of the line, lit and waiting in the catch cup, and everything behind it slides forward by exactly one place so the groove never has a hole in it. The count on the rail is the number of the marble at the head, and the name beside it is the chapter.

Station VI · where the marble is supposed to stop

The Catch Cup

The last piece of a run is the cup, and the cup is usually the most elaborate object on the board, which is a mistake that every manufacturer has made since 1920. A cup that is too deep traps the marble out of sight. A cup that is too shallow lets it run out and hit the floor, which on a hard board is a sound that ends the game immediately and permanently.

The right cup is about a centimetre deeper than the marble is wide and has a felted bottom, so the marble arrives, slows, and stops where it can be picked up with a thumb and a forefinger. Everything on this page exists to put one marble in that cup, and the marble in the cup is the chapter you are in.
