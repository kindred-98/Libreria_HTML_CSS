01 / Signal Overview

Photons counted, never averaged

At 21:40 UTC the array leaves standby and begins a slow sweep of the northern meridian. The intake is deliberately unhurried: individual photons are counted rather than averaged, so a faint transient needs four whole seconds of agreement before it earns a line on this console.

The overview reports the state of the whole instrument at a glance — feed health across every pod, how much of the night the shutters stayed open, and which observation windows are still unclaimed.

02 / Aperture Intake

Shutters that breathe with the weather

Each dome carries a pair of leaf shutters driven by hydraulic arms that open in eleven seconds and close in three. Humidity above seventy percent, gravel in the wind or a single degree of mirror drift is enough to retire an aperture for the rest of the night.

Intake runs through a vacuum bench that strips thermal glow from the path before the beam reaches the counters. What survives is a stream of time-stamped arrivals, each one carrying the pod id, the exposure it belonged to and a checksum written on the spot.

03 / Compute Array

Five hundred and twelve cores of patience

Raw arrivals are useless until they are calibrated, and calibration is where the array spends its money. Four racks of nodes fold dark frames, flat fields and bias maps into every exposure, discarding cosmic-ray strikes by comparing three neighbouring counters against each other.

A pipeline pass over one night takes nineteen minutes on paper and usually takes twenty-six in practice. The console shows the honest number, because an operator who trusts a rounded figure will eventually trust a broken one.

04 / Cold Archive

Eleven petabytes kept at liquid nitrogen temperature

Nothing observed at Meridian is ever discarded. Exposures move to the cold vault within an hour, held at minus one hundred and ninety-six degrees on shelves that are written twice and read once a year to prove the bits still sit where they were put.

The archive is indexed by sky coordinate rather than by date, which means a researcher asking for every observation of a two-degree patch can be answered without knowing when anyone looked at it. Retrieval from cold storage takes under four minutes.

05 / Uplink Window

Two passes, six partners, no retries

Results leave the mountain on two scheduled satellite passes, each fourteen minutes long. Six partner observatories listen on the same channel, so the frame is compressed, signed and sent exactly once; a missed pass is a missed night, not a delayed one.

The bar reports the countdown to the next window in tabular figures so it never shifts under your eye. When the lamp turns amber the link is already open, and everything still on the queue has less than nine minutes to clear.
