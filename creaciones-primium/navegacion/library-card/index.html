Rules of the branch

One card per reader. Six loans on a card. The card is punched on issue and the hole is the only record of the date the library will argue from.

Return is stamped, never written. The date stamp at the right of each line is inked with the office pad and pressed once, so every stamp on this card is slightly crooked.

The rod through the holes is copper because the holes are made with a bradawl and the rod keeps the card from tearing along the perforations. This is not recorded anywhere and was done anyway.

Loan one · due 14 March

A History of Ordinary Bridges

A book about stone bridges that nobody has ever lost a copy of, because it is a reference and everybody who opens it is looking for one specific span. The chapter on packhorse bridges is the one people read, on the grounds that a bridge built for a horse that can carry sixty pounds has a great deal to say about what the horse was expected to be carrying.

It went out on a Saturday and came back eleven days late. It went out again in November and came back four days early, which the clerk noted as a matter of public record because the system had no other way of saying that a reader had changed.

Loan two · due 2 April

Tide Tables, 1911 To 1938

Twenty-eight years of predicted tide for every port on the coast, printed in columns small enough that a person has to mean it. It is kept on the open shelf, chained to a lectern, and it is the most consulted object in the building by a factor of about nine. Ferry men, fishermen, two police, an insurance man and a man who comes every Tuesday and does not work.

The chain is longer than it needs to be so that the book can be carried to the window. Somebody lengthened it in 1954 with no note, and the length is exactly right, which suggests somebody thought about it longer than they let on.

Loan three · due 19 April

The Making Of A Harbour

Twenty-eight pages about dredging, published by the harbour board at its own expense to explain to ratepayers why their money had gone into a channel. Reprinted three times. Out of print all three times. The third printing was done on the back of the stock left over from the first, so the third edition is the first edition with a different cover.

It contains the only honest sentence ever written about the project, on page nineteen: the channel will fill again, and the ratepayers are buying ten years. This sentence was quoted at every subsequent meeting of the board for thirty years, always by somebody who wanted the money stopped, and always in isolation from the sentence before it, which was about the port.

Loan four · due 30 April

Night Work On The Northern Line

Written by a permanent-way man who worked from 10 at night to 6 in the morning for thirty-one years, and who sent the manuscript to two publishers who both refused it because there was no story in it. It was printed in 1957 by a firm of four people in a town that has since lost the firm.

The chapter called Nights Off describes, in eleven pages, what a man with a fixed roster does with the one evening in five that is his own. There is no event in it. It is the chapter the librarian in this branch recommends to anybody who says they cannot get into a book, and it is the reason the book has been out ninety-one times.

Loan five · due 8 May

A Grammar Of The Cornish Dialect

A grammar of a language with no speakers, compiled from written sources and from four people over the age of seventy, all of whom were recorded without being told that they were the last. It cost more to print than the branch's annual budget for children's books and it has never been out of the building.

Eleven people have read it. All eleven were asked to write in the back, which the library encourages and nobody else does. There are eleven comments in the back and ten of them are about the weather.

Loan six · due 21 May

Almanac Of The Coast Guard

Every station on the coast, its keeper, its boat, and the number of nights in the year that a boat was launched. It reads like a list and is not one. Launch counts rise and fall with nothing visible: a stretch of coast where the fishing is poor launches almost nothing, and a stretch where the cliffs are unstable launches constantly, because the cliffs are what the boats are launched from.

The last entry in the almanac is for a station abolished in 1958, kept in with a note that reads, kept for completeness, which is the phrase this whole card is built on.
