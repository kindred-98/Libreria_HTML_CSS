Bearing 000

Runway 27L

The westerly runway and the one the approach plate is built around. Localiser from 20 miles, glideslope from 10, and a decision height of 200 feet above the runway on the standard approach, which is low enough to be uncomfortable in a westerly crosswind and high enough to be flown by everyone.

With a surface wind at 240 and gusts to 14, the crosswind component on 27L is 12 knots, just inside the limit for the type. Traffic is separated by a half minute and a mile, which is why the board below the scope is the busier of the two displays.

Bearing 052

Taxiway Alpha

Alpha is the only taxiway that connects the northern apron to the runway 27 thresholds, and it is a single lane with passing bays every 400 metres. Aircraft larger than a regional jet hold on the bay markers and are passed by everything else.

The surface is wet this morning, so the taxi speed limit drops to 15 knots along the full length, and the runway entry lights at hold short C are flashing amber. A controller who forgets the wet figure is the reason a morning takes an extra hour.

Bearing 118

Runway 14R

The southeasterly runway is used when the wind swings, and it is the busy one for departures in the morning because it points away from the arrival stream. It has no instrument approach, so an arrival on it needs a visual circuit or a one in a thousand wind.

Its crosswind limit is the same as the opposite direction, which sounds obvious until you remember that a runway is not a single heading. The operational number is always the component along the runway, whatever the tower is calling.

Bearing 186

Apron East

Four stands, two of which take wide bodies, and a pushback lane that is only wide enough for one aircraft at a time. Stand 3 is on power and stand 4 is on ground power only, which is a sentence that matters to a crew in January and to nobody else in July.

Apron East fills first in the morning because it is on the terminal side of the field. Everything that has to be back in the air within the hour is parked here, and everything that can wait is pushed out to the western stands.

Bearing 246

Deicing pad

Two bays, a holding point that is comfortably clear of the taxiway, and a holdover time board that is the most consulted document in the building between November and March. Type one fluid for an all clear, type three when there is going to be a delay.

The bay is a shared resource and the sequencing is nothing like fair: departures leaving with a frozen wing are separated from arrivals that have already taken a slot, because a departure that is delayed is still leaving the building a gate earlier.

Bearing 312

Radar feed north

The northern feed is what gives this console its 24 mile range, and it is a single site on a ridge with a view that stops abruptly about 18 miles out where the ground rises. Beyond that line the picture is whatever the neighbouring sector is willing to pass on.

Two aircraft are usually visible on it in marginal conditions, both of them private, both of them asking for a field that is closed. Coverage is not a circle in the way a training slide suggests; it is whatever the terrain and the antenna allow.
