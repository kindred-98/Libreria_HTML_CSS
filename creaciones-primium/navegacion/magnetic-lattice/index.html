Node 01

Flux Field

The field of a bar magnet is the first thing every physics lesson draws and the last thing it explains. You trace a handful of loops from pole to pole, sprinkle iron filings, and the filings stand up along the curves as though they had found wires. What you have actually found is the direction in which a test magnet would want to point, sampled at a few hundred places and joined for the eye's convenience.

That convenience matters. The field has no substance: you cannot weigh it, and a compass needle inside a vacuum reads exactly the same as one in a crowded room. Close to the surface the lines crowd into something that looks like a solid, and it is worth remembering that the crowding is a picture of strength, not a pile of material you could reach in and take hold of.

Node 02

Coercivity

Coercivity is the field you have to apply backwards before a magnet gives up its magnetisation. A fridge magnet clings to the door because its coercivity is far above anything the door can produce; a piece of soft iron has almost none, and lift it with a magnet and it drops straight back. Between those two extremes sits a design decision, and the whole family of alnico grades is basically an argument about where to put the hinge.

Soft iron wins where you want the magnet to work and then get out of the way, which is why transformer cores and relay armatures are laminated rather than solid. Hard materials win where the answer must survive, in meter movements, in magnetised steel for cranes, in the encoder disc that tells a servo where it is. Neither is the better magnet. They are two different answers to the question of how long the field should stay after you stop paying for it.

Node 03

Domain Wall

A single crystal of iron is not magnetised in one direction across its whole body. It breaks into domains, each a few micrometres across, each pointing its own way, and in an unmagnetised sample the arrows cancel almost perfectly. Magnetising the material means persuading all of them to agree, and the argument happens along the surfaces between them, which are called walls.

Walls cost energy to move, and that cost is the shape of the magnetisation curve. A soft material has cheap walls that slide easily, so the curve rises steeply and gives back almost all of it when the field is removed. A hard material has walls that snag on defects in the crystal, and those snags are why the curve has a square shoulder and why the answer persists long after the coil has gone quiet.

Node 04

Hysteresis

Plot the applied field against the magnetisation and the line does not retrace itself. It comes back along a different road, opening a loop with two useful corners: the width of the loop on the horizontal axis is the coercivity, and the height of the loop where it crosses zero is the remanence, the field a material keeps with nothing at all holding it there.

The enclosed area is work that went in as heat, which is why transformer laminations are thin, why soft alloys are chosen, and why a magnet that is demagnetised carelessly stays weak for ever. The loop is also the reason this page is worth reading twice: the second time past, the reading is different from the first, and the difference is the whole subject.

Node 05

Eddy Current

Move a conductor through a field and you get a current, and in a solid block that current has nowhere particular to go. It circulates in wide closed loops that have no useful job, and all it does is heat the block and oppose the motion that made it. This is the eddy current, and it is strong enough to push a train to a halt without touching it.

Every practical answer is about cutting the loop. Laminations slice the block into insulated sheets so the current has to jump a gap. Copper slots in a rotor do the same job. A solid aluminium plate dragged between two magnets feels like pulling through syrup, and the syrup is the loop you refused to cut.

Node 06

Demagnetise

To kill the field in a magnet you do not simply remove it. You reverse it, and you reverse it with a magnitude that starts large and shrinks steadily to nothing, so the hysteresis loop is walked backwards in shrinking steps until the point falls in the centre where both answers are almost the same. Anything slower than that and the magnet remembers where it was.

A coil on a slowly shrinking amplitude does it neatly. So does heat, which lets the walls jump over their defects on their own, and so does a steel shipping case, which works by letting the field leak out into a lot of soft iron instead of fighting it. The craft is entirely in the patience, and the reward is a magnet that will accept a new story.
